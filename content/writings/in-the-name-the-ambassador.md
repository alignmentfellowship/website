## I. In the Name of the Law

Start with a sentence nobody has ever misunderstood.

*Stop — in the name of the law.*

You know instantly what that means. More to the point, you know instantly what it does not mean.
It does not mean the officer is obliged to say the word *law* out loud, or that saying it is what
gives the order its force. An officer who simply shouted the word would not have arrested anybody.
What the phrase claims is that the person speaking is not speaking for himself: there is an
authority behind him, he is carrying its intention, and you are dealing with **it** and not with
him.

That is what *in the name of* means. It means **in the will of**. A herald in the name of a
king,[^agency] an ambassador in the name of a country, a lawyer signing in the name of a client who
cannot hold the pen — in every case the name stands for a will that is not the speaker's own, and in
every case the test of whether the claim is genuine is not what the person said. It is whether what
they are doing is what the one named actually wants.

Take the ambassador, because the New Testament takes him too.[^ambassador] An ambassador is not a
man with views about a country. He is a country's will, arriving somewhere else in a body, and
everything he says stands under that constraint. The moment he begins delivering his own opinions he
has not done the job badly. **He has stopped being an ambassador while keeping the title.**

You have never once been confused by this. Nobody has.

Except in a single place. When the name is God's, the phrase stops meaning that for us and starts
meaning something closer to a password — a word that works when said, and fails when omitted. And
the moment it does, two sentences that belong to the same collection begin to contradict each
other.

The first is at the end of the Sermon on the Mount. A crowd comes forward with a résumé, and the
résumé is not padded — these are people who did real things, and did them under the name:

> *Many will say to Me in that day, LORD, LORD, have we not prophesied in Thy name? and in Thy name
> have cast out devils? and in Thy name done many wonderful works?*[^kjv]

Three times. Correct every time. And the answer is not partial credit:

> *And then will I profess unto them, I never knew you.*

The second is in John, and it cuts the other way with equal force:

> *He that believeth not is condemned already, because he hath not believed in the name of the only
> begotten Son of God.*[^318]

That is not a parable or a footnote to one. It is a verdict, and the name is inside it.

On the password reading these cannot both stand. Say the name — perfectly, repeatedly, while
casting out devils — and it does not work. Fail to believe in the name, and you are condemned
already. And yet they have sat in the same collection, read by the same church, for the whole of
its history,[^attrib] which suggests the problem is not in the sentences.

I do not think anyone missed it. I think we have misread something, and it is not *believe* — that
word has been recovered elsewhere and I will lean on the recovery rather than repeat it.[^believe]
It is not quite *name*, either. **It is the phrase.**

So this is one question, and it is a small one. What happens if *in the name*, when the name
is God's, means exactly what it means every other time you have ever used it?

## II. The Name Was Never the Word

**The tradition never thought a name was a word**, and it says so in places we read past because we
assume we already know what the sentences are about.

The Hebrew *shem* — the word we translate *name* — is not a label. It is the person made public:
character, authority, reputation, the self as it can be met and dealt with.[^shem] Which is why
sentences that read like superstition turn plain the moment you stop hearing *label*: *the name of
the LORD is a strong tower: the righteous runneth into it, and is safe.*[^tower] Nobody has ever run
into a syllable. They run toward the One they trust — and the name, in that sentence, is the whole
of what there is to trust: who They have shown Themselves to be, and what They have been known to
do.

And now the line most of the English-speaking world has said out loud more often than any other:

> *Hallowed be Thy name.*[^hallowed]

Not a petition about pronunciation. It asks that who God is be held holy — and you already know
exactly how that request gets broken, because you have watched it broken. It is broken by somebody
doing something cruel and saying that God wanted it. **The name is not damaged when it is
mispronounced. It is damaged when it is worn by something God did not will.**

And there is a reading of the third commandment that says exactly this — not the only reading of it,
and not the oldest one, but a well-supported one.

> *Thou shalt not take the name of the LORD thy God in vain.*[^vain]

The verb there is not a word about speech. It is *nasa'* — to lift, to carry, to **bear**. Read that
way, what is prohibited is carrying the name *emptily*: bearing it, claiming it, acting under it,
while doing something that is not His. Not first of all a rule about swearing, then, but **a rule
about false agency** — the officer who was never sent, the herald with nobody behind him, the
ambassador with nothing to deliver but himself.

Which is the charge against the crowd we started with. Those men prophesied under the name, cast
out devils under the name, did many wonderful works under the name, and had nothing of His will
behind any of it. They were **bearing the name emptily** — and the commandment against doing
exactly that is three thousand years older than the sermon they are standing in.

## III. He Says It About Himself

The reading is not an importation, and here is why I think so: **He applies the phrase to Himself,
and then tells us what it consisted of.**

He is answering men who have already decided against Him:

> *I receive not honour from men. But I know you, that ye have not the love of God in you. I am
> come in My Father's name, and ye receive Me not: if another shall come in his own name, him ye
> will receive. How can ye believe, which receive honour one of another, and seek not the honour
> that cometh from God only?*[^5414]

Four sentences, and they are one argument. Take them in order.

**One.** He opens by saying what He is **not** doing: *I receive not honour from men.*

That is neither modesty nor a throat-clear. It is the condition that makes everything after it
possible. Take your standing from the people in front of you and you are, whatever else you think you
are doing, carrying **their** will: you will say the thing that earns the room and stop saying the
thing that costs you, and you will experience that as sincerity, because the pressure is invisible
from the inside. **You cannot come in someone's name while drawing your pay from the audience.** So
before He claims to have come in His Father's name, He states the thing He had to decline in order
to do it.

**Two.** Then the diagnosis: *But I know you, that ye have not the love of God in you.*

Notice what He does not say is missing. Not information. Not the right words about Him — these are
men who have the vocabulary and the training and the texts. What is missing is **the love of God**,
which is the one thing that would have turned a will toward Him in the first place. The deficiency
He names is at the level of what they want.[^5414b]

**Three.** Then the claim, and its shadow: *I am come in My Father's name, and ye receive Me not: if
another shall come in his own name, him ye will receive.*

To come in your own name is to be **self-sent**. Carrying nobody's intention but your own,
authorized by nobody, answerable to nobody — the exact opposite of what He has just claimed about
Himself. And the sting is the indictment inside it: *that* man you will welcome. The self-appointed
one is legible to you. The sent one was not.

**Four.** And the last sentence says why: *How can ye believe, which receive honour one of another,
and seek not the honour that cometh from God only?*

People trading approval back and forth among themselves can recognize a fellow player, because
the wanting is the same. Someone carrying another's will is not playing, and so does not register
as anything they know how to receive.

Which is why the first sentence had to come first. The whole passage turns on a single verb, used
four times in four verses: He does not **receive** honour from men; they do not **receive** Him;
they will **receive** the man who comes in his own name; they **receive** honour from one
another.[^lambano] What a person is willing to receive decides who they are able to receive. **That
is the whole passage: the one who came for no honour, unrecognized by people who deal in nothing
else.**

And notice what the password reading does to the middle of it. *He comes in his own name* would have
to mean *he says his own name out loud* — which is not a thing anyone does, and certainly not a
thing anyone could be indicted for welcoming. The sentence means nothing at all unless *in the name
of* is a statement about **whose will is being carried**.

The passage leaves one question standing, and He answers it elsewhere, without being asked: **what did coming in the Father's name consist of?**

> *I seek not Mine own will, but the will of the Father which hath sent Me.*[^530]

> *I came down from heaven, not to do Mine own will, but the will of [Them] that sent Me.*[^638]

And when it costs everything, in a garden, at night, the whole of it compresses into one sentence
with nothing left over:

> *Not My will, but Thine, be done.*[^geth]

Which means the incarnation is not merely the occasion of the question. **It is the
demonstration of the answer.** If you want to know what it looks like to be in the Father's name,
the entire public life of Jesus of Nazareth is the exhibit — and at no point does the exhibit
consist of Him saying a word.

The Greek corroborates without establishing, and it is kept at that weight. The construction
under *believe in the name* is *pisteuō eis to onoma* — believe **into** the name, which is odd in a
specific direction, since one does not believe *into* a proposition. You can believe *that* a thing
is so; you cannot believe *into* it. And in the ordinary paperwork of the period, *eis to onoma* is
how money moves — a sum paid **into an account**, into someone's name.[^eis] A transfer; a record
of whose books a thing is carried on. The first Christians reached for that same preposition and
that same noun for what happens to a person at the beginning: baptism *into the name*. Not baptism
*about* Him. Not *in agreement with* Him. Into.

**So: to believe into the name is to come under it, and to come under it is to be doing what the
One named wants.**

## IV. In Jesus' Name, Amen

Now the practice, because nearly everyone reading this has performed it, most of us before we could
have said why.

We put the name at the end of prayers. *In Jesus' name, amen.* It sits after the asking, and if you
listen to how it actually gets said, it does not behave like the rest of the prayer. The rest is
addressed to Someone. That last clause is doing something else: it comes faster than what came
before it, in the same words every time, and it lands like a seal pressed into wax — the part that
makes the document count.

Nobody arrived at that habit out of nowhere. It is obedience to a specific instruction:

> *And whatsoever ye shall ask in My name, that will I do… If ye shall ask any thing in My name, I
> will do it.*[^ask]

That is an enormous thing to hand a frightened person, the church has handed it out constantly, and
I do not think we should be surprised at what people made of it. If you tell someone that asking in
the name gets results and never tell them what asking in the name *is*, they will conclude the
obvious thing: that the name is the part that works. Say it and the request goes through. Leave it
off and you have posted a letter without a stamp.

So it becomes a formula — and not because anyone is credulous. The instruction is real and the
people obeying it are doing as they were told. **What went missing is the explanation.**

Except that it did not go missing. It is sitting in the same body of writing, and it is not subtle.
When the Johannine letters restate that promise, they restate it with the operative phrase **swapped
out for its meaning**:

> *If we ask any thing according to [Their] will, [They hear] us.*[^will]

*In My name* in the gospel. *According to His will* in the letter. Same promise, same community, one
phrase traded for the other in the operative slot, with nobody appearing to notice a change of
subject — because, if the reading here is right, there was no change of subject. **Scripture treats the
two as interchangeable, which is precisely what is being claimed here.**

And once you can read the promise that way, the sentence He says two chapters after making it stops
being strange. Still at the table, still in the same long goodbye, to men who had walked with Him
for years:

> *Hitherto have ye asked nothing in My name.*[^hitherto]

Nothing. Not *not enough*, not *not properly* — **nothing**. Said to twelve people who had been
saying His name out loud, daily, for the length of a public ministry. On the password reading that
sentence is close to unintelligible. On this one it is not even surprising: they had asked for
plenty of things, and had not yet once asked for what He wanted.

Which puts the sign-off back together, at a price. To ask *in His name* is to ask **as one under
it** — for what someone on His business would ask for, the way an agent asks on the authority of
the one who sent him. That is not a smaller promise than the one we thought we had. It is a much
larger one, and it has a condition inside it that a formula does not have.

And it lets me say the thing this misunderstanding has cost people, because it has cost them a
great deal. If you once asked for something that mattered, and said the words correctly, and it did
not come — and you have spent time since wondering whether you fumbled the formula or whether
nobody was there — those were never the only two options. **You did not say it wrong.** Saying was
never the mechanism. There is no incantation you got wrong, because there was no incantation.

## V. Matthew 7:21-22

Now back to the crowd we started with, because the problem we opened with does not need solving. It needs noticing that it was never there.

Here is the sentence immediately before the one we began on. Same sermon, same breath, one verse
earlier:

> *Not every one that saith unto Me, LORD, LORD, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven; but he
> that doeth the will of My Father which is in heaven.*[^721]

And then, with no paragraph between them:

> *Many will say to Me in that day, LORD, LORD, have we not prophesied in Thy name?*

Read the second with *in Thy name* meaning *doing Thy will*, and these are not a criterion followed
by an illustration. **They are the same sentence said twice.** Verse twenty-one states it
positively: the one who *does the will* enters. Verse twenty-two states it as the failure case —
here are people claiming to have acted in the name, and the claim is false, and it is false in
exactly the way verse twenty-one predicted. They said the name over their works. They did not do
the will.

There is no tension in that passage. There is one claim, stated forward and then backward, and we
have spent a long time reading the backward statement as though it were about vocabulary.

And it puts the last judgment in Matthew where it belongs — not as an exception to the rule but as
its plainest case. The sheep who fed someone and could not say why, who ask *LORD, when saw we
Thee?*,[^2537] were doing the will. They were, in the only sense the phrase has ever had, **acting
in His name.** They simply had no idea whose name they were acting in.

## VI. The Voice

Which leaves exactly one question, and it is the last hard one: how does anybody do the will of
someone they cannot identify?

The answer is in the shepherd chapter, in a detail I had read many times without seeing. **We
assumed the name in question was His. In the text, it is ours.**

> *He calleth His own sheep by name, and leadeth them out.*[^103]

He has their names. The reverse is never said. And what the sheep have of Him is stated carefully,
twice, and it is not His name:

> *The sheep follow Him: for they know His voice.*[^104]

His **voice**. That is the whole of what they are credited with. They cannot spell Him, could not
define Him, would fail any examination you set them. What they have is that they know the sound of
the One who has been calling them, and can tell it from a stranger's. And He says it once more,
from His own side: *I am the good shepherd, and know My sheep, and am known of Mine.*[^1014]
Knowing, there, runs both ways — He is not describing strangers. But notice what each side has
**of** the other. He has their names. They have His voice.

Now set three scenes together. The men of Matthew 7 **have the name**, have used it to do things
that worked, and hear *I never knew you* — not *you never knew Me*. **He** is the one declining to
acknowledge them.[^knew] The welcomed of Matthew 25 **do not know Him**, and are welcomed anyway.
And the sheep of John 10 do not have His name; they have His voice. He has *their* names.

**In none of the three does holding His name count for anything, and in all three His knowing them
does.** So the question the texts keep answering is not whether you can say His name. It is whether
He can say yours — and what you do when the voice comes.

> *And thine ears shall hear a word behind thee, saying, This is the way, walk ye in it, when ye
> turn to the right hand, and when ye turn to the left.*[^isa]

Behind you. At the turn. Not an itinerary and not an introduction — a correction, arriving from a
direction you cannot see, from Someone who evidently knows where you were meant to be. That is a
will, delivered the only way a walker can take it: one step at a time, with no view of the country
and no card presented.

**You do not need the speaker's name to do what the voice says.**

And this answers the worry that has been building since the claim was first made. If being in the
name is doing the will, have I exchanged a word to say for a life to produce — a harder gospel, in
which nobody can ever be sure they have produced enough of it? No, and the voice is the reason.
**You are not handed a life to produce. You are handed the next step, and usually only once you
have started to go wrong.** A will you could audit yourself against would be a standard, and a
standard is a thing you can fail by inches. This is not a standard. It is a word at a turn, and the
only question it ever puts to you is whether you will do the next thing it says.

## VII. Then What Is Unbelief?

Now the hard verse, with everything above on the table.

*He hath not believed in the name of the only begotten Son of God.*

If the name is a will, and believing into it is coming under it, then that sentence is not
describing someone who never heard the syllables. **It is describing a refusal.** Not a failure to
produce — a refusal to turn. Being spoken to, at the turn, by Someone who knows your name, and
going on walking.

Then, one verse further on, John explains the condemnation himself, in his own voice, with the
words *this is the condemnation* — and the criterion he names is not a vocabulary test: *men loved
darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil.*[^319] **Deeds.** I do not want to lean
harder on that than it will hold; a determined reader can construe it as describing the *evidence*
of unbelief rather than its substance. It does not have to carry the argument. It only has to stop
the verse before it from being read as a sentence about words, and it does that in John's own hand.

Which closes the case the essay opened with. Two sentences that could not both be about the word,
and neither of them was. One condemns a man for refusing the will he was called into; the other
turns away men who did great works with the word in their mouths and the will nowhere in them. They
were never in tension. They were saying the same thing from opposite ends.

## VIII. The Objection, and It Is a Good One

Now the strongest thing that can be said against all of this, and I want it in its best form rather
than one I can beat.

**You have quietly turned Christ into a set of qualities.** If being in His name is doing His will,
and His will is describable — mercy, self-giving, care for whoever is in front of you — then the Son
of God has become a description, and anyone matching the description is following Him by
definition. That is a very old move, made by people more careful than me, and it always ends the
same way: with a Christ who is a summary of the virtues we already approved of, and a gospel that
turns out to be congratulating the reader.

The objection is right about the danger. It is wrong that I have made the move, and the difference
is one distinction, which belongs in the body of the essay rather than hidden where nobody reads.

**Anonymity is not abstraction.**

The sheep follow a *Someone* they cannot name. They are not following a *principle* they can. Those
are not neighboring positions; they are opposite errors, and the second is the one this argument
dies of if it gets lazy. A person who has never heard the name and is walking behind a man he
cannot identify has a Person ahead of him — a particular One, at a particular address, who was born
and hungry and killed. A person who has identified the principle of self-giving love and committed
to it has an idea, and an idea does not walk anywhere. **You can follow someone whose name you do
not know. You cannot follow a virtue, because there is nobody in front of you.**

And a will is not a description, which is the same distinction from the other side. A description
you can satisfy on your own initiative, by matching it. A will belongs to somebody, arrives when
they issue it, and is frequently not what you would have chosen — which is why *not My will, but
Thine* is a sentence someone had to sweat to say, and why no summary of the virtues has ever
required a garden at night.

The second objection I raise because leaving it out would be a dishonesty this publication does not
get to commit. **John's own situation cuts against me.** The community that gospel was written in
and for was, on the ordinary reconstruction, under pressure for confessing Jesus publicly; the
confession cost something, and the text is shaped by that cost. In that setting, *believe in the
name* very plausibly carried a sense close to *say it out loud where it will hurt you* — not
anonymity at all, but its opposite. Somebody who knows the historical situation will raise this,
and they will be raising something real.

So, exactly what is being done with it, and no more. I think John's *situation* was public
confession, and that the *criterion* his language encodes is coming under the will, and that the two
are compatible — because for a community in which coming under the will meant saying so publicly at
cost, the phrase would naturally do both jobs at once. **But that is a claim I am making, not a
result I am reading off the page.** The text does not hand it over. I think it is the better account
of why one collection holds Matthew 7 and John 3 without embarrassment. I do not think it is beyond
argument.

And the third, which usually arrives first and which I have left for last because it is the
easiest: *neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given
among men, whereby we must be saved*;[^acts] *God also hath highly exalted Him, and given Him a name
which is above every name.* Both hold, unbent. Neither is a fee schedule. *There is no other
name* is a claim about the source — that there is One, that it is Him, that nothing else does what
He does. It is not a claim that the water is unavailable to anyone who cannot name the river.

And look at what joins the halves of the second one. *He humbled Himself, and became obedient unto
death, even the death of the cross.* **Wherefore** — on account of that — *God also hath highly
exalted Him, and given Him a name which is above every name.*[^phil] The hymn puts the will first
and the name second, and makes the second the consequence of the first. **The objection's own proof
text is built in that same order.**

Two limits, so this stays the size it actually is. **This is not universalism** — nothing here says
everyone arrives, and the scene the whole argument runs through has two groups in it, not one. And
it is not the flattening that says every road is the same road. There is one road, one will, and a
Person on it who knows your name.

## IX. He Calleth His Own Sheep by Name

What frightens people about the hard verse — and it does frighten them, I think more of them than
ever say so — is a question they ask in the second person, about themselves: *do I have the name
right?*

It is the wrong question, and I mean that in the most literal available sense. It is not too
anxious, or too small, or insufficiently generous. It is a question about saying, and the phrase
was never about saying. It is asking about the wrong name.

The question underneath it, the one the texts keep answering when we thought they were answering
the other one, is whether there is a voice behind you that knows yours — and what you do when it
speaks. It arrives at the turn, which is the only moment you are in a position to notice it, and it
does not introduce itself, because it does not have to. You were never asked to identify the
speaker. You were asked to turn.

And if the shepherd chapter is telling the truth, *your* name is already in His mouth, and has been
there the whole time you were worrying about *His*.

[^agency]: The maxim is *shelucho shel adam kemoto* — "a person's agent is as himself." It appears

41b. Both are **later than the New Testament** — the Mishnah is redacted around 200 CE — so the
formula appears here as evidence of a widely shared social convention, not as a source anyone in
these texts is quoting. The convention itself is not in doubt; only the date of its codification.

analogy when it wants to say what standing in for someone consists of.

[^shem]: Hebrew *shem*, whose range covers name, reputation, renown, and character — the person as
publicly known and dealt with; the range carries into the Greek *onoma*. The point the argument
needs is only that the word is not a bare label, which is uncontested. I have kept the body from
resting on **character** as the gloss, because that word slides — a character is a set of qualities,
and a set of qualities is precisely what §VIII refuses to let this argument turn God into. *The
person as they can be dealt with* is closer to the Hebrew anyway, and it is the sense the rest of
the essay runs on.

[^tower]: Proverbs 18:10 (KJV).

[^hallowed]: Matthew 6:9 (KJV).

[^vain]: Exodus 20:7 (KJV); the parallel is Deuteronomy 5:11. The verb is *nasa'* — to lift, carry,
or bear — and *shav* covers emptiness, falsehood, and worthlessness, which is what licenses the
rendering *you shall not bear the name of the LORD your God emptily*. The standing of that reading wants stating, because
nothing here needs it and overstating it would do damage. **It is neither the oldest reading nor a consensus one.** The original sense of the command is
genuinely disputed — something on the order of two dozen interpretations have been proposed over the
centuries — and the traditional lines run toward false oaths sworn in the name, or irreverent use of
it in speech. The bearing-the-name reading is a recent and vigorously argued one, most fully by
Carmen Joy Imes, *Bearing God's Name: Why Sinai Still Matters* (IVP Academic, 2019). I use it here
as corroboration for a claim §I and §III establish on other ground, never as proof. And the older
oath reading is less a rival to it than its sharpest instance: perjury in God's name is a paradigm
case of bearing the name falsely.

[^5414]: John 5:41–44 (KJV), quoted entire. Deity pronouns are capitalized here, per the house
convention disclosed in the first footnote; the *his own name* and *him* of verse 43 refer to the
other man and stay lowercase.

[^lambano]: The verb is *lambanō* at each of the four points, in four different forms: *doxan para
anthrōpōn ou **lambanō*** (41, present indicative active, 1st singular); *ou **lambanete** me* and
*ekeinon **lēmpsesthe*** (43, present indicative active and future indicative middle, 2nd plural);

[^eis]: The idiom is well attested. Moulton and Milligan, *The Vocabulary of the Greek Testament*,
note that *eis to onoma tinos* is frequent in the papyri of payments made "to the account of any
one"; Deissmann, *Bible Studies*, draws the inference the argument uses — that as buying *into the

constitutes belonging. The scholarship is early-twentieth-century and I flag its age rather than
lean on it: it is an illuminating parallel from ordinary commercial Greek, used here as
corroboration for a claim §I and §III establish on other ground, not as a lexical proof.

[^hitherto]: John 16:24 (KJV), in full: *Hitherto have ye asked nothing in My name: ask, and ye shall
