## I. The Last Meal

There is a show on the internet called *Last Meals.* It runs on a YouTube cooking channel, Mythical
Kitchen, and its conceit is exactly as morbid as it sounds: the host, Josh Scherer, cooks a guest the
meal they say they'd want as their last one on earth, sits them down at a chintzy diner set, and
interviews them about dying while they eat it. Every person, Scherer likes to open by saying, has
exactly two things in common: we all have to eat, and we are all going to die.[^1] It's a food
channel — celebrity guest, big laughs, an obscene quantity of gravy. It should not keep turning into
a confessional. It does, nearly every episode, because someone made to look at their own death over
a plate of the food they love tends to stop performing, and someone who has stopped performing will
tell you what they actually believe.

The guest is Corey Taylor. Frontman of Slipknot — nine men in masks, the loudest, most deliberately
frightening thing Middle America produced in the nineties, the band your church warned you about. He
is fifty-two. He is, by his own repeated account, a recovering alcoholic and addict who spent most of
his life without, in his words, "a foundation in believing in something," and who was "pretty
nihilistic" about the whole business of meaning. He once, drunk, tried to crowd-surf down a flight
of stairs and lost a fight with a ceiling fan. This is not a man you expect to say anything true
about God.

What he reaches for anyway, once the gravy is in front of him and the subject is the end, is not
nihilism. It arrives in him by the one road that
reliably gets a person there, which is the collapse of everything he used to think held him up.

I want to use his last meal to argue something about the first one. About a tree, and a piece of
fruit, and the particular knowledge it's said to have handed us — the knowledge, the story calls it,
of good and evil. My claim is that this knowledge is the knowledge of *two powers:* the conviction,
which nearly all of us run on without noticing we hold it, that reality is split down the middle
into a good force and an evil one — God and an adversary, us and them — locked in a war we are each
enlisted in at birth. That conviction is the engine under most of what we call religion. It is the
exact thing standing between us and the man that religion says it follows. He did not believe in two
powers. He believed in one, and He asked us to trust it.

## II. Two Powers

The man before the collapse is a clean specimen of the belief the rest of this essay is about. The belief is that there are two powers, and that your job is to fight on the
right side. It does not feel like a belief. It feels like reality.

Ask Corey where his drive came from and he doesn't reach for a gift. He reaches for a wound — being
abused as a child, he says, and ridiculed, and bullied; being someone whose worth was decided for him
by other people. The engine he built out of that was resentment, and he is precise about how it ran:
he would watch people he judged less talented get the opportunities he felt he'd earned, and it
fanned the resentment, he says, rather than shaming him out of it. Every show was, in his word, a
"dogfight." He was at war, and the war was the point.

That war requires a particular world. It requires two sides — a good one, his, and a bad one,
everyone in his way. It requires a scoreboard. It requires that the self be walled off and defended,
because on the far side of the wall are the rivals, the cheats, the ones who got what was his.
Everything was stuck in his own orbit, he says of who he was; it had to be about him or he could not
enjoy it. He got what the war promised — the arenas, the Grammy, the world — and found it empty: one
day, he says, you wake up holding the world and find you do not care. He had spent decades sorting
reality into two powers, mine and against-mine, and the sorting had walled him off so completely
that by the end, he says, he "had hurt everyone I love" and could not feel any of it.

This is not a rock star's peculiar disease. It is the water we all swim in — the good guys and the
bad guys, our side and their side, the war on this and the fight against that. And the tradition says
the assumption has a birthday.

## III. The Tree of Two Powers

Of all the trees in the garden, one is off-limits: *the tree of the knowledge of good and
evil.*[^2] Not the tree of wickedness. Not the tree of disobedience. The knowledge *of good and
evil* — the capacity to divide the world into two and take a side.

The serpent's promise is not what we were taught. It is not *you will become wicked.* It is *ye shall
be as gods, knowing good and evil.*[^3] The bait is a position — the judge's seat, the vantage from
which you sort creation into two piles and rule on which is which. And there is an old way of
reading what happens the moment they take it, kept alive more by the mystics than by the pulpit,
which I lean on while owning that it is a reading and not the consensus of the scholars. On this
reading, the fall is not first the arrival of sin. It is the arrival of *two.* The instant their eyes
are opened, the first thing they know is that they are naked, and ashamed: a self, suddenly, at war
with its own body. Then they hide: a self cut off from God. Then, confronted, the man blames the woman
and the woman blames the serpent: a self cut off from every other self.[^4] Shame, hiding, blame —
three walls, in three verses.

So what Adam acquires at the tree is not wickedness. It is a worldview. He gains the conviction that
there are two powers and that he must referee them, and every wall we have ever built runs back to
it: the wall in Corey's chest, and the wall we put up against the person we have decided is on the
other side. The tree taught us to see double. Almost everything we call human history is the record
of what we did once we could.

## IV. There Was Only Ever One

Here is the strange part, the part the tradition mostly forgot it was holding. Its own deepest layer
says the two powers were never real. There is only one.

Isaiah has it at its most uncompromising, in the mouth of God: *I form the light, and create
darkness: I make peace, and create evil: I the LORD do all these things.*[^5] That sentence refuses a
great deal. It refuses a rival. It refuses a second throne across the field where a dark god
rules the dark half of things. The verse is a hammer swung directly at the two-power picture: not
*God will defeat the darkness,* which would still grant the darkness a throne to be knocked off, but
*there is no second power to defeat.* The same note sounds through the whole tradition — *Hear, O
Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one*; *I am the LORD, and there is none else*; *Shall there be
evil in a city, and the LORD hath not done it?*[^6] One. Not one-winning-against-two. One.

Which means the thing we ate at the tree was not knowledge at all. It was a mistake — a way of seeing
double that the tradition, at its clearest, keeps trying to correct. The devil was never a second
god. Read the book of Job and the adversary is not an independent kingdom; he is a member of the
divine court, on a leash. There are not two. There was only ever one, and we have spent the whole of
our exile from the garden refereeing a war between forces only one of which was ever there.

## V. What Jesus Does With the Two Powers

Everything Jesus does is unintelligible until you see it as the refusal of the second power.

Much of the religion built in His name runs *on* the two powers. It does not question them; it enlists on the good
side and calls the enlistment holiness. Saved and damned. Clean and unclean. Scherer mentions, in
passing, that he grew up forbidden to listen to Slipknot — his father, "not religious" and no believer
in God or Satan either, took one look at the band and pronounced it "satanic." The two-power reflex
doesn't even need a religion to run it: a man who believed in none of it still reached, on sight,
for the darker of the two forces and filed a group of masked musicians under it, certain he himself
stood with the light. And Corey, who spent that whole decade of what he calls "a satanic panic" cast
as the enemy in other people's war, answers more gently than it deserves — that, he tells Scherer,
says more about his father than it does about him.

Set beside the man it claims to follow, that machinery seizes. Jesus does not take
the good side of the war between two powers. He calls off the war. *Resist not evil,* He says — do
not even fight it, because to fight a thing is to grant it the standing of a power, and He will not
grant it that.[^7] *Judge not* — put down the referee's whistle you picked up at the tree. To the
crowd holding rocks: *He that is without sin among you, let him first cast a stone at her* — and the
courtyard empties, and to the woman left standing: *Neither do I condemn thee.*[^8] And then the
sentence that should end the refereeing business for good, spoken to a man who came looking for the
top grade. Someone calls Him *Good Master,* and Jesus stops him cold: *Why callest thou Me good? there
is none good but one, that is, God.*[^9]

The one place every religious person is most desperate to stand — on the
good side, the winning power's side — Jesus knocks out from under the very man reaching for it. The
rich young ruler wanted his side of the ledger validated; what he got instead was the news that the
ledger, and the two sides it kept, were the thing he had to give away. He couldn't. Almost nobody
can. Because being told there are not two powers, and no good side to win onto, feels to the divided
self exactly like being told there is no self — and it is not wrong to feel that, because the self it
means is the one built at the tree. Call it the empty self. Not empty because it isn't there — it is
there every morning — but because there is nothing under it. It is a position, not a person. It has a
voice and a grievance and a scoreboard, and it has no ground.

*None good but God* is the end of that self's career, because its career was grading: itself, hour
by hour, against a standard it had appointed itself to keep, and everyone else against the same. He
does not tell the young man he is bad. He tells him the grade was never his to give or to get. That is
not a harder rule. It is the end of the rule, and it is the one thing the empty self cannot survive,
which is why the young man went away sorrowful.

This is why the belief in two powers is not a flaw in our following of Jesus. It is the thing that
makes following Him impossible. You cannot join a man who has called off the war while your entire
operating system is the fighting of it. He is standing in the no man's land we cannot cross, with the
people we drew the line against, because to Him there was never a line, only people, and one power
under all of them.

## VI. One Power, and a Someone

Tear down a worldview and you owe the reader an account of what stands when it's gone. What does
Jesus put where the two powers used to be? Not a better refereeing. And not, either, the thing you
may be expecting from an essay that has said *one* this many times: a oneness to see through to, a
single Self behind all the faces that you are supposed to realize you are. What He puts there is a
Someone.

*That they all may be one,* He prays the night before He dies, *as Thou, Father, art in Me, and I in
Thee.*[^10] The oneness He means is the one He has with His Father — and He and the Father are not
the same person. It is a oneness of will and of love between two who address each other, and He asks
for us to be let into it. *I am the vine, ye are the branches* — a single life running through every
branch, and every branch still a branch; the one that thinks itself a separate life has only
forgotten where the sap comes from, and the cure is not to become the vine.[^11] And the test He says
the whole judgment turns on drops the *as if* we keep trying to smuggle back in: whatever you did to
the least of these, He says, you did *to Me.*[^12] Not *as though* to Me. He is in the one in front of
you. He is not identical with them, and He is not absent from them, and you were dealing with Him the
whole time and did not know it.

Even the commandment we quote most and examine least reads this way once the second power is gone.
*Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself* — not only as much as yourself, but as one the same Life
holds, because the One who looks out of your eyes looks out of theirs.[^13] Paul, reaching for a picture,
lands on a body: many members, one body, and the eye cannot say unto the hand *I have no need of
thee* — not because the eye is secretly the hand, but because neither of them is the head. *By Him,*
Paul says, *all things consist.* One power. Many someones. And a Someone who holds them.

Corey Taylor, sober and stripped of his war, reached for this at that last meal and could only find
studio-rat words for it. Life is "symbiotic," he says; a series of systems, all
of them permeating each other. He gropes for an image and finds the one on the floor of every stage
he's played: a nest of tangled cables. You look at it, he says, and think you'll never untangle it —
and the best of it is that you do not have to, because they are all running to different places and
all doing the same thing. Every one of them, in his phrase, built to make sound, to make "beautiful
noise." That is the vine and the branches in the only vocabulary he had, and the detail he gets right
is the one people who talk about oneness get wrong: the cables are all running to *different places*,
and no cable becomes another. And the reckoning he describes is the ethic of one body exactly:
starting over by looking at how he had treated the people who worked with him, the people he loved,
and "people who I didn't need anything from." Regard with nothing to win from it.

## VII. How the War Ends

There is a way to hear all of this as a new and heavier demand — *stop judging, love your enemies,
see the one* — a fresh scoreboard to lose on. That would be the two powers swallowing the gospel
again. So it matters exactly how the war ended for Corey, because it did not end by his winning it.

It ended by his laying it down. He woke up one day, about three years before this interview, and
could no longer stand the man he had built. "I wasn't happy," he says. "I had hurt everyone I love."
Not a victory — a surrender. What rebuilt him was not willpower aimed at the enemy; it was the
abandonment of the whole campaign: three therapists at once, a twelve-step program, and, because he
couldn't trust the God the panic had handed him, a higher power he had to improvise from scratch. He
calls it "Lord Mother," and describes it without any idea whose words he is speaking: "the yin and
the yang. It's the alpha and the omega. It's the ending, the beginning, and it's everything around
us." Everything around us. One power, in a metalhead's mouth — and a *Someone*, before it is a
doctrine. He did not find a principle. He found a name to say things to.[^14]

The two-power world says: fight the darkness, resist the evil, win. The tradition says: stop. You cannot defeat a second power, because there isn't one; there is only the fight
itself, keeping you from the One who was always here. Corey didn't beat his demons. He stopped
granting them the war. Once he redirected that gravity, he says, the old rage "shrunk." He does a
gratitude list every night now — his wife, his daughter, the people behind the curtain, the person
who made his coffee — because life, as he puts it, isn't just about you. He set the scoreboard down,
and the wall it was written on came down with it.

And no bill comes due. This is the other thing the two-power world can't imagine — a God who does
not punish. The wreckage of Corey's old life was real, but it was never a sentence handed down from
a throne; it was what that way of living produces, the way a stone dropped produces a fall.
*Whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap.*[^15] Reaping is not wrath. It is not a second
power called justice balancing the scales against a first power called mercy. It is one law, in one
world, and the moment the sowing changes, so does the harvest. Corey is not being made to pay. He is
being, in the oldest sense of the word, forgiven — which turns out to mean nothing more mysterious
than this: the door was never locked, and there was never a warden.

## VIII. Two Objections, Named Before You Raise Them

By now two complaints are standing up, and both are good.

The first is the serious one. *If there are not two powers, if you erase good and evil, haven't you
erased right and wrong — was what happened to Corey as a child not evil?* Concede it completely,
because the objection is right about everything except its target. To deny two cosmic *powers* is not
to deny that a loving act and a cruel one differ, or that the harm done to a boy was anything less
than harm. The difference between kindness and cruelty is real, and it is worth your entire life.
What dissolves is not the difference between good and evil *acts.* It is the sorting of *persons*
into good and evil *kinds* — the filing of a human being, whole, onto the far side of a line, so that
we may stop owing them anything. Paul, staring at his own worst behavior, does not say *I am evil.* He
says: *it is no more I that do it, but sin that dwelleth in me.*[^16] The wrong is real; it is not
identical with the person. That is what Jesus does: fierce about the harm, never willing to lose the
actor. The act can be condemned. The person cannot be filed under *evil* and shelved, because there
is no such shelf.

The second objection is the cheap-sounding one. *This is just pantheism — "we're all one, man" — the
exact stoned epiphany you'd expect a metal singer to have over a bowl of nachos.* Grant the
resemblance. The beanbag version says
God is the sum of the stuff, a warm fog we are all dissolved in, so that nobody is finally anybody and
nothing is finally owed. This essay says there is one power and no second, and that the one power is
not a fog but a Someone — a Father Jesus talks to, a *Me* in the least of these, a name Corey Taylor
had to invent because he needed somebody to address. Pantheism has no one to say *Thy will be done*
to. This has no one else to say it to. And that is not a comfort. It is the hardest thing anyone has
ever been asked to believe, because it means the person you most want to keep on the far side of the
line — the abuser, the enemy, the one the panic marked satanic — is not a second power, and is
someone God has not left, and is loved by the same One who loves you. "We're all one, man" is a
beanbag. This is a cross. The two only rhyme.

## IX. Eat, Die, and Everybody Who Looks Like You

The two things the show is built on turn out to be the whole argument in disguise. The reason
Scherer can seat anyone — the rock
star, the addict, the nihilist — and reach the same honest place is that he starts from the two facts
no line survives: everyone eats, and everyone dies.

Everyone eats. Strip away the arenas and the masks and the scoreboard, and the body is one need, the
same need, in every person who ever lived. There is no good side and evil side of hunger. And
everyone dies — which is the leveler that finally undoes the divided self, because the divided self is
the one thing that does not survive it. Ask Corey what he thinks death is and, again without knowing
whose language he's speaking, he describes a return: energy, he says, cannot be created or destroyed,
so his own will be given back to the power source and dealt out again to whoever's next. He means it
as a shrug. It is not a shrug. The self he spent forty-nine years defending turns out to have been on
loan, about to be returned to the One it was borrowed from. He built a whole war to protect an orbit,
and the last fact of his biology is that there was never anything at the center of it to protect.
Only an empty self that thought there was. The man is not empty. The position was.

The line, one more time — the one you have been on the right side of your whole life.
Between you and the enemy. Between you and God, off across the field, commanding the good army
against the bad. And between you and a fifty-two-year-old metal singer eating chicken-fried steak
under a slightly sublethal dose of gravy, with a ceiling-fan scar on his forehead and a made-up god
named Lord Mother, telling a food channel the truest thing he knows. To follow Jesus all the way down
is to watch that line go transparent — to see that there were never two powers with you enlisted on
the better one, only one Being, looking out through your enemy's eyes and your own with the same
light, and loving what He sees. And the sentence that began as a rebuke turns, at the very end, into
the last light in the room: *there is none good but one, that is, God* — none; not you, not your
enemy, not the boy in the makeup — and God is the only good there is, and is in every one of those
faces, eating with them and dying with them and bringing every one of them home.

What that leaves you to do is smaller than you were afraid of. There was a lay brother in a Carmelite
kitchen in Paris, three and a half centuries ago, who never resolved a single question about good and
evil and did not try. He said he *was pleased when he could take up a straw from the ground for the
love of GOD, seeking Him only, and nothing else, not even His gifts.*[^straw] That is what stands where
the two powers stood. Not a verdict you finally get right. A straw, picked up for Someone, and the
grading left to Him.

At the end the host asks for his last words — the actual last words, this being a last meal. And
Corey, who came in a nihilist and spent the hour accidentally testifying, points at the camera and
delivers them, twice, as an obscenity aimed at everybody who looks like him. The host plays it for the
laugh — Corey Taylor, ladies and gentlemen; what are you promoting, inner peace? — and Corey, with
nothing to sell, gives the real last word: make every day matter.

It is not a benediction. It's a man told to say something final who tells the camera to f\*\*k off.
But that is the point, and I would not clean it up if I could. The curse turns before he can even finish
it: *and everybody who looks like you.* He cannot aim the contempt at the audience without,
in the same breath, widening it to take in everyone — which is the tell that it was never really
contempt. No product to plug, no persona left to defend, the mask he made a career of just a thing on
the table beside the gravy, and what comes out of him is a middle finger that can't help opening into
an embrace. And then, when the host jokes that what he's promoting must be inner peace, he says the
truest small thing he has: *make every day matter.* He thinks he's flubbing an outro. He's saying the
whole essay. The one Life, looking out of a recovering nihilist in a diner set, telling everybody who
looks like him — everybody — that there was never an enemy here.

Not a verdict. A table, and a seat at it with your name on the card, set by Someone else.
