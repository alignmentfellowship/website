## I. The object

It's in your pocket, and it weighs nothing.

Open the app and there's a search field at the top. You don't have to know where a verse is. You
don't have to know the verse. Type three words you half remember and it returns every place they
occur — in about the time it takes to lift your thumb, in any translation you want, for nothing.

It's a beautiful piece of engineering, and it's a machine. Every part of it is built for a single
operation — looking things up.

Nobody in the gospels could have held one.

What existed then was scrolls. Held by a community rather than a person, kept in a synagogue
rather than a house, taken out, unrolled, read aloud, rolled up, put back. Scripture was something
you *heard*, standing in a room with other people, in a society where the great majority could not
read at all.[^literacy] There was no bound book to hold open on your lap; the codex is a later
technology and largely a Christian one. There were no chapter numbers and no verse numbers, so
there was no way to cite a sentence — you cited by quoting, or by naming a scroll and trusting
your hearer knew it. And there was no New Testament. Nobody in the New Testament ever read the New
Testament.

The apparatus is younger than it feels, and its order of assembly is the interesting part. Chapter
divisions are conventionally credited to Stephen Langton, at Paris, around 1227. The first
concordance follows within about three years — the Latin index compiled by Hugh of Saint-Cher and
the Dominicans of Saint-Jacques, finished in 1230, which locates a word by book and chapter because
there were no verses yet to locate it by.[^concordance] You can't build an index until somebody has
invented addresses, and the moment the addresses existed, someone built the index. Verse numbers
arrive three centuries later still, with Robert Estienne's Greek New Testament of 1551. The ability
to cite a single sentence of scripture is younger than the printing press and barely older than the
King James. The concordance in the back of an English reader's Bible is younger than
either — Alexander Cruden's, 1737. The search field is younger than most of the people using it.

So the object is new. The character is not.

Open the gospels and he's already there: fully drawn, recurring, and cast as the antagonist. The
man who knows the text. Who can produce the relevant passage. Who produces it at the precise moment
a human being in front of him needs something else. No search field, and no need of one.
Which means the thing the gospels keep describing was never a technology at all. It's a relationship
to a text — and every improvement in the technology has only made that relationship easier to fall
into and faster to act on.

[They/Them](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/theythem) made the case that the picture of God most of
us carry — the pronoun, the face — was installed rather than revealed, and that the tradition's own
oldest law tells us not to hang it. This is the same argument about different material. The picture
made of grammar and pigment isn't the only one there is. There's one made of paper, and it's harder
to recognize as a picture, because it's assembled out of the very words that told us not to make
one.

The lookup machine is eight hundred years old. The addresses it runs on are barely older. The
problem it solves is older than both, and so is the problem it causes.

## II. The villains are the serious ones

The people who oppose Jesus most persistently in the gospels are not the ignorant, and they are not
the wicked; they're the ones who know scripture best.

That sentence has an ugly history. Christians have used it for two thousand years to mean something
it doesn't mean — that a particular people were spiritually deaf, and that we are not. So,
specifically, what's true.

The Pharisees were not an elite but a popular reform movement, largely lay: in E. P.
Sanders's description, mostly ordinary people concerned to study, interpret, and apply the biblical
law.[^sanders] The elite were the Sadducees — aristocratic, tied to the temple, holding the
chief-priestly seats — and they're a different group entirely. Blurring the two is the commonest
careless move here, and it's careless in a particular direction: it files a lay reform
movement under *the establishment*, which is the reverse of what they were.

After the temple fell in 70, the Sadducees effectively vanished and the Pharisaic stream became the
root of rabbinic Judaism. To caricature the Pharisees is therefore to caricature the ancestors of a
living faith — one with its own two thousand years of holiness, and its own martyrs, a great many of
them killed by people holding the book this argument is made of.

And the gospel portrait is polemical. It was written from inside an intra-Jewish argument, by one
party to it, and then read for two millennia by the side that went on to win the surrounding
culture. That is the worst combination imaginable for fairness: a family quarrel preserved in one
brother's words and handed to an empire.

The gospels themselves supply the correction, in quantity. Jesus eats at Pharisees' tables,
repeatedly and by invitation (Luke 7:36, 11:37, 14:1). A Pharisee comes to warn Him that Herod means
to kill Him (Luke 13:31). Nicodemus is one. Gamaliel defends the apostles before the council (Acts
5:34–39). Paul, on trial for his life, claims the identity in the present tense — *I am a Pharisee,
the son of a Pharisee* (Acts 23:6). And the arguments themselves run the way rabbis argue: citation
against citation, text against text. You do not trade proof-texts with someone you hold beneath you.

Amy-Jill Levine states the hazard plainly: making the Pharisees, and through them Judaism, look bad
in order to make Jesus look good is bad history, bad theology, and bad morality.[^levine] All three,
and the third is the one that governs here.

There's a sharper version of the problem, and it sits inside the chapter this essay is built on.
John mostly doesn't write *the Pharisees*; he writes *the Jews*, and that phrase in that gospel has
underwritten more Christian violence than any other three words in the New Testament. It's coming
shortly, in a verse quoted here for its flattest and hardest line, and it is neither softened nor
paraphrased. What it means, in the scene, is a specific set of sabbath-observant, scripture-serious
people standing at a particular pool on a particular morning. It doesn't mean a people. Reading it
as a people isn't a small interpretive error. It's the error, and it has a body count.

Which brings the difficulty home. If the gospels can be read as a story about ancient Jews who read
too much, then everyone reading has been quietly let off, and the text has been converted into the
one thing it least wants to be: an account of somebody else.

The figure on those pages is not a Jew who read too much. It's the devout person for whom the text
became the thing itself. That posture is available in every tradition that has a book, and it isn't
distributed evenly — it's most available to whoever is most serious. Nobody drifts into knowing
scripture well. You get there by discipline, by early mornings, by years. The failure the gospels
keep drawing is a failure of effort, not of laziness, which is why it goes on catching the best
people in the room.

And one more turn, which everything after this depends on. The feeling that rises when you picture
the woman with the ready citation and the verse already open — the small, warm contempt, the
certainty that you wouldn't do that — is itself the posture under examination. A verdict passed at
a distance, on a person you haven't met, on the strength of what you already know. That's the
mechanism, entire. It doesn't stop being the mechanism when the text you're confident about is this
one.

## III. John 5, straight through

The tradition's sharpest sentence about leaning too hard on scripture is spoken by Jesus, and the
scene that produces it also contains a crutch. One chapter, one continuous argument — and the two
halves are usually read a hundred pages apart.

There's a pool in Jerusalem by the sheep market, called Bethesda, with five porches, and under them
lie *a great multitude of impotent folk, of blind, halt, withered* (John 5:2–3). Among them is a man
with *an infirmity thirty and eight years* (5:5) — longer, probably, than most people at that pool
have been alive. Jesus asks him something that sounds almost cruel until you've been stuck a long
time: *Wilt thou be made whole?* (5:6)

The man doesn't say yes; he explains the mechanism of his failure: *Sir, I have no man, when the
water is troubled, to put me into the pool: but while I am coming, another steppeth down before me*
(5:7). Thirty-eight years have furnished him with a complete and accurate account of why it can't
work. That is what the years give you.

*Rise, take up thy bed, and walk* (5:8). And he does.

And here is the response that entered the record:

> *It is the sabbath day: it is not lawful for thee to carry thy bed.* (5:10)

A man walked for the first time in a generation, and the reply was a citation.

The objection isn't stupid, which is the part worth sitting with. It's correct. Carrying a burden on
the sabbath is prohibited; the man doing it was, minutes earlier, a legitimate object of sabbath
mercy and is now visibly in violation. Everyone in that exchange is being consistent. Consistency is
what the apparatus produces, and consistency is not the same thing as sight.

Then the detail the cliché misses. The man isn't told to leave the bed; he's told to carry it. The
mat is not destroyed, not renounced, not left by the water as evidence. It goes with him.

The dispute escalates from there (5:16–18), the answer runs long, and near the end of it comes this:

> *Search the scriptures; for in them ye think ye have eternal life: and they are they which testify
> of Me. And ye will not come to Me, that ye might have life.* (5:39–40)[^caps]

The King James renders that first verb as a command, and four centuries of English-speaking piety
have taken it as one. *Search the scriptures* has been a motto on study-Bible covers and
Sunday-school walls for as long as there have been either.

The form doesn't settle it. The Greek is identical in the indicative and the imperative — *you
search* and *search!* are the same word — and that much is conceded even by those who argue hardest
for the command.[^eraunate] What tips it is the sentence around it. Every neighboring verb describes
what these hearers are already doing: *ye have neither heard*, *nor seen*, *ye have not [Their]
word*, *ye believe not*, *ye think*. And the clause immediately after reads as the second half of a
portrait rather than the sequel to an instruction — *and ye will not come to Me*. Leon Morris's
judgment is the ordinary modern one: almost certainly indicative.[^morris] The imperative reading
is genuinely available and has a long line of readers behind it, and nothing here needs it dead.

Because the argument doesn't live in that verb. It lives in the middle clause, and the middle clause
survives either reading.

Not *ye search*, which is neutral, or good. Not *they testify of Me*, which is the highest thing
that could be said of a text. The sentence turns on the five words at its center: *in them ye think
ye have eternal life.*

The error named is not reading. It isn't reading too much, either — there's no such thing, and
nothing here says otherwise. The error is thinking the life is in the book.

And *they are they which testify of Me* is not a consolation handed to scripture after a scolding.
It's the job description. A testimony is a pointing. A witness mistaken for the thing witnessed has
been honored in the one way that makes them useless — you can praise a signpost all afternoon and
never arrive anywhere, and the more beautiful the signpost, the more reasonable it looks to stop.

The chapter ends on a line often heard as an attack, which is nearly its opposite:

> *For had ye believed Moses, ye would have believed Me; for he wrote of Me.* (5:46)

That's not a complaint about scripture. It's the largest claim anybody in the chapter makes for it —
that the writings are so faithful to their subject that believing them and believing Him should have
been one act. The charge isn't that they read Moses. It's that they read Moses closely, for years,
and didn't recognize the person Moses was describing when He was standing in the room.

## IV. We have piped unto you

The tradition's own image for this failure is musical, and it belongs to Jesus.

> *But whereunto shall I liken this generation? It is like unto children sitting in the markets,
> and calling unto their fellows, and saying, We have piped unto you, and ye have not danced; we
> have mourned unto you, and ye have not lamented.* (Matthew 11:16–17)

Children in a marketplace, calling across to other children who won't play. One group starts a
wedding game and pipes; nobody dances. Then they switch to a funeral game and wail; nobody mourns.
It's a small, exact picture, and the very next lines show what it's diagnosing.

> *For John came neither eating nor drinking, and they say, He hath a devil. The Son of man came
> eating and drinking, and they say, Behold a man gluttonous, and a winebibber, a friend of
> publicans and sinners.* (11:18–19)

Two opposite tunes. The ascetic in the desert and the man at the dinner table — as far apart as two
lives can get — and each was found wanting on the grounds of not being the other. That is the point
of putting them side by side. If only one had been rejected, the complaint would be about
discernment: they judged wrongly, they picked the wrong prophet. Both are rejected, so the
complaint is about something else. **Nothing played moved them.** The verdict was already in, and
the music was audited against it rather than heard.

That is a description of a faculty, not of an opinion. And the tradition has a harder text for it,
which Jesus quotes in His own voice, out of Isaiah:

> *By hearing ye shall hear, and shall not understand; and seeing ye shall see, and shall not
> perceive: for this people's heart is waxed gross, and their ears are dull of hearing.*
> (Matthew 13:14–15, quoting Isaiah 6:9–10)

*Dull of hearing.* The condition has a name in the tradition, and it's an ear condition.

What follows in the same verse is the sentence people quietly skip, and it shouldn't be softened:

> *…lest at any time they should see with their eyes, and hear with their ears, and should
> understand with their heart, and should be converted, and I should heal them.* (13:15)

*Lest… I should heal them.* The eyes are closed and the ears are dulled **against being healed**.
Which means the deafness isn't simple misfortune. Something is being protected by it, and healing
would cost whatever that is. Anyone who has held a position long enough to have built a life on it
knows the arithmetic without needing it explained. (And the same caution applies here as before:
*this people* in Isaiah's mouth is Isaiah's own people, addressed by their own prophet, in the
oldest and most inward form of self-criticism there is. It becomes something else entirely when
it's aimed outward by people who were never in the room.)

Then there's the formula nobody counts. *He that hath ears to hear, let him hear* — and its
variants, *who hath ears* and *if any man have ears* — turns up in Matthew, Mark, and Luke, and
then again seven times over in Revelation, once to each of the seven churches.[^ears] It's the
tradition's standing call for reception, repeated more often than almost anything else attributed
to Jesus.

It is never *let him read*.

## V. What the writing was for

Every place scripture states its own purpose, the purpose sits outside the text. The purpose
clauses are all *that*s, and every *that* points past the page.

*These are written,* John says, ***that** ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God;
and that believing ye might have life through His name* (20:31). The terminus is belief, and then
life — neither of which happens on paper. Paul: *whatsoever things were written aforetime were
written for our learning, **that** we through patience and comfort of the scriptures might have
hope* (Romans 15:4). Hope is the object; the scriptures are the means. Neither sentence has its
endpoint on the page.

The prophets take it further than that. Jeremiah's new covenant is a promise about where the law
ends up:

> *I will put My law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts… and they shall teach no
> more every man his neighbour, and every man his brother, saying, Know the LORD: for they shall
> all know Me.* (Jeremiah 31:33–34)

The promised end state of the written law is a condition in which nobody has to look it up. It
isn't that the text failed. It finished. And Moses says something similar about where the
word is meant to live: *it is not in heaven… neither is it beyond the sea… But the word is very
nigh unto thee, in thy mouth, and in thy heart, that thou mayest do it* (Deuteronomy 30:11–14, and
Paul quotes it at Romans 10:8). Every clause is about the word getting off the scroll.

And then the gospels supply the experiment.

Two people are walking to Emmaus on the afternoon of the resurrection, and a stranger falls in with
them. *But their eyes were holden that they should not know Him* (Luke 24:16). He asks what they're
discussing, listens to them get it wrong, and then does the thing:

> *And beginning at Moses and all the prophets, He expounded unto them in all the scriptures the
> things concerning Himself.* (24:27)

That's the most complete exposition of scripture in the New Testament, delivered by its subject,
to two people who wanted it, over a walk of some hours. Every text, correctly explained, by the one
they were about.

And they still didn't know Him.

The recognition, when it comes, comes somewhere else. At the village they press Him to stay, and
they sit down to eat — *and He took bread, and blessed it, and brake, and gave to them. And their eyes were
opened, and they knew Him* (24:30–31). Not at the exegesis. At the table, at a gesture, at
supper.

And the text makes the concession itself, in full: the exposition was not nothing.
*Did not our heart burn within us, while He talked with us by the way, and while He opened to us
the scriptures?* (24:32) **The burning was real.** Something true was happening for hours, and they
felt it, and they were right to feel it. It just wasn't recognition. The road did what roads do —
it got them to the table.

And the sentence Luke leaves the scene on doesn't mention the scriptures at all: they told the
others *how He was known of them in breaking of bread* (24:35).

## VI. The objections, at full strength

Three objections. The first two are answerable. The third one isn't.

**The first is the tradition's highest sentence about its own text.**

> *All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for
> correction, for instruction in righteousness: that the man of God may be perfect, thoroughly
> furnished unto all good works.* (2 Timothy 3:16–17)

The weight of that objection is usually misplaced, including by people arguing against it. It does
**not** rest on *profitable* — that's knocking over something nobody is holding. It rests on the
purpose clause: *thoroughly furnished unto **all** good works.* If the text equips a person for
every good work there is, what's left for a voice to add?

It doesn't need to be beaten. Two things are true of it, and neither is a knockdown.

The first is what the sentence says is complete, which isn't the text. *That the **man of God** may
be perfect, thoroughly furnished* — the completeness belongs to a person, and the scriptures are
what equipped him. A tool that leaves a worker fully equipped has been praised exactly this highly,
and still hasn't been called the work.

The second is the direction the sentence faces. *Furnished unto all good works.* Grant sufficiency
entirely — grant that nothing else is needed — and the terminus named is still **works**, out in a
life, among people. Taken at its strongest, the objection points at the door.

One more thing, and it's a point about reference rather than authority: when 2 Timothy was written,
*all scripture* could not have denoted a collection containing 2 Timothy. The first list of exactly
the twenty-seven books an English reader now holds is Athanasius's Easter letter of 367.[^canon]
Whatever *graphē* meant in that sentence, it did not mean the object this essay opened on.

**The second objection is a direct counterexample.**

> *These were more noble than those in Thessalonica, in that they received the word with all
> readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so.* (Acts 17:11)

Searching, commended — and commended in the comparative. *More noble.* The Bereans are the people
this essay is supposedly worried about, and Luke gives them a medal.

So look at what they were searching *against*. A living man had come into their synagogue and was
saying something to them, and they went to the text to test it. The word came first; the searching
was the test. And the sentence that follows is *therefore many of them believed* (17:12). The
searching served the hearing, and it ended in something happening to them. That is not a
counterexample. It is the proposal, with the order intact.

Psalm 119 belongs here too, and it deserves better than a concession. A hundred and seventy-six
verses of unembarrassed love for the law of God — delight, longing, waking in the night to think
about it — and none of it is a mistake or an early symptom. It is one of the great love poems in
any language, and its most quoted line has been handled by
[The Highest Peak](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-highest-peak), which reads *thy word is a
lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path* (119:105) for what it actually promises: enough light
for the next few feet, and not a map of the country. A lamp is carried. It is for walking.

**The third objection is the real one, and it isn't going to be answered here.**

*How do you know the voice isn't your own?*

I don't have an answer to that, and I've stopped looking for one.

The history behind that question isn't hypothetical. People have done monstrous things, to other
people, on the strength of an inner voice they were entirely certain of — and the text is the oldest
check the tradition has against exactly that. It's what a person can be held to when they claim God
told them something. Any argument that loosens the check owes the reader an accounting, and the
accounting can't be *trust yourself more*.

What can honestly be said is narrower. The check is real, and it was never meant to be operated
alone. *Believe not every spirit, but try the spirits whether they are of God* (1 John 4:1) — an
instruction to test, addressed to a community. When the early church faced the largest question it
ever faced, it did not resolve it by lookup; it argued in a room, at length — *when there had been
much disputing* (Acts 15:7) — and sent out a letter that began *it seemed good to the Holy Ghost,
and to us* (15:28). And the test offered for distinguishing a true voice from a convincing one is
neither a citation nor a feeling: *ye shall know them by their fruits* (Matthew 7:16), which is
slow, public, and checkable by other people. Love, joy, peace, longsuffering (Galatians 5:22–23).
You cannot fake those for very long in front of witnesses.

None of that closes the question, and pretending otherwise would be a cheat. A person can be wrong
about a voice. A person can also be wrong about a verse, and has been, at scale — the same book has
been read to sanctify slavery and to abolish it, and both readers had the apparatus open.

The claim here is smaller than the objection needs it to be. **A check is not a destination.**
Nobody argues against a crutch under a broken leg; there it is simply correct. The
argument is about what happens when the leg heals and the crutch stays — and about a person who has
decided, in advance, that it never will.

## VII. Obey is a hearing word

*Obey* doesn't mean submit to a rule. In all three of the languages this tradition was written and
transmitted in, the word for obedience is built out of the word for listening.

Etymology isn't meaning, and this is exactly the kind of observation that gets stretched into
claiming it is. Where a word came from doesn't settle what anybody means by it now. What a shared
derivation records is narrower and still worth having: **what the concept was modeled on.**

Hebrew has one verb, **שָׁמַע** *shama'*, doing both jobs — to hear and to heed are not two acts
requiring two words. Where an English Bible says *obey*, this is very often what's underneath it.
And it's the first word of the prayer at the center of the tradition: *Hear, O Israel* (Deuteronomy
6:4).

Latin builds the word out of the same material. *Oboedire* is *ob-* plus *audire* — to listen
toward. English *obey* comes down through Old French from exactly that.

Greek does it a third time. **ὑπακούω** *hypakouō* is *hypo-* plus *akouō*: to hear under. And it
isn't an abstraction in a lexicon — it's what a doorkeeper does. When Peter knocks in the middle of
the night, *a damsel came to hearken, named Rhoda* (Acts 12:13), and the verb under *came to
hearken* is that one. Obedience, in that sentence, means going to the door because somebody is on
the other side of it.

Three languages, at three different times, reaching for the same material. That doesn't prove what
obedience is. It records what it was taken to be: **a response to someone.** And it means the
question *whose voice?* was never an eccentric one to ask. It's the question the word is made of.

Which is why the tradition keeps telling the story the way it does. A boy is sleeping in the temple.
*Now Samuel did not yet know the LORD, neither was the word of the LORD yet revealed unto him*
(1 Samuel 3:7) — no training, no office, nothing — and he's the one who hears it. Eli has the
office and the training and the whole text, and Eli doesn't hear a thing.

Eli should be read generously, because the text does: he works out what's happening, he knows
exactly what to do about it, and his whole contribution is one sentence long: *Speak, LORD; for thy
servant heareth* (3:9). That's real expertise, correctly applied, and it's enough. It's also all of
it. Nothing in the story is against Eli; what it's against is mistaking Eli's sentence for the
voice. And when the boy finally answers, he doesn't say it the way he was taught — he drops the
name. *Speak; for thy servant heareth* (3:10).

Elijah gets the same lesson from the other direction. Not the great wind, not the earthquake, not
the fire — *but the LORD was not in the wind*, and not in the fire — and then *a still small voice*
(1 Kings 19:11–12). The three loud things are precisely the three that would have been
unmistakable. Whatever else is going on there, it is not a story about God making Themself easy to
verify. Wind, earthquake, fire — every one of them louder than a voice. So is a citation. What had
to be heard was underneath all of it.

And then, on the last night, the sentence that ought to be more disturbing than it is:

> *I have yet many things to say unto you, but ye cannot bear them now. Howbeit when [She], the
> Spirit of truth, is come, [She] will guide you into all truth.* (John 16:12–13)

That is not an argument that the canon is open, and nothing here is.[^spirit] The smaller and
defensible claim: whatever that promise is, it is a promise of continuing guidance **to persons** —
made to men who already had scripture and were about to be handed more of it. It says the speaking
didn't stop. It says nothing whatever about what gets bound between covers.

Paul puts it in a present tense that's easy to read past: *as many as are **led** by the Spirit of
God, they are the sons of God* (Romans 8:14). Not *were led*. Led — which is what happens to someone
currently walking, on a road, who therefore has to keep listening for Her who is doing the
leading.

## VIII. Two verbs the library hangs from

A lawyer asks a text question — *which is the great commandment in the law?* (Matthew 22:36) It's
the right question, asked the normal way, by someone qualified to ask it.

The answer isn't a text. Love God with everything you have. Love your neighbor as yourself. And
then the line that should reorganize the shelf:

> *On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets.* (22:40)

*Hang.* The entire written tradition, suspended from two acts. That's the reverse of the picture
most of us arrive with, in which the acts are conclusions carefully drawn out of the library. The
library hangs off the acts. Which means a reading that doesn't hold those two up has come off the
hook, whatever else can be said for it.

The same criterion turns up too often to be a proof-text. *Woe unto you… for ye pay tithe of mint
and anise and cummin, and have omitted the weightier matters of the law, judgment, mercy, and faith*
(23:23) — precision on the small thing, at the cost of the large one. And the clause people skip is
the one that keeps this honest: ***these ought ye to have done, and not to leave the other
undone.*** The mint and the anise are not abolished. Nobody is told to put anything down. The
correction is about **weight**.

Again, when an accusation is made against His disciples out of the text, He answers with a text —
and picks the one that says the weight is elsewhere: *I will have mercy, and not sacrifice* (12:7,
quoting Hosea). And once more from James, in a verse that is usually read as a warning against
passive reading: *be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only* (James 1:22). Even there, the word
is **hearers**.

So here is the practice, stated plainly, because a diagnosis that doesn't change what you do on a
Tuesday isn't worth the paper.

The prayer the tradition hands out as its model is not a request for information. *Thy kingdom come.
Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven* (Matthew 6:10). That is a petition to be **used**,
not to be answered. Under maximum pressure it's said again, and the honest half isn't edited out —
*Father, if thou be willing, remove this cup from Me: nevertheless not My will, but Thine, be done*
(Luke 22:42). The *nevertheless* stays. The wanting-away is real and is not pretended out of the
sentence. And Paul concedes the underlying problem outright: *we know not what we should pray for as
we ought* (Romans 8:26).

**So stop asking what you want from God. Ask what They want done, and offer to do it.**

That is a small change in what you say on an ordinary weekday, and it moves you across the whole
argument. A prayer that asks for an answer leaves you holding it — still the one who decides what the
answer is worth and what to do about it. A prayer that asks to be used puts you where Rhoda was — up,
and moving, because somebody is knocking.

## IX. Take up thy bed

I have been following Jesus since August. One month — which makes me close to the last person
entitled to tell anybody they read too much.

It's worse than inexperience. I was raised in the contempt this could easily have become.
Anti-theist household, anti-theist city: the devout were people who needed a comforting story,
and I was smarter and stronger than that. I didn't arrive
there by examining anyone. It was a verdict passed at a distance, on the strength of what I already
knew, about people standing in front of me.

That is the same verdict. It is John 5 with the polarity reversed and nothing else changed.

What moved me wasn't an argument, and I'd leave this out if I were building a case, because it
doesn't look like evidence.

I went out to Roosevelt Island to have lunch with a friend, and the moment I stepped off the train I
fell in love with the place. Afterward I walked north, stopping in the lobby of every apartment
building and asking for the leasing agent. On that walk I passed the Chapel of the Good Shepherd.
It was beautiful. I kept going.

Manhattan Park answered first. I signed a lease that day and moved in two days later, on a Saturday.
The next day was a snowstorm, and I walked back down to that chapel and went to church for the first
time in my life.

It was the singing.

Not the sermon, not the reading, not anything anybody said to me. Worship — for a reason I couldn't
yet understand. It went straight past the part of me that had spent my whole life on guard, and it
did it early, in a room I'd walked into mostly because the building was beautiful and it was
snowing.

And I had felt it before. Years earlier, in Los Angeles, I wandered into kirtan and stayed —
call-and-response chanting, most of it in Sanskrit — and then Bhakti Fest in the desert, and once
alongside Krishna Das. What that door was, and what it wasn't, is the subject of
[Krishna Is Not Christ](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/krishna-is-not-christ). What matters here is
only the mechanism: the same opening, under the intellect, where my defenses were never posted,
years before I had a single doctrine to attach it to.

I heard the music before I could read a word of it.

The reading came after. In April I first let myself consider that the gospels might be true — the
most humbling thing I have ever done, and a descent with its own essay in [The Way Home Is
Down](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-way-home-is-down) — and April is when I started studying
scripture. The following itself came in August.

So the order is exact, and the order is the argument. The singing in February. The book in April.
Following anyone at all, in August. The music arrived two months before I opened the text, and the
text had four more months of work to do after that — and what it turned out to be for was teaching
me to recognize a voice I had already heard once, under the words, without knowing whose it was.

So the standing this essay has was never *I read less than you do.* The object it opened on is the
one in my pocket. I open the app most days. Nothing here has been asking anyone to put it down.

Go back to the pool, because the text already did this and did it better. The man is not told to
leave the mat; he's told to carry it. The thing that held him up for thirty-eight years goes out the
door on his shoulder — into the street, on a working day, past people with strong opinions about it.
Same object. Opposite relationship. It used to carry him.

That's the entire proposal, and it was sitting in the sentence the whole time.

*Ye are not your own* (1 Corinthians 6:19). My life is not mine to live.

Read cold, that's what someone says who has been taken over, so it's worth being exact about what
stops being mine. Not the wanting, and not the will. The **ownership** — the life as a holding I
have to defend, account for, and author by myself, with no other voice admitted to the room. What
replaces it isn't a master with a rulebook. It's *led* (Romans 8:14), a present-tense verb about a
road, and what it costs me is the position of being the only one whose voice counts. That position
was the exhausting one. I mistook it for freedom for a very long time.

The most consequential yes in this tradition is spoken by a young woman with no text in the room at
all. *Behold the handmaid of the LORD; be it unto me according to thy word* (Luke 1:38). Somebody
said something to her, and she answered.

My phone goes everywhere I go. It will find me any word I can remember, and a good many I can't.

It has never once told me who was speaking.

[^literacy]: Estimates are contested and the category is soft — "literacy" in antiquity covers
    everything from signing a name to reading a scroll aloud, and reading and writing were taught as
    separate skills, so far fewer could write than read. Meir Bar-Ilan's calculation for Roman
    Palestine is that the total literacy rate "was probably less than 3%," with Catherine Hezser
    concurring and noting that literacy clustered in the towns while some villages fell below 1%
    (*Jewish Literacy in Roman Palestine*, 2001, which declines to fix a single rate and argues
    instead that Jewish Palestine had lower literacy and more restricted use of texts than the
    Greco-Roman world around it). William Harris puts the ceiling for antiquity generally at around
    10%. The claim this paragraph actually needs is the uncontested one: scripture was encountered
    by most people as something heard, not as something owned.

[^spirit]: The Spirit takes **She/Her** here, and the bracketed **[She]** in the quotation above is
    mine — the King James reads *when he, the Spirit of truth, is come, he will guide you*. The
    bracket marks the substitution rather than making it silently; an unmarked pronoun inside a
    quotation reads as the original. The feminine is the
    older habit rather than a new one: Hebrew *ruach* is feminine (Genesis 1:2 pairs it with the
    feminine participle *merachefet*, hovering, the verb used of a mother bird over her nest at
    Deuteronomy 32:11), Syriac *ruḥa* likewise, and the Spirit is feminine across the Acts of
    Thomas, Aphrahat, Ephrem, and the second-century Odes of Solomon. Greek *pneuma* is neuter and
    Latin *spiritus* masculine; English inherited its *he* from the Latin. Grammatical gender is not
    sex, which is the point — it cannot settle the question either way, so the pronoun is a choice,
    and *he* is the one that mistakes an accident of translation for something revealed.

[^caps]: Deity pronouns take the house capital in the body throughout (*of Me*, *they shall all
    know Me*, *they knew Him*), and LORD is set in capitals wherever the word names God; the King
    James lowercases the pronouns and reserves LORD for the divine Name, and the notes keep the King James. Square brackets elsewhere mark a pronoun for
    God that is this publication's rather than the King James's — at John 5:38 the King James reads
    *ye have not his word*.

[^eraunate]: The verb at John 5:39 is ἐραυνᾶτε, second person plural present active, in which the
    indicative and the imperative are identical in form — so the reading is a contextual judgment,
    not a grammatical one. This is granted by the imperative's own defenders: the Trinitarian Bible
    Society, arguing for the King James rendering, states that the verb "has exactly the same form
    in the imperative 'search!' as in the indicative 'you search.'" The imperative stands in
    Tyndale, Luther, the Geneva Bible, and the Authorized Version, and among modern commentators in
    R. C. H. Lenski; the indicative is carried by the NASB, NIV, NKJV, and ESV, and by a line
    running back through Westcott, Godet, Meyer, and Bengel.

[^ears]: The formula varies in the King James rather than repeating verbatim, which is worth
    noting since the point is its frequency, not a fixed phrase: *He that hath ears to hear, let

[^morris]: Leon Morris, on John 5:39: "We should almost certainly take it as indicative" — quoted,
