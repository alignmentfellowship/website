## I. The question, put at its sharpest

Here is the question, and I want to put it the way its strongest form puts it, not the
soft version a believer can answer in their sleep.

God is said to be all-loving, all-knowing, and all-powerful. Sin exists. Pick any two of
the three attributes and the third seems to break. If God is all-knowing, They saw sin
coming. If God is all-powerful, They could have made a world without it. If God is
all-loving, They would have wanted to. So either God wanted sin, and is not all-loving; or
couldn't prevent it, and is not all-powerful; or never saw it arrive, and is not
all-knowing. The atheist I used to be thought this was checkmate, and I no longer think he
was being lazy. It is a real question. It deserves a real answer and not a dodge, and most
of what gets offered in reply is a dodge — a hand waved at *mystery*, a quick pivot to how
sin is really our fault, an appeal to some greater good kept offstage where it can't be
examined.

I want to try to answer it in the open. And the answer begins by refusing the question's
first hidden assumption: that sin is a thing God made.

## II. God did not create sin the way They created trees

Run back through the list of what God is said to have created. Light. Water. Dry land.
The green things that grow, the fish in the sea and the birds in the air. A man, and then a
woman. Every item on that list has the same grammar: it is a *thing*, an object, some
piece of what there is, spoken into being and standing there afterward with an existence
of its own.

Sin is not on that list, and it is not the kind of item that could be. Sin is not a
substance. There is no shelf in creation where the sin was kept, no material God stirred
into the mix. Sin is not a thing at all; it is a *use* — a direction a will can take, a
bending of something good toward something it was not for. Cruelty is not a created object;
it is love's machinery turned to the opposite purpose. A lie is not a thing; it is the
faculty of speech, which is good, aimed at concealment. Evil, the tradition has said for a
very long time and in more careful language than I'm using, has no substance of its own. It
is a privation, a swerve, a good thing spent wrongly.[^augustine]

So when we ask why God created sin, we have already smuggled in a false picture — sin as
one more item on the manufacturing list, sitting between the oceans and the animals. God
did not create sin. What God created was something far stranger and far more dangerous than
any object: God created beings with wills of their own.

A will is not a thing either. It is a capacity to *originate* — to be, in some real and
frightening sense, a genuine source of what happens next, and not merely a channel through
which prior causes run. That is what it is to give a creature a will and mean it. And here
is the part there is no way around: to make a will that can genuinely say yes is to make a
will that can genuinely say no. You cannot make the first without making the second,
because they are not two powers but one. The capacity to choose *is* the capacity to choose
wrongly. God did not author the wrong choice. God authored the capacity — and the capacity,
by its nature, held the wrong choice inside it as a possibility from the first day.

This is worth being precise about, because the whole argument turns on it. The claim is not
that God made sin. The claim is that God made freedom, and that sin is what freedom looks
like when it is used against the One who gave it. The possibility was created. The act was
ours.

## III. The tree was a real no

The garden makes this concrete, which is what the garden is for.

God plants a tree and gives a single command about it: of every other tree, eat freely;
of this one, do not eat.[^gen2] Read as a trap, it is a strange and petty thing — a
forbidden object dangled in front of creatures who were bound to want it, a setup with a
punchline already written. But it is not a trap. It is the opposite of a trap. It is the
one place in a garden full of *yes* where a *no* is available. It is freedom given an
address.

Think about what a command actually is. A command you are incapable of breaking is not a
command; it is a description. If I "command" a stone to fall when I let go of it, I have not
issued an order, I have stated a law of nature. An order is only an order where disobedience
is possible. So the moment God gives Adam a real command — a *thou shalt not* that his hand
is fully able to reach past — God has done something with enormous consequences: They have
made the man free. The tree is not the thing that ruins the garden. The tree is the proof
that the garden was ever a place where love, and not mere mechanism, could happen.

I'll leave to one side what the fruit is said to do — what the *knowledge* of good and evil
is, and why coming to possess it reads in scripture less like an upgrade than like an exile.
That's another argument, and I've tried to make it elsewhere.[^kge] Here the tree matters
for one reason only: it is a choice that can go the other way. Take that away and you have
not made the garden safer. You have made it a diorama.

## IV. "But They knew"

Now the real objection arrives, and it is not the cartoon version. It is sharp, and if you
have felt the force of the question at all you have felt this.

Fine — a will that can say no. But God is all-knowing. God knew, before the man's hand
moved, exactly where it would go. God knew Adam would eat. So the freedom was a formality.
Why hand a creature a choice whose outcome you have already read? Why give the hands at all,
if you have already watched them reach? A test whose result is known in advance is not a
test; it is a performance with a foregone ending, and a cruel one, since the failure was
scheduled and the punishment real. That is the objection, and it is a good one. I want to
concede the whole of its factual premise before I say anything back. Yes. God knew.

But look hard at the move the objection makes in its middle, because that is where it goes
wrong. It slides from *God knew what the man would do* to *God made the man do it*, and
those are not the same, and the distance between them is the entire matter. Knowing is not
causing. I can know my friend well enough to tell you, with something close to certainty,
what he'll order at the restaurant before he opens the menu. My knowing does not reach into
him and place the order. He still chooses it; I have simply seen truly. Foreknowledge of a
free act is knowledge *of* a free act — the freedom is inside the thing known, not erased by
the knowing of it. An infinite mind seeing the whole of what a creature will freely do takes
nothing from the creature; it only means the seeing is complete.[^peak]

And there is a second, deeper thing the objection misses, which is this. The objection
assumes that foreknowing the bad outcome obligates God to prevent it — that if you can see
the no coming and you love, you close off the road to it. But closing off the road to the
no is closing off the road to the yes. They are the same road. To reach in and guarantee
the outcome — to make sure the hand could not reach the tree — is to unmake the freedom, and
with it everything the freedom was for. God did not prevent it, not because God is
indifferent to the cost, and not because God failed to see it, but because preventing it, at
the only price at which it could be prevented, would have cost the very thing the whole
arrangement exists to make possible.

Which brings us to what that thing is.

## V. The lock, from the inside

I know this part from the inside, and I would rather tell you that than hand you a
hypothetical.

There was a relationship I left. I am not going to narrate my way through another person's
heart, and I am not going to make anyone the villain of an argument about God — the person is
real, this is only ever my side of it, and the last thing I want is to win a point about love
by spending someone I once loved. So I will tell you only the part that was mine to feel,
because it is the whole reason this essay is not just tidy to me but true. What I could not,
in the end, talk myself out of was this: a love you are not free to leave slowly stops being
able to feel like love. Not because there is too little of it. Because there is no door in it
— and a devotion with no door in it does something to itself over time. The holding becomes
the whole substance, and the thing being held quietly goes out of the room. I stayed a long
while before I understood that staying, past a certain point, was not the loving thing it
looked like from outside. Then I left, and it was among the hardest things I have done, and I
do not get to tell it as a man who came out of it clean. Leaving is not innocent. I only know
that I could no longer call it love while I could not leave it — and that the leaving, which
looked from every angle like the failure of love, was the first thing in a long time that had
any freedom in it.

I tell you that not to litigate my own history in the footnotes of a theology, but because it
taught me, from the wrong side of the door, exactly what God will not do.

For love to be given freely there has to be a giver — someone standing there genuinely able
to say yes. And a someone genuinely able to say yes is, in the same breath and by the same
power, someone able to say no. You cannot have the one without the other. The yes and the no
are cut from a single cloth. A yes with no no available to it is not a yes at all; it is the
click of a mechanism, the fall of the stone when the hand opens. It carries no love because
there was no self behind it free to withhold.

God could have made that. This is the part I find almost unbearable to sit with. God could
have made a house full of creatures who mechanically adore Them — who turn toward Their
maker the way a sunflower tracks the light, with no more choice in it and no more meaning. A
room of dolls with their arms out. Perfect, unbroken, unanimous obedience, and not one grain
of it worth anything, because obedience that cannot be withheld is not the same as being
chosen. God did not want that. God did not want a house of prisoners
calling their captivity love. So God did the one thing that makes love possible and makes
everything that follows from it possible too. God built the door, and left it unlocked.

Freedom, in other words, is not a flaw in the design of love that God tolerated for the sake
of some other good. Freedom *is* the design. It is not the risk love runs; it is the
condition under which there is any such thing as love to run a risk at all.

## VI. The horror, named plainly

I am not going to spiritualize what that unlocked door has meant. To do so here, of all
places, would be its own kind of lie, and the argument does not need it.

The same freedom that makes it possible for me to love God freely makes it possible for me
to do the other thing, and the other thing has a history, and the history is not abstract.
The freedom that lets a person choose their maker is the identical freedom by which people
have chosen the camp, the auction block, the child's body, the mass grave. It is one
capacity. There is no version of it that comes with a governor installed, that permits the
yes and forbids the atrocity, because the moment you install the governor you are back to
the sunflower and the doll. We can hurt each other, without limit and without end, for the
same reason and by the same power that we can love each other at all. That is the terrible
arithmetic of the thing, and I will not pretend the sum comes out small.

So I am not going to tell you God is innocent of it. That is the sentimental move and it is
false. God is not innocent of the freedom's cost. God knew — this is the whole force of the
objection in section four, and I mean to keep it, not to have quietly disposed of it — God
knew what the freedom would cost, saw the mass graves from before the foundation, and made
us free anyway. There is a version of God who kept everyone safe by keeping everyone locked,
and that God does not exist, and the reason that God does not exist is that a locked safety
is not love and God would not counterfeit love even to spare us. Over Jerusalem, at the end,
the note is not triumph. It is grief with the door still open: *how often would I have
gathered thy children together, and ye would not.*[^matt] *And ye would not.* That is God, all-powerful,
declining to make the gathering happen by force, and grieving the refusal instead of
overriding it.

Hold that. Don't resolve it too fast. The honest thing to say is not that the cost is
acceptable — I don't know what scale that word would even be weighed on — but that the cost
is the price of the only thing that could ever have been worth paying it, and that God paid
in first and paid the most. Which is the last thing, and the thing that makes this a
specifically Christian answer and not just a philosopher's.

## VII. He came looking

Every version of the free-will answer I have given so far, an honest atheist can follow
right up to its edge and then stop, unmoved, and say: all right. Suppose all of that. Suppose
freedom is the price of love and God paid it by letting us loose. That only makes God the one
who opened the cage. We are still the ones bleeding in it. So God set the terms and walked
away — the deist's clockmaker, admirable, distant, and no comfort to anyone actually inside
the wreck the freedom made.

And this is exactly where Christianity says something that no other answer to the question
says, something that changes what kind of answer it is.

The story does not end at *fine.* It does not end with *you chose yourself, so you're on
your own now.* Go back to the garden, to the very first moment after the very first no. God
does not arrive with a sentence prepared. The first thing out of God's mouth, in the ruined
afternoon, is a question, and the question is a search: *Where art thou?*[^gen3] Not *how
dare you* — *where are you.* And of course They know where we are; omniscience does not
misplace anybody, and the question was never a request for information. It is the way a parent
says *now where could she be?* to a child hiding under a blanket with both feet sticking out —
not because they cannot see her, but because the looking is the love, and being found is the
whole point of the hiding. The maker walking the garden in the cool of the day, calling for a
creature They can see perfectly well, asking anyway, so that the one who is crouched there in
the leaves can hear that they are wanted and not hunted. That is the note struck at the
beginning, and the whole rest of the book is that note getting louder.

Because God, having authored the freedom and foreseen to the last grave what we would do
with it, did not stay outside the wreck. God came into it. Not as a rescuer lowering a rope
from a safe height, but the other way — all the way down, into a particular body, at a
particular address, under an occupying army. Christ enters the thing itself. Temptation: He
was tried at every point we are tried.[^heb] Humiliation: He emptied Himself and took the
form of a servant and was mocked and stripped.[^phil] Death: the state killed Him slowly and
in public. He goes into suffering, into the exact machinery our freedom built, and — this is
the hinge — He remains faithful in it where we did not. Where the first freedom said no, His
freedom, in the same kind of body, under worse pressure, says yes. Not a forced yes. A yes
that had every road to no wide open in front of it, in a garden of its own, at night, with
the cup right there.[^gethsemane]

So no. I do not think God created sin because They wanted us to suffer. I think God created
creatures who could genuinely choose Them, and a genuine yes means something for the one
reason that it could have been a no. God did not make us prisoners and call the captivity
love. God made us free — free enough to walk out the unlocked door, which is exactly what we
did — and then, rather than let the walking out be the end of it, came after us. Still comes.
The book ends not with a lock but with a knock: *Behold, I stand at the door.*[^rev] Notice
which side the handle is on. He does not break it down. He came all this way, into all of
it, and He is standing outside a door that opens from the inside, waiting to be let in by the
same freedom that let Him out.

Which means the question I started with has quietly turned into a different one. Not *why
would God create sin* — God didn't. The question that's left is the one standing at your
door with a hand already raised, and it was always going to come down to what you do with a thing
that can, genuinely, go either way.

in the argument depends on the technical apparatus; the plain point is that cruelty and the
lie have no material of their own — they are goods (love's machinery, the faculty of speech)
turned.
