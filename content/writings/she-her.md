## I. An unassigned pronoun is not an empty slot

An unassigned pronoun is not an empty slot. Nothing in a language stays empty — whatever the
rules leave unsettled, the default settles, and it does it quietly enough that nobody involved
experiences it as a settlement.

I found that out by needing a pronoun for the Holy Spirit and not having one. This publication
had settled the other two. God the Father, the transcendent Godhead, takes **They/Them** — I
made that case at length in [*They/Them — The Pronoun as
Iconoclasm*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/theythem), and it's the practice here now. Jesus of
Nazareth takes **He/Him**, because the whole scandal of the incarnation is that God showed up as
somebody in particular: a specific man, in a specific body, at a specific address. Two persons,
two pronouns, each of them doing work the other couldn't.

And then the third person of the Trinity, and nothing. Not a rule I'd weighed and set aside. A
hole where a rule should have been.

The hole wasn't empty. *He* was already sitting in it — in every English Bible on my shelf, in
the hymns, in the sentence I'd been about to write without noticing I was writing it. It had
been there so long, and so quietly, that it had never once had to present itself as a decision.
It doesn't look like a choice anyone made. It looks like the language.

But it was a choice, and it can be traced. Where *he* came from is documented, and the
documentation is not flattering.

## II. The masculine is the late arrival, and it came in by grammar

The *he* in your Bible is not revealed, is not apostolic, and was not decided by anybody. It's
the ending of a Latin noun.

Follow the word across its borders.

In Hebrew it is *ruach* — breath, wind, spirit — and it is **feminine**, in most of its uses
though not in every one.[^1] The second verse of Genesis puts Her over the face of the water in
a feminine participle: *merachefet*, hovering, brooding. The verb occurs three times in the
whole Hebrew Bible, and the only other one that means hovering is an eagle over her nest,
stirring above her young.[^2] Before God speaks, before there is light to see by, the posture
the text reaches for is a bird's — not standing over the deep and not commanding it. Brooding on
it, the way a thing is kept warm until it is ready.

In Aramaic and Syriac, *ruḥa*. Still feminine.[^3]

Rendered into Greek, the Spirit becomes *pneuma* — **neuter**.[^4] A thing. Of the three options
English would eventually have available, that's the one these essays rule out entirely, and
Greek got to it first.

Carried into Latin, *spiritus* — **masculine**.[^5] And the Latin West set the terms for the
next thousand years, and English, standing at the bottom of the whole inheritance, said *he*.

No council convened to degender the Spirit. No father argued Her out of Her femininity; no
heresy was named and condemned. The word crossed three linguistic borders, and at each crossing
the local grammar did what grammar does, until a feminine breath at the opening of Genesis had
become a masculine pronoun in a language that didn't exist yet. The change has a mechanism, and
the mechanism is translation. It has a direction, too, and the direction is away from where it
started.

## III. The Syriac writers were not obeying syntax

Where the grammar didn't force the change, you can watch what people actually did with it.

Early Syriac Christianity wrote in a Semitic language that kept *ruḥa* feminine, and it kept the
Spirit a *she* — not as inert concord but in warm, deliberate imagery. She is feminine across
what Brock calls the three main monuments of that literature: the *Acts of Thomas*, Aphrahat,
Ephrem.[^6] The second-century *Odes of Solomon* go further and make Her the nurse — the Father
is milked, and the Holy Spirit is the one who milks Him.[^7] A fragment of the *Gospel of the
Hebrews*, preserved by Origen, has Jesus speak of His mother, the Holy Spirit.[^8]

Syntax obliges nobody to imagine nursing. These writers reached past the noun-ending into images
no grammar asks for, because they had received Her that way and were saying so out loud. And
they did not invent the register. It was in the first sentence anybody wrote about Her — the
bird over the water — and they were reading forward from Genesis, not backward from their own
grammar.

And then Syriac itself goes over, in stages. She is feminine as a matter of course until about
the year 400. From the early fifth century some writers begin treating Her as masculine, and by
the sixth the masculine is the norm in theological writing — the old feminine surviving mostly
in the liturgy and in poetry, where a poet might take either gender depending on which fit the
meter.[^9] Even in the one language that had kept Her, She was eventually written out.

## IV. The concession, and it cuts both ways

Now the objection a careful reader has been holding, and it is a good one.

**Grammatical gender is not sex.** French tables are feminine and nobody believes tables are
women. That *ruach* is a feminine noun does not, by itself, make the Spirit female — and if I
lean my weight on a Hebrew noun-ending to install a *she*, I'm doing the sloppy thing I'd object
to instantly if it were done in the other direction.

I concede it, completely and without conditions. The Hebrew cannot establish that the Spirit is
a woman.

Then notice which way the blade falls. If grammatical gender can't establish *she*, it can't
establish *he* — and grammar is the only credential *he* has ever held. The feminine at least
has something standing behind it besides the ending: a body of writers reaching for mothering
and nursing, which syntax never asked them for — and, before any of them, the text's own first
move. **A noun-ending is grammar. A brooding bird is not.** No rule of Hebrew obliged Genesis to
reach for an eagle over a nest; that is an image, chosen, in the sentence that introduces Her.
The masculine has the ending and nothing else. No council, no verse, no image, no argument. Only
*spiritus*, and inheritance.

And the man who built the Latin said as much himself. Jerome — whose translation is why the West
says *spiritus*, and so why English says *he* — noticed the problem and wrote it down: the
Spirit is feminine among the Hebrews, masculine in Latin, neuter in Greek, *for in the deity
there is no gender.*[^jerome] He is not a witness for *She*, and would not have wanted to be. He
is a witness against *he* meaning anything — and he is the last man with an interest in saying
so.

So the concession doesn't hand the seat back to the default. It takes away the default's only
claim to have been sitting there. Once grammar is out of the room the pronoun is a **choice** —
it always was one — and the only question left is which choice does the least damage.

## V. The strongest objection is mine, and I published it

The objection is not hypothetical, and it is not hostile. It is in print, under my name.

In *[They/Them](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/theythem)* I got as far as this exact point —
the feminine at the headwaters, the trail of the word, the Syriac mothers — and then refused the
obvious next step, in writing:

> The obvious move now would be to restore the Spirit as *the* feminine person of the Trinity —
> to answer the masculine God with a divine She, at last. Don't. Two masculine persons and one
> feminine doesn't correct the imbalance; it encodes it, and it seats the feminine in the one
> person of the three with the least agency — the one who proceeds, who is sent, who is given.
> That isn't a rescue. It's the old arrangement handing out a consolation prize.

I meant it, and every clause of it is still true. Elizabeth Johnson saw the same trap and
stepped around it the same way — declining to gender one person, bringing Sophia to bear on all
three instead.[^10] A Trinity of two masculine persons and one feminine one doesn't distribute
anything. It ratifies the arrangement it appears to soften. The norm stays male, the feminine
becomes the exception to it, and the exception gets seated in the person who is sent rather than
the person who sends.

That is the case at its strongest, and it rests on one word.

## VI. There is no masculine default left

The refusal was conditioned, and the condition was **two masculine persons.**

This publication doesn't have two. The Father isn't *He* here. The Father is **They** — settled
first, on its own grounds, in an essay with no interest whatsoever in the Spirit's pronoun.

Johnson's trap needs a masculine default for the feminine to be an exception to. Take the
default away and there's nothing left for the trap to close on. **They**, **He**, **She**: three
persons, three distinct pronoun sets, and not one of them the norm the other two depart from.
The feminine stops being the odd seat at a masculine table, because there's no masculine table.
It's one of three, and each of the three is strange in its own direction.

That answers half of the refusal. The other half is still standing: even with the arithmetic
fixed, *she* still lands on the person of the three with the least agency — the one who
proceeds, who is sent, who is given. She is the sent one; that part is simply true. But look at
what has to be assumed to make it an objection. It takes *sent* for the low seat and *sending*
for the high one, and ranks the three by how much each initiates — which is the scale this
publication has been refusing from the start. [*The Novelist
DM*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-novelist-dm) argues that Christ is God not by
metaphysical exception but by the complete shedding of separateness, and the Son is the one who
says He can of His own self do nothing, that the doctrine is not His, that He came not to do His
own will. The one who is sent, who does not speak of Herself, who is given: on that account it
is not the lowest of the three positions. It is the shape all three turn out to have. The
objection measures the Trinity with a ruler borrowed from somewhere else, and I would rather put
the ruler down than win with it.

And the choice isn't free-floating. Run the elimination on the third person and see how few
doors there are.

*It* is forbidden, and not narrowly. Nothing here lets God be a *what*, in any person or any
mood.

*He* is the inherited accident, and grammar was the whole of its credential.

[*They/Them*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/theythem) is spoken for. It belongs to the Father, and it's carrying a particular load —
the refusal to let the infinite settle into a picture. Reuse it for the Spirit and the grammar
stops telling the persons apart at precisely the moment it's being asked to.

And there is one more door, and it is held open by this publication's own rule, which has never
held that God must take a pronoun at all. Repeating the noun is compliant, and frequently better — so the
standing option is to go on saying *the Spirit*, indefinitely, and never choose. **But refusing
to choose is not neutral.** Leave the slot open and the reader fills it, and what they fill it
with is the *he* I found already sitting there. Declining to pick a pronoun for the Spirit is
not how the default is avoided. It is how the default arrived.

**She/Her is what's left.** It is also the only one of the four pronouns with any warrant at all
in the languages the tradition spoke first.

The temptation is to read that as a victory. It isn't a promotion, and nothing has been restored
to anybody. It is what is left standing when the other doors close — and what is left turns out
to be where the whole thing started, in the second sentence of the book, before there was any
light to see it by.

## VII. A refusal, not a portrait

Two limits, and they travel with the rule wherever it goes.

**The first: this is a refusal, not a portrait.** The whole argument of
*[They/Them](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/theythem)* was that you don't answer a broken image
by hanging a better one in the empty frame. That rule binds hardest exactly where the temptation
is strongest. *She* is here to decline an inheritance, not to give the Spirit a face. The moment
a sentence of mine starts describing what She is like *as a woman*, I've used the pronoun to do
the one thing it was adopted to prevent, and you should hold me to it. A mother bird is the hard
case, and the distinction is worth being exact about. It is scripture's image and not mine, and
what it gives is a **posture** — hovering, brooding, waiting — not a face. An image of where
someone is and what they are doing is not an image of what they look like, and only the second
kind is an idol.

**The second: where I change a word, you will see me change it.** The King James gives John
16:13 as *when he, the Spirit of truth, is come.* Here it reads:

> when [She], the Spirit of truth, is come, [She] will guide you into all truth

The brackets do what brackets have always done — that word is mine and not the source's, and the
footnote gives you the King James as it stands.[^11] I don't quietly edit a text I am citing. I
also don't get to quietly leave one alone, because an unmarked pronoun inside a quotation reads
as the original: leave *he* standing there unannotated and the verse tells the reader the
pronoun came with the revelation. It didn't. It came with the Latin.

And none of this falls on a person who says *he*. Almost nobody who says it ever chose it. The
gavel, if one is falling here, comes down on a noun-ending, and on the long habit of mistaking
an accident for a revelation.

What's left is the oldest sentence anyone wrote about Her, and it isn't an argument at all.

Before the light. Before the word that made the light. The world is dark and formless and
covered over in water, and something is moving on the face of it — not ruling yet, not speaking
yet, not commanding anything into being. *Merachefet.* Hovering. The verb the Bible otherwise
saves for an eagle over her nest, stirring above her young, waiting for something in there to be
ready.

Stay with how little that posture claims. She is not enthroned above the water; She is down on
it, close enough to warm it. She is not making anything yet. She is over something that is not
ready, and what She is waiting for cannot be hurried into being ready — it can only be kept warm
while it becomes so. Whatever the tradition went on to say about the Spirit across the next two
thousand years, that is the first thing it said, and it said it before it had words for the
rest.

That's how the story opens. A mother bird, over the deep.

[^2]: Genesis 1:2; the verb *r-ḥ-f* (Strong's H7363) in the piel participle *merachefet*. It occurs

    Deuteronomy is the only real parallel.

[^3]: Syriac *ruḥa* is feminine; so is the Spirit in the Old Syriac gospels, late second or early

    the Syrian Baptismal Tradition* (Gorgias, 2013), 175–188.

[^4]: Greek *pneuma* (πνεῦμα) is neuter.

[^5]: Latin *spiritus* is masculine.

[^6]: Brock's own phrase — the Spirit is feminine in "the three main monuments of early Syriac

[^7]: *Odes of Solomon* 19:1–4: the Son is the cup, the Father is the one who was milked, and the

    text in which the masculine adjective with *ruḥa* does occur several times. It is the richest
    maternal witness and not a uniform one.

[^8]: The *Gospel of the Hebrews*, quoted by Origen, *Commentary on John* 2.12(.87), and several

[^9]: Brock's three stages: feminine as standard until c. AD 400; some writers treating *ruḥa* as

    century on, with the feminine surviving chiefly in liturgical texts and in poetry, where poets
    might use either gender according to the meter.

[^10]: Elizabeth A. Johnson, *She Who Is: The Mystery of God in Feminist Theological Discourse*
    (1992) — Sophia applied across all three persons (Spirit-Sophia, Jesus-Sophia, Mother-Sophia)

[^11]: The King James reads *Howbeit when he, the Spirit of truth, is come, he will guide you into
    all truth: for he shall not speak of himself…*; the bracketed **[She]** is mine, marked as an
    editorial substitution rather than made silently. **The translators were not being arbitrary,
    and the honest version of this note has to say so:** the Greek carries a masculine
    demonstrative, ἐκεῖνος, whose antecedent is **παράκλητος** — Comforter, Advocate, a masculine
    noun, from 16:7 — and not **πνεῦμα**, which is neuter. That is grammatical concord, not a claim
    about the Spirit — the same accident of grammar, surfacing one more time in the
    underlying text. My bracket is a choice made on purpose; theirs was agreement doing its ordinary work.
    Both are visible now. (The KJV lowercases deity pronouns; the house convention
    capitalizes them, which is itself disclosed.)

[^jerome]: Jerome, *Commentary on Isaiah* — *in divinitate enim nullus est sexus*, "for in the
    deity there is no gender." He says it while quoting the *Gospel of the Hebrews* line above,
    explaining that the Spirit is feminine among the Hebrews, masculine in Latin, and neuter in
    Greek. **Note precisely what he does and does not underwrite:** the passage rules out
    reading *any* grammatical gender theologically. That is a concession, not a
    warrant for the feminine.
