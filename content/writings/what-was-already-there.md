## I. The Lacquer

A flute is tuned by what was taken out of it. Not by anything added — by the bore, and by the
holes, which are absences, cut at measured distances before anybody ever put a mouth to it. Move
one of them a few millimeters and the note moves with it. Leave it where it is and the note is
where it is.

You can do a great deal to the outside of a flute. Sand it, oil it, lacquer it until it catches the
light and somebody in the row behind you leans over to ask what it's made of. None of that moves a
single pitch.

[*The Hollow Flute*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-hollow-flute-how-to-be-loved) takes that
instrument all the way down for a different question — where the breath comes from, and the fact
that it was never mine. What matters here is older than the breath. **The tuning was set before the
player arrived**, by cuts nobody consulted the flute about, and no work on the surface reaches it.

I worked on the surface.

In my doctoral program I noticed something about the researchers whose talks were worth sitting
through. It wasn't polish. Some of them were bad presenters — they lost the thread, they apologized
to the slides. What they had was enthusiasm about their own work, and it was not manufactured. You
could tell from the back of the room.

So I tried to do that.

Not the results; I wasn't pretending to have findings I didn't have. The *interest*. The lean
forward, the quickened voice, the sense that the next slide mattered to me. I mimed it, and I got
good at miming it. I thought that if I put the enthusiasm on the outside, something on the inside
would come up to meet it.

The research did not become more inspired.

I've done it at jobs since, more times than I'd like, convincing myself I cared about what I was
working on. I didn't. It never got easier the way a skill gets easier. It got smoother, the way a
finish gets smoother. That's the one thing you'd expect practice to fix and the one thing practice
made worse.

The causation went the other way.

I have run the thing described here backwards, on purpose, more than once. It doesn't work.

## II. The Ordinary Part

You're waiting for the part where this gets strange. It doesn't.

What you notice changes what you do. What you do changes where you end up. Someone who notices
something they genuinely love behaves differently toward it — calls, returns, makes room, gives it
the hour that was going somewhere else — and enough of those hours is a different life. No mystery,
and no mechanism anybody needs a theory for. It's the least exotic causal chain there is.

There's a version of this I refuse and a version I don't, and from the outside they look alike,
which is most of the trouble.

**What I refuse is navigation.** You know the one: pick the version of your life where it worked
out, and steer toward it. That doesn't hold, and I spent a whole essay on why. [*Where the Timelines
Agree*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/where-the-timelines-agree) argues that the label saying
which history you're in is an index and not a direction, so there's nothing in it to
steer.[^companion] It's also the old error with better equipment — as [*The Machine You
Expect*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-machine-you-expect) puts it, you do not get what you
want, you meet what you are, and the correction the tradition names is not visualizing harder.

**What I don't refuse is that a change of mind moves you.** The tradition has never been shy about
that and has a word for it: *metanoia*, usually flattened into *repent*, and meaning underneath the
churchiness a change of mind. It says the same of forgiveness — that it puts a person somewhere they
were not. The claim was never that nothing moves. The claim is that **you are not the one steering.
Grace guides where you land.**

So what I'm describing isn't a smaller thing than that. It's the same act at the domestic end, where
what's at stake is a commute and a job and a habit rather than a life. [*Nobody Crosses on
Evidence*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/nobody-crosses-on-evidence) makes the larger argument
properly.[^twowills] This one sits inside it.

So: interpretation is an act. Acts change what you do next. Where they take you isn't yours to plot.

Now look again at what failed on that stage, because the shape of that failure is the whole
argument. **You can act on what you notice. You cannot notice on command.** The direction is fixed,
and I learned it from the wrong end.

Which leaves a problem you may already be forming, and it's the right one.

[*Flow*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/flow) argued that neither how something feels nor how it
turns out is a readout of whether you're where you should be.[^flow] A terrible stretch doesn't
prove you strayed. A good one doesn't prove you were led. Bring that here and it bites at once,
because gratitude is a practice whose most reliable effect is feeling better, which puts it in
permanent danger of grading its own homework.

So the objection is yours, and it's aimed at me:

> You're telling me that what I notice changes my route, and your only evidence is that yours did.
> By your own rule, feeling better doesn't count. So how would you know if it hadn't?

I'd know because I have a case where it didn't, and what told me wasn't a feeling. It was the work.
The talks did not get better. The research did not get more inspired. Whatever was happening inside
me while I worked the finish, the papers were the same papers, and papers aren't diplomatic.
A colleague reads them or doesn't. The experiment says something or it doesn't.

**The evidence is outside you.** That's the only reason any of this is checkable, and the only
reason I trust the one case where it worked.

And **a practice that can't certify itself can't license leaving anything alone.**

## III. The Recognition

Jesus found me last summer, and almost immediately I had a problem I hadn't anticipated.

I love kirtan. Call-and-response chanting, a room of people, the same name over and over. A
*satsang*: the word means the company of the true. What I felt for God there was love, and I knew it — not because it was easy, but because it arrived without
the arguing.

A devout friend was persuading me it was idol worship.

My friend isn't a fool, and the thing she's pointing at is real. The commandment doesn't hedge. *No
man can serve two masters*, said by the same Person I had just started following, isn't a verse you
get to read around.[^masters] Every tradition that has thought carefully about worship has worried
about exactly this: that the heart is inventive, that it takes the shape of whatever it's given to
hold, and that a name sung over and over is not a neutral act. Somebody who tells you that isn't
policing you; they're taking the thing seriously, which is more than most people will do for you.

I didn't win that argument.

What settled it was smaller and much harder to argue with. I knew that the love I felt for God in
the satsang was real, and genuine, and could be trusted.

Not a conclusion I reasoned to. Not a feeling I worked up — you have seen what happens when I try to
work a feeling up. Something already in place, that had been in place, that I'd been walking past.
It cost no effort at all, which is exactly the opposite of everything I had put into the finish.

And then it did what the claim says it does. It changed what I did next.

I put Krishna Das back on. I chanted on my commute, out loud, through the city, and I'd been doing
neither.

And then it kept moving. The names gave way to love songs to God in English
— the language I actually think in — and that's what I hum on the commute now. It brings me back to
the love I have for God, and it aligns my thinking mind in a way the chanting didn't. I still go to
kirtan, weekly. What I go for is the satsang.

None of that is a verdict on Krishna, and [*Krishna Is Not
Christ*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/krishna-is-not-christ) already holds that ground. Its
finding isn't that the traditions arrive at the same place — they don't, the theologies genuinely
part, and the cross stands alone — but that the devotion is one. The instrument was already
tuned.[^kc]

What made the recognition trustworthy isn't a claim about Krishna at all. [*The Author Is Not a
Character*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-author-is-not-a-character) puts it as plainly as I
know how: **the Maker is not one more item inside the made world, not a character you could meet on
a road in the story.** And there cannot be two infinites — one infinite creator of all things, or
the word means nothing.[^oneinfinite] So the question in the satsang was never whether the love had
the right name on it. It was whether the love was aimed past every name at the only Author there is,
and a well-tuned instrument doesn't go out of tune when you learn more about who is listening.

Which is smaller than it sounds, and it's the one thing needed: **a love you actually feel
is information, and it can be trusted enough to act on.** My friend and I may still disagree about
the larger thing. We probably do.

There's an order to my own faith that I don't know what to do with. The music came first. A chapel,
a snowstorm, worship songs, in February — months before I opened my mind to whether any of it might
be true, and half a year before I followed anybody anywhere. The singing preceded the believing. As
doctrine I can't place it. As biography it is simply what happened, and it makes me slow to tell
anybody that a song in the wrong room can't be love.

## IV. Genuinely

The word carrying all of it is *genuinely*, and it hasn't been paid for.

Put the two cases beside each other. In one I noticed something I loved, and it changed a commute
and then a week. In the other I decided to love something, and nothing changed at all — not the
work, not the wanting, not even the difficulty of the pretending, which never eased.

The difference isn't effort. **I tried much harder at the lacquer.** The recognition in the satsang
cost me nothing; it was over in the time it takes to know something. The manufactured enthusiasm
cost me a great deal of discipline, and I was, in my way, good at it.

The difference is whether the thing was there before I looked.

The strongest case against that was made to a room of graduating seniors. David Foster Wallace told
them that the real work of an education is learning what to pay attention to: that the default
setting is to experience everything from inside your own skull, that the damage gets done in the
supermarket line and the traffic jam rather than in the dramatic hours, and that you can choose
otherwise. Nearly all of that is right, and I have been leaning on it. Attention is where
the work happens. The default really is the self. It really does cost you the dull years rather than
the memorable ones.

Then the sentence I have to part with: *the only thing that's capital-T True is that you get to
decide how you're gonna try to see it.*[^dfw]

That's the lever, stated better than I could state it. It says the content is yours to set — that
whether the thing in front of you is actually lovable can be bracketed, and the choosing carries the
weight.

I ran that experiment. I worked the finish until it shone and the pitch never moved. What that
speech calls a choice, I attempted as a discipline, and the attempt is the only evidence I have. It
points the other way.

The fish in his parable can't see the water because it's everywhere. What I'm describing is smaller and more
particular than that: not the medium you're swimming in, but one thing inside it that you actually
love.

So this isn't a technique, in the way you want a technique. **There's no lever.** You can't decide
to find something lovable and thereby find it lovable; the deciding is the part that does nothing.
What you can do is duller and smaller and available right now, which is to look at what's actually
in front of you and be honest about what you find.

If something's there, it will change what you do next.

If nothing's there, saying otherwise won't put it there. You have my word on that, at my own
expense.

This is also the honest reading of the verse everybody reaches for. *Whatsoever things are true,
whatsoever things are honest, whatsoever things are just, whatsoever things are pure, whatsoever
things are lovely… think on these things.*[^phil] It gets quoted as an instruction to be positive.
Read it exactly and it isn't. The loveliness sits in the premise — *whatsoever things **are** lovely*
— and the instruction is only about where to put your attention among things that already have that
property. It tells you to attend. It doesn't tell you to invent. It would be a strange verse if it
did.

## V. What This Doesn't Touch

None of this reaches grief. The door is standing open and what goes through it is cruel.

Nothing about what a person notices changes whether the loss happened. It isn't a matter of framing,
or of attention, or of what they failed to be grateful for. It sits in their actual life, at an
actual address, and no rearrangement of the inside of anybody's head reaches back and alters it.

*Have you tried being grateful*, said to someone in the middle of it, is a way of not being in the
room. It has a gentler face than the other things people say. It's the same failure: a sentence that
arrives to relieve the speaker.

There's a verse that gets used here, and the preposition is doing more work than the people quoting
it notice. *In every thing give thanks.*[^thess] **In** every thing — not **for** every thing. It
isn't a command to be grateful that the worst thing happened. Read the other way it becomes an
instruction to lie about your own life in the presence of God, which the tradition has never
actually asked for, and which has done real harm to people who were already having the worst year of
their lives.

So don't hand this to somebody who is still in it. Not the practice, not the verse, not the essay.
There's nothing to fix in that room and no cosmology that helps.

What to do there has been known for two thousand years and takes one line.[^weep]

## VI. The Toothbrush

I've never liked brushing my teeth. For years I was a digital nomad, traveling through Latin
America, and I didn't have a regular dental care practice — I hadn't seen a dentist in years. When I
got back to the States and went, the visit turned into remediation: deep cleaning, scaling, pockets
that had opened around the teeth.

The gums did not care what I noticed. Not what I loved, not how I framed it, not what I'd come to
appreciate about the ritual of dental care. There was a thing to be done and it wasn't being done,
and the tissue kept its own accounting the whole time and presented the bill at the end.

A practice that can't certify itself can't license leaving anything alone — and the version of this
essay where a change of attention quietly dissolves your problems is refuted by my own mouth.

**Problems should be addressed.** No clause after it.

The flattering version says: *if only he'd found something to love in it, the gums would have been
fine.* Maybe. I don't know and I don't get to say, **because I never ran it there.** I never tried
to notice anything about it. I simply didn't have the practice, for years, on a different continent.
That isn't evidence about where the technique stops. It's the absence of evidence, and dressing it
up as the first would be converting a failure into a testimonial.

A limiting case is only worth having if you refuse to make it flattering.

## VII. Set It Down

**There's no lever.** Nothing you pull in the morning to come out somewhere better by evening, no
procedure that converts a decision to love something into actually loving it. Every time I've
reached for one it has come apart in my hands.

Which is not the same as nothing to do.

What I do is sing. Love songs to God, in English, on the days I've lost the thread rather than the
days I feel like singing. It doesn't produce the love. On the days it does nothing, it does nothing,
and I've had those. What it does is quiet me down enough to hear what's already playing. Take it or
leave it. It isn't a lever, and the reason it isn't one is that you can't do it without listening.

The tuning was never mine to set. The listening is the only part that was ever on my side of it.

And the reason there's no lever isn't that nothing moves. Things move. It's that the hand on the
tiller was never going to be mine, which is the one piece of good news in an essay that has spent
its whole length taking things away.

What's left is smaller than what I've taken.

You can look at what's in front of you. If something there is genuinely loved — not decided on, not
worked up, *found* — it will change what you do next, and the change will show up outside your
feelings, which is the only kind of showing up that counts. And if nothing's there, no amount of
saying otherwise will put it there.

That's less than I wanted when I started. It doesn't fix the thing you haven't fixed. It doesn't
reach the room where the loss is.

A man on a train, humming, on the way to somewhere ordinary. Nothing on the outside worth looking
at. No finish, and nobody in the row behind leaning over to ask what it's made of.

You could tell from the back of the room, once. Not this. This one gets checked later, and
elsewhere, and never by asking me how it felt.

I don't know yet what it will have changed.

is the other half: that a change of mind moves you anyway, and that you are not the one
choosing where it lands.

refusing to let anyone steer between histories does not refuse the turn. **I am pointing at that
argument, not making it**, and the evidence here reaches the domestic case and no further.

[^flow]: The claim I am inheriting and not re-arguing: neither how a state feels nor how it

[^masters]: Matthew 6:24, KJV — *No man can serve two masters: for either he will hate the one,
and love the other; or else he will hold to the one, and despise the other. Ye cannot serve God
and mammon.* The body quotes the opening clause only; the rest of the verse is the argument for it
and does not change its sense.

[^kc]: [*Krishna Is Not Christ*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/krishna-is-not-christ) — its
subtitle is *But the devotion is one*, and its closing section is titled *The Instrument Was
Already Tuned*. **Note what it does not say, because nothing here should be read as saying it
either:** not that the traditions arrive at the same place, and not that Krishna is Christ under
another name. The theologies part and the cross stands alone. What carries over here is the
narrower claim about the devotion.

[^oneinfinite]: The argument that there cannot be two infinites — and so that people addressing
the one Maker under different names are not addressing different Makers — is made at length in
[*Jealous of a Calf*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/jealous-of-a-calf), and endorsed rather than
re-argued in *The Author Is Not a Character*. **I am relying on it here, not proving it.** What it
licenses is narrow: that the satsang could be pointed at the one Author. It says nothing about where
the traditions arrive, which is a different question and not the one asked here.

[^dfw]: David Foster Wallace, the 2005 commencement address at Kenyon College, published as *This
Is Water* (Little, Brown, 2009). **What I am taking and what I am refusing are both worth
naming.** The diagnosis is his and I am relying on it — attention is the site, the default setting
is the self, and the cost is paid in ordinary hours. The prescription is where we part: it
brackets whether the thing attended to is actually there, and that bracket is the whole of my
disagreement.

[^phil]: Philippians 4:8, KJV. Two disclosures, since the body quotes it with an ellipsis and a
capital the King James does not have. **What the ellipsis drops:** *whatsoever things are of good
report; if there be any virtue, and if there be any praise.* It is elided for length and **it runs
the same way as the rest** — *if there be any* is as conditional as *whatsoever things are*, so
the omitted clauses corroborate the reading rather than qualify it. **And the quotation begins
mid-sentence**, after *Finally, brethren*, so its opening *W* is mine; the King James lowercases
it. The reading itself is the plain one and does not depend on the Greek: the loveliness sits in
the relative clauses as a premise, and the imperative governs only the attending.

[^thess]: 1 Thessalonians 5:18, KJV — *In every thing give thanks: for this is the will of God in
Christ Jesus concerning you.* **The Greek carries the distinction the body rests on:** ἐν παντὶ
εὐχαριστεῖτε — ἐν with the dative παντί, a preposition of circumstance, so *in* everything and not
*for* it. **And the counterweight, which a reader who knows the letters will raise, so it is
better raised here:** Paul uses the other construction elsewhere: Ephesians 5:20 has ὑπὲρ πάντων —
*giving thanks always **for all things*** — and *hyper* with the genitive does mean *for*. So the
two prepositions are both his. **What survives is the narrower claim, and it is the only one the
body makes:** this verse is not the one that commands gratitude *for* the worst thing that
happened to you. Whether Ephesians asks something else, and what it would mean if it did, is a
real question and not one I am settling here.

[^weep]: Romans 12:15. The instruction and its argument belong to [*Where the Timelines
Agree*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/where-the-timelines-agree), which treats the same subject;
I am pointing rather than restating it.
