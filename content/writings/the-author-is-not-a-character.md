## I. The Tradition That Guards Our Own First Premise

Start with the thing almost nobody says out loud, because it sounds, at first, like a concession
a Christian is not supposed to make.

Of all the world's traditions, the one that holds the first premise of the Christian mystics
most fiercely — and turns it most pointedly against the Christian claim itself — is Islam.

I do not mean that the two religions agree. They do not, and I am going to spend a good part of what
follows on the exact place they come apart, and I am not going to soften it. I mean something
narrower and stranger. There is a sentence that the deepest Christian account of God rests on —
that the Maker is not one more item inside the made world, not a very large object standing
beside the other objects, not a character you could meet on a road in the story. And there is no
tradition on earth that guards that sentence more jealously than the one Christians most often
treat as the outside objection.

The Muslim confession of it is *tawhid* — the oneness of God, held with an intensity that will
give up anything before it gives up the One. *Say: He is Allah, the One!… He begetteth not nor
was begotten. And there is none comparable unto Him.*[^ikhlas] And, in the phrase that has
anchored a thousand years of Muslim negative theology: *Naught is as His likeness.*[^shura]
Nothing beside Them, nothing like Them, nothing that could so much as stand as a second.

I have argued elsewhere that this is not a foreign idea a Christian must hold at arm's length but
the plainest reading of Christianity's own two oldest words for God. In
[*The Novelist DM*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-novelist-dm) I took *omnipotent* and
*omnipresent* at full weight and watched them forbid a world made the way we make things: if
God's power is total there is no second engine running anywhere on its own, and if God is
everywhere there is no empty shelf to set a thing that is not God — *no beside, no outside, no
second, no rival, no independent anything.* The image that survived the argument was a story and
its teller: a world made wholly of one mind, alive with characters who think the author's
thoughts and look out through an awareness that was never their own. And the hinge of the whole
essay was one sentence about that arrangement. **The author is not a character in the book.** You
never pass the novelist on a street in the novel. The characters do not hold the pen.

That sentence is *tawhid*. It is the Muslim's whole hand, closed hard around the one thing they
will not put down.

So before anything divides us, notice what has already happened. The tradition a great many
Christians file under *rival* has its grip on the exact premise our own mystics reasoned their
way to — the incomparable One, the God with no likeness, the Author who is not among the written.
Which means the argument between the two faiths is not, as it is usually staged, a fight about
whether God is one. Both say yes, and both mean it more literally than most of their own members
dare. The argument is somewhere else, and it is sharper for being somewhere else.

Two guardrails first, and the argument is worthless without them.

The first: I am not going to relitigate whether the Christian and the Muslim are pointed at the
same God. I think they are — there cannot be two infinites, and *Allah* is simply the Arabic word
for God, the one an Arabic Bible uses in its first verse — but that is a separate argument, made
at length in [*Jealous of a Calf*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/jealous-of-a-calf), and I am
going to lean on its conclusion rather than rebuild it here.[^calf] Take it as granted or take it as owed; either way,
the argument begins after it.

The second, and more important: this is not the essay that says it is all one underneath. It is
close enough to that essay to be mistaken for it, so the distance goes on the page early. I
have written, in [*Krishna Is Not Christ*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/krishna-is-not-christ),
against exactly the lazy syncretism that stirs the traditions into one gray paste and calls the
result tolerance; that essay ends by insisting the great devotions do not arrive at the same
place. What I am doing here is the opposite of stirring. I am mapping a seam — running my thumb
down the single join where Islam and the gospel meet and part — and a seam is precisely what you
cannot see if you have melted the two things together. Mapping the join sharpens the difference.
It does not dissolve it. Hold me to that: if by the end I have blurred the cross into a comparative
footnote, I have failed.

Islam guards our first premise. It guards it with three great denials, and the
denials are usually read as three separate offenses against Christian doctrine — no Son, no
Trinity, no cross. But they are not three. They are one grip, closing on one
truth, three times. And once you see them as one grip, two of the three turn out to be guarding a
door the gospel also guards, while the third is the only one where the disagreement is about a
fact and not an interpretation — the only place the road truly forks. That is worth far more than
a reconciliation would have been.

## II. *Shirk*, and the Character Who Grabs the Pen

Here is the first denial, and it is the one that looks most like an attack on the center of the
faith: *He begetteth not nor was begotten.*[^ikhlas2] Islam denies that God has a Son.

The gravest sin in Islam is *shirk* — association, the setting of any partner alongside God, the
placing of a second anywhere in the vicinity of the One.[^shirk] And to the Muslim ear, *Son of
God* sounds like *shirk* said out loud: a second divine being, begotten, standing next to the
Father, a partner in the godhead. The denial is not casual and it is not ignorant. It is the same
reflex that made Sinai thunder against the calf — a horror of the second, of the rival, of the
creature hoisted up into the place that belongs to God alone.

Now watch what that horror is, exactly, because I have described it before under another name.

[*The Novelist DM*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-novelist-dm) — the essay that gave me the Author-and-character picture — spends its whole
middle section on a single error, and names it the oldest error there is: the character who forgets they are written and takes themselves for the one doing the writing. The figure in the story who stands up, feels the whole world running through them — which is true, they *are* made of the author, every thought on loan — and draws the wrong conclusion: *then I am the author.* The Hindu scriptures have a
sentence for the truth underneath this, *aham brahmasmi*, "I am Brahman," and I argued that
everything turns on who says it. Said by the ego swelling up to claim its godhood, it is the
oldest lie in the world — the character snatching the pen. The whole exhausting comedy of the
self is a character trying to be its own author, trying to write and secure and justify a life
that was never its to hold.

That grasping — the creature reaching for the Creator's chair — is what *shirk* names. The Muslim
word for the gravest sin and the Christian mystic's word for the fundamental illusion are
pointing at one act: something that is not God being set in the place of God. When Islam recoils
from *Son of God*, it is recoiling from the thing my own tradition, at its most rigorous, recoils
from too. It hears the doctrine as a demigod installed beside the Father, a second engine, a
partner — and against *that*, its no is not an error. Its no is correct. A begotten second god,
standing on its own, sharing the throne, would be exactly the idol both traditions were built to
refuse.

And press the specific word Islam chooses, because it is more exact than a Christian usually
notices. *He begetteth not.* Begetting is manufacture — the making of another of your kind, a
separate thing that then stands there in its own right. And the God of
[*The Novelist DM*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-novelist-dm) cannot beget in that sense any more than They can build a world in that sense,
because there is no outside for the begotten thing to stand in and no spare power for it to run
on. The incarnation I argued for was not a begotten second at all. It was the infinite cast into
one human life *with nothing added* — the opposite of a rival, the opposite of a partner, a
single life so emptied of any self of its own that the whole of God could stand in it undistorted.
If *begotten* means a manufactured second deity, then *He begetteth not* is a sentence the
Christian apophatic tradition can sign without a tremor. We do not believe in that Son either.

And a Christian can hold a harder version of this than the Qur'an asks for. In a companion essay,
[*Son of Joseph*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/son-of-joseph), I deny even the *biological*
begetting — the virgin birth — because it makes
Christ an exception in His genes rather than in His emptying, and because Matthew's own genealogy,
forty-odd *begats* deep, flinches at the one verse the doctrine needs it to break. So when
*begotten* is denied of God, I deny it further down than a Muslim usually expects: no manufactured
second deity, and no manufactured origin for the man either. The one exception left standing is
neither a second god nor a miracle in the biology — it is of a wholly different kind, and §VI is
where it gets its name.

So the first denial is not, at bottom, a disagreement about whether a creature may be God. Both
traditions say no, and say it with the same fear. The disagreement is about one man, and about one
sentence he said, and about whether there is any way for that sentence to be true that is not
*shirk*. Which is the whole of the next movement.

## III. The Words Are Identical

Here is the claim everything balances on, stated before it is defended: the
gospel and the Qur'an are not, on the sonship, holding two incompatible views of one sentence.
They hold the same view of the sentence — the same test for when it damns and when it saves — and
divide only on the verdict it returns for the one man who said it.

Go back to *aham brahmasmi*, "I am Brahman," and to the knife's edge I said everything balanced
on. Said by the ego standing up to claim its godhood, it is the oldest lie. Said by the ego seen
all the way through — the self so completely surrendered that the only One left to say the word is
the God who was speaking through it the whole time — it is the truth, and it has been said that way once. For the rest of us the true form of the sentence is smaller and is not *I am*; it is *I am Theirs*. **The words are identical. Everything turns on who is left to say them.**

Now put the sentences that got Jesus killed against that edge. *I and My Father are one. Before
Abraham was, I am. He that hath seen Me hath seen the Father.*[^johnclaims] From a man reaching
for godhood, these are the proudest words ever spoken — they are *shirk* in its purest form, the
character seizing the pen and announcing that he wrote the book. And the Muslim who hears them
that way, and refuses them, is refusing the right thing. If that is who is saying them, they must
be refused.

But the gospel's entire claim is that it is not who is saying them. The same mouth that said *I
and My Father are one* said, and said relentlessly, the opposite of a man reaching for anything:
*The Son can do nothing of Himself. I seek not Mine own will, but the will of the Father. My
doctrine is not Mine, but [Theirs] that sent Me.*[^kenosis] Every sentence hands the power, the
teaching, the very act, upward and away. This is not a man climbing toward the throne. It is a man
emptying himself of everything, himself included, until — and this is the Christian claim in one
line — there is no self of his own left to occupy the words, and the only *I* still standing in
them is the Father's. The most audacious sentence in history, said not by the fullest man but by
the most emptied one. The pen handed *back* so completely that the Author could take up the whole
of the story in that one hand.

Islam cannot hear that second reading. And this next part is the opposite of a rebuke: Islam is *right not to trust it.* From the outside, the two readings are
indistinguishable. The words are identical. There is no test you can run on a man's sentence to
tell whether the *I* in it is the ego swollen to bursting or the ego gone entirely — and the
history of religion is a long parade of the first kind swearing they were the second. Every
charlatan who ever claimed divinity said, in effect, *I and my Father are one.* A tradition that
has watched the character grab the pen in God's name, over and over, and call the theft revelation,
has every reason to forbid the sentence outright — to say *no one gets to say this, because the
ones who say it are lying, and we cannot tell the liars from the one who might not be.*

So set the two traditions side by side and see what they actually are. **Islam is the faith that
will not risk the sentence.** It has counted the cost of letting a creature say *I am one with
God*, and it has decided — not foolishly — that the safe and reverent thing, the thing that
guards the incomparable One, is to shut the sentence entirely, to let no one through that door,
because the ones who come to it are almost always thieves. **Christianity is the faith that stakes
everything on exactly one man having said it truly** — one crossing, in all of history, where the
words were not the lie; where the character who said *I am the author* was not grabbing the pen but
had been handed it, because he had first handed back everything of his own.

They are guarding the same seam. One guards it by barring the door. The other guards it by
insisting a single door, once, was real — and is the way through for everyone else. This is not a
small difference and I am not pretending it is. But it is a different kind of difference than the
one we usually name. It is not that Islam sees a second God where Christianity sees one. It is that
both see the danger of the second God with perfect clarity, and disagree about whether the man
from Nazareth was that danger or its final undoing.

## IV. Say Not "Three"

The second denial is quicker, and I will keep it quick, because the honest thing to say about it is
short and I do not want to pad it into a false weight.

*Say not "Three"… Allah is only One Allah. Far is it removed from His Transcendent Majesty that He
should have a son.*[^three] The Qur'an warns the People of the Book against saying *Three* — and
if what *Three* means is three gods, three divine beings side by side, a committee in heaven, then
once again the denial is guarding a door Christianity guards from its own side. The Church has
never confessed three gods. It has spent its sharpest centuries and its hardest councils insisting
that the doctrine of the Trinity is *not* tritheism, that the three are not three whats, that to
count God to three the way you count coins to three is precisely the heresy. A Muslim who refuses
*three gods* and a Christian who refuses *three gods* are refusing the same thing.

I could reach here for the doubled figure of [*The Novelist DM*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-novelist-dm) — one mind wearing two roles, the
teller who is also the one improvising the living world — and try to make it carry a picture of how
One can hold an inner distinction without becoming three. I am not going to, and the restraint is
the point. That image was built to do a different job, and pressed into service as a model of the
Trinity it would prove too much or too little and I would be performing a solution instead of
having one. The honest report is smaller: on the bare negation — *not three gods* — Islam and the
Church stand together, and the real disagreement is not over the number but over whether any
distinction internal to the One is already the *shirk* Islam fears. That is a genuine
disagreement, and I would rather leave it standing clearly than plaster it with an analogy that
makes it look settled.

Two of the three denials, then, guard a door we guard too; the disagreements they leave are real,
but interpretive — downstream of a negation both traditions share. The third shares no such
negation. It is a flat contradiction about something that either happened or did not, and it is
where the road forks for real. I am not going to pretend the frame closes it.

## V. It Appeared So Unto Them

The third denial is the cross. *They slew him not nor crucified him, but it appeared so unto
them.*[^nisa] The Qur'an denies that Jesus was crucified.

And here the picture I have been building does not reach across the gap. It stands on the far side
of it, and that is said first, because the temptation of an essay like this one is to make all
three denials fold up neatly into the frame, and this one will not fold, and it
should not be made to.

The essay this whole reading grows out of is, at its core, the most anti-illusion argument I know
how to make. When it turned to face the worst thing — the cancer ward, the child in the cellar — it
refused, with everything it had, the whisper that says *it is only a story, it is made of mind, so
nothing is really happening.* *Made of mind is not the same as make-believe.* The nightmare is
total to the one inside it; a thing made of consciousness has never once meant it does not cut. I
said in that essay that if any part of the reader wanted to file real suffering under *illusion*,
the whole thing deserved to be thrown across the room, and I would throw it first.

The Arabic at the heart of this verse is *shubbiha lahum* — "it was made to appear so to them," it
was made to seem.[^shubbiha] And that is the exact instinct my own essay exists to refuse: the
flight into *it only appeared.* On the plainest level, the level of what happened outside Jerusalem
on a Friday, I hold that the crucifixion was real, that it is the center of everything, and that
here the gospel and the Qur'an cannot both be right. Somebody is wrong about a Friday. I have said
so before and I am not going to un-say it to make this movement more comfortable than the truth.

But the frame is not useless here. It cannot close the gap, but it can tell you *why the gap is
tempting* — why a reverent, God-fearing tradition would deny the cross — and that turns out to be
worth having.

Ask what the denial is *protecting*. Not, at root, a point of history. It is protecting God's
faithfulness to God's own. The unbearable thought the verse rises up against is the thought that
God would hand a beloved prophet over to the most shameful death the world could invent, and stand
by, and do nothing — that the righteous one could be publicly, totally defeated, and heaven be
silent. Against *that*, the Qur'an says: no. It did not happen. He was not abandoned to it. *Allah
took him up unto Himself.*[^raised] The denial is a refusal of the nightmare in which God's
faithful one is overcome and God does not save Him.

And now hear how close that is to the gospel's own refusal of the very same nightmare — reached by
the opposite road. The providence essay I wrote,
[*The Highest Peak*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-highest-peak), put its hardest sentence in
the garden: no one has ever been more precisely on the path than the man in Gethsemane, and no one
has suffered more for being on it. And [*The Novelist DM*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-novelist-dm), the immanence essay, put it more starkly still — the reason
God showed us, of all things, a man tortured to death by the state, is that God did not write the
cellar from a safe desk; They wrote Themselves into the story and bled in it. *The cross is not
God's excuse for suffering. It is God's whereabouts.* Both essays are refusing exactly what the
Qur'an refuses: the picture of a God who leaves the faithful one to be destroyed and looks away.

So the two traditions are answering the same horror. Watch precisely where they split, because it
is not where you would expect. *It appeared so unto them* — read through the frame — names
something true. The crucifixion *as sheer defeat*, the crucifixion as the end of the story, the
crucifixion as God's abandonment of His own: that reading **is** an appearance. It is the view from
the ground, the local reading, what the thing looked like to the eyes at the foot of the cross and
to the disciples scattering in the dark. And that appearance is false. On this, astonishingly, the
Qur'an and the gospel agree: the eyes at the foot of the cross did not see what was really
happening.

The split is over what to do with the appearance. **The Qur'an corrects the false appearance by
unmaking the event** — if the defeat cannot be real, then the death cannot be real; He was not
crucified, it only seemed so, God lifted Him clear. **The gospel corrects the false appearance by
keeping the event and turning it inside out** — the death was utterly real, and the defeat the eyes
saw was the exact shape of the rescue; the losing *was* the winning; the descent to the lowest
place was the road home, and there was no other. One tradition saves the prophet by lifting him
above the cross. The other saves the world by leaving him on it and calling that, and not its
avoidance, the victory.

I owe the reader one honesty here, and it matters. When I say the verse "names a true appearance,"
I am reading it through my frame, not reporting how Muslims read it. The mainstream Islamic
understanding of this verse is not "the defeat was only apparent"; it is substitution — that
another was made to bear Jesus's likeness and died in his place, and that Jesus himself was taken
up alive.[^substitution] A Muslim is entitled to be wary of a Christian arriving with a clever
frame and telling him what his own scripture was really getting at. I am not telling him that. I am
saying what the denial looks like *from inside the gospel's account of the cross* — that it is a
refusal of a real nightmare, aimed at a defeat that the gospel also calls unreal, differing over
whether you undo the horror or walk through it. That is a reading from one side of the seam, offered
as one, and it leaves the disagreement standing at full height. It does not resolve the
Friday. Nothing here resolves the Friday.

## VI. East and West Shoulders

Now stand back from the three denials and look at the whole shape, because there is a geometry
under them, and I have used it before.

In [*The Highest Peak*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-highest-peak) I put the reader on a mountain range in the dark, everyone climbing toward
the one highest peak, and I said the thing the whole picture turned on: two people on opposite
shoulders of the same mountain must walk in opposite directions to reach the same top. A man
heading east and a man heading west are not necessarily disagreeing. They may be doing exactly the
same thing. A great deal of what we take for opposition is the geometry of standing somewhere else
on the same slope.

Watch the two traditions as two walkers on one mountain. Islam, holding the incomparable One with
everything it has, walks *away* from every image, every form, every face, every embodiment —
because each of those is a place where a creature might get mistaken for the Creator, and the whole
labor of the faith is to keep that from happening. It is a via negativa with a drawn sword: *naught
is as His likeness*, so turn from every likeness, and above all from the likeness that would put
God in a body and call it a Son. Christianity, holding the same One, walks *toward* the one
particular face — toward a Galilean with a mother and a death, and says *there*, in that one life,
the whole of God stood undistorted. One walker climbs by stripping every form away. The other
climbs by pouring the whole of the formless into a single form. From the outside these look like
contradictory religions. On the mountain, they may be opposite shoulders of the one truth about
God, which is that They are both utterly beyond us and, if They chose, able to be utterly with us.

And here is where the doubled figure of [*The Novelist DM*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-novelist-dm) finally earns its keep, because it holds
both shoulders at once. The Author is not a character in the book — that is Islam's whole grip, and
it is true, and the essay built its foundation on it. But the essay's scandal, the thing that makes
it a Christian essay and not a Vedantic one, is the next sentence: this Author, and only this
Author, wrote Themselves *into* the book — took a character's flesh, walked a road in the story,
bled in the cellar They had made. That is not a violation of the rule that the author is not a
character. It is the one exception that only the Author could make. A character cannot climb out of
the book and seize the pen; that way is barred, and *shirk* is the name of trying. But the Author
can always, if the Author chooses, walk *into* the book — appear on a road in the story, as one of
the written, without for one moment ceasing to be the one writing it. No character can cross that
line from below. The Author can cross it from above, and the crossing is a gift, not a theft.

That is the seam. Islam guards the rule with its life: no character is the Author, and woe to the
one who claims it. Christianity confesses the single exception the rule itself allows: the Author,
once, wrote Himself in. You can hold the rule and deny the exception — that is Islam, and it is not
a stupid position; it is a vigilant one. You can hold the rule and confess the exception — that is
the gospel. What you cannot do, and what neither tradition does, is drop the rule. Both are
guarding the same closed door against every creature that would force it. They disagree about
whether the One on the far side ever, even once, opened it from inside and stepped through.

And one thing more, because without it the exception curdles straight back into a fence — the very
fence the gospel spent itself tearing down. Jesus is the one exception *so far.* Not the only one
there could ever be; the first. The Author who came through the door did not pull it shut behind
Him — He came through to hold it open, and what He said from the far side was never *admire what
cannot be done again.* It was *follow me*, and *greater works than these shall he do.*[^greater] The
crossing stays the Author's, always from above, always a gift: the invitation is never to climb out
and seize the pen, which is barred, and has a name, and the name is still *shirk.* It is to empty a
self so completely, as He did, that the same Author can write the whole of Themselves into it too.
He is not the exception that keeps the rest of us out. He is the first one through, and He is holding
the door.

## VII. The Sentence Islam Is Named After

I said at the start that the argument begins after the question of whether the two traditions are
pointed at the same God, and lands somewhere else. Here is where it lands, and I have been walking
toward it the whole time.

[*The Highest Peak*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-highest-peak) made a claim that unsettles people, and I stand by it: you do not have to be a
Christian to follow Jesus. The criterion He gave for the last sorting was not a password but a
direction — the sheep who fed Him without knowing it was Him, the son who said *I will not* and then
went and did the work, the outsider casting out devils in a name he did not belong to, and *forbid
him not.* The toll booth — the idea that saying the name is the fee for the road — is not in the
gospel. What puts a person on the road is the direction of their feet.

So turn, at the end, to the feet.

The word *islam* means submission. *Muslim* means one who submits — one who hands himself over to
the will of God.[^islam] The whole architecture of the faith is a bending of the human will to the
divine one, five times a day, at the sound of a call. And the single thing [*The Novelist DM*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-novelist-dm) said
you could actually *do* — the one motion under all the argument, the door that does not lead up but
down, into a single sentence — was *not my will, but Thine.* It is the sentence Jesus prayed in the
garden with the whole terror of the story bearing down on Him. It is the four words at the dead
center of the prayer He taught. It is the entire operation of the surrendered self, the character
handing the pen back to the One whose story it always was.

Islam is named after that sentence. Not after a doctrine about the Son, which it denies. Not after
the cross, which it denies. After the *motion* — the surrender of the will to God — which is the
one thing both of my essays said was the whole of it, the thing you do tonight, the door. A
civilization organized around the bend of the will toward God is organized around the gesture the
gospel puts at its own center and calls the way home.

This wants more care than the symmetry does, because it is the place where an essay like this
tips over into saying more than it has earned. I am not saying the
two faiths are the same faith with different furniture; the last four movements were spent showing
they are not, and the cross stands between them unmoved. I am not issuing anyone a verdict — not
the Muslim at prayer, not the Christian in the pew — because the whole of [*The Highest Peak*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-highest-peak) was a
refusal of the local view's claim to read anyone's standing off the outside of their life, and I am
not going to violate that here for a tidy close. And I am not smuggling back the *all roads*
flattening I disowned in the first movement; opposite shoulders of one mountain are still walking in
opposite directions, and the direction is not nothing.

What I am saying is only this, and it is enough. The tradition Christians most often cast as the
great denier turns out to deny, in its three great denials, mostly the things the gospel also
denies — the second god, the three gods, the abandonment of the faithful one — and to be built, at
its foundation and in its very name, on the one motion the gospel calls the door. It guards our
first premise more fiercely than we do. It bars a sentence we stake everything on one man having
said truly. It walks the far shoulder of our own mountain. And it is named for the surrender we say
is the whole of the way.

The author is not a character in the book. Islam will die on that sentence, and it is right to.

The gospel says the Author wrote Himself in anyway — once, on a Friday, all the way down to the
place the eyes at the foot of the cross mistook for the end.

Between those two there is a real seam, and I have tried to run my thumb the whole length of it
without once pretending it closed. It does not close. But look at what it joins.

[^ikhlas]: Qur'an 112 (*al-Ikhlāṣ*), Pickthall: *"Say: He is Allah, the One! Allah, the eternally
Besought of all! He begetteth not nor was begotten. And there is none comparable unto Him."*
Checked verse by verse against corpus.quran.com's labeled Pickthall text; not collated against a
print edition.

[^shura]: Qur'an 42:11 (*ash-Shūrā*), Pickthall: *"Naught is as His likeness; and He is the Hearer,
the Seer."* The Arabic *laysa ka-mithlihi shay'* is the anchor verse of Muslim negative theology.
Wording checked against corpus.quran.com's Pickthall; not against print.

[^calf]: The same-God argument — that there cannot be two infinites (Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae*
I.11.3), that *Allah* is the Arabic word for God cognate with the Hebrew *Eloah* and the Aramaic
*Alāhā* Jesus would have used, and that the Qur'an itself tells believers *our Allah and your Allah
is One* (29:46, Pickthall's own rendering of the word as *Allah*) — is made in the companion essay named in the body.
That conclusion is granted here and not rebuilt.

[^ikhlas2]: Qur'an 112:3, Pickthall: *"He begetteth not nor was begotten."* Checked against
corpus.quran.com's Pickthall; not against print.

[^shirk]: *Shirk* — "association," the setting of any partner alongside God — is in Islam the
gravest sin, the one the Qur'an singles out as unforgiven where unrepented: *"Lo! Allah forgiveth
not that a partner should be ascribed unto Him"* (4:48, Pickthall, checked against corpus.quran.com).
Its opposite, *tawhid*, is the doctrine of the divine unity.

[^johnclaims]: John 10:30; John 8:58 (echoing the divine name of Exodus 3:14, *I AM*); John 14:9
(KJV, quoted exactly here — the King James lowercases the Son's pronouns, and this note keeps them
that way so the verses stay checkable). The body takes the house capital (He/Him), per the house rule that casing is a typographic convention and not a change to the source's words. One
deliberate exception, further down: where §V puts *I and my Father are one* in the mouth of a
charlatan claiming divinity, the pronoun stays lowercase — there the speaker is not the Son, and the
capital would concede the very thing the sentence is being used to deny.

[^kenosis]: John 5:19; John 5:30; John 7:16 (KJV) — the "I do nothing of myself" sayings. The
emptying they describe is *kenosis*, from Philippians 2:7, *ekénōsen heautón*, "he emptied himself";
argued at length in [*The Novelist DM*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-novelist-dm), §VI.

[^three]: Qur'an 4:171 (*an-Nisā'*), Pickthall: *"…and say not "Three" — Cease! (it is) better for
you! — Allah is only One Allah. Far is it removed from His Transcendent Majesty that He should have
a son."* Checked against corpus.quran.com's Pickthall (not against print): Pickthall renders the
Arabic *Allāh* as "Allah," not "God" — as he does at 29:46 — and capitalizes "Transcendent
Majesty."

[^nisa]: Qur'an 4:157 (*an-Nisā'*), Pickthall: *"…they slew him not nor crucified him, but it
appeared so unto them."* Checked against corpus.quran.com's Pickthall; not against print.

[^shubbiha]: The Arabic *shubbiha lahum* is standardly rendered "it was made to appear so to them"
or "it was made dubious to them" — a passive verb of the root *sh-b-h*, likeness or resemblance, the
same root behind *tashbīh*, the theological error of likening God to creatures (corpus.quran.com
word-analysis; the sense is standard across the classical *tafsīr*).

[^raised]: Qur'an 4:158, Pickthall: *"But Allah took him up unto Himself. Allah was ever Mighty,
Wise."* Checked against corpus.quran.com's Pickthall; not against print.

[^substitution]: The dominant classical reading of 4:157–158 is substitutionist — that God cast
Jesus's likeness upon another, who was crucified in his place, while Jesus was raised bodily and
alive; the *tafsīr* tradition preserves several versions of who the substitute was (Ibn Kathīr and
al-Ṭabarī on 4:157). A minority of modern interpreters read the passage otherwise. The point in the
body stands on the frame, not on the mainstream reading, and says so.

[^greater]: John 14:12 (KJV): *"the works that I do shall he do also; and greater works than these
shall he do."* That the invitation was always to *become* what He was — not to admire it from behind
a fence — is the burden of [*The Novelist DM*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-novelist-dm),
§VII, on the deification (*theosis*) tradition of the Christian East.

[^islam]: *Islām* and *muslim* derive from the Arabic root *s-l-m*; the form-IV verb *aslama* means
"to submit, surrender, or hand oneself over" — here, to God. The same root gives *salām*, peace. The
morphology is standard (the root *s-l-m*; *islām* as the Form IV verbal noun).
