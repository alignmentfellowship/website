## I. I Believe in You

You have said this, and meant it.

The night before the hard thing — the surgery, the audition, the day in court — someone who
loves you takes your face in their hands and says it: *I believe in you.* And whatever you
heard in those four words, it was not a report on your existence. Your existence was not in
question. You were standing right there. They were not saying *I have concluded that you are
real*; they were saying something enormous and particular and impossible to say any other
way, and you knew exactly what it was, and it went straight past your mind and landed
somewhere lower down.

Now set another sentence beside it: *I have faith in you.* Notice that it means the same
thing. Not something close; the same thing. You could swap them at the door of the operating
room and lose nothing. Two of the most ordinary sentences in the language, and neither one is
about whether anybody exists — and the two are interchangeable. That is a strange fact to sit
still under, and I want to sit under it, because almost everything that has gone wrong in the
last two centuries of arguing about God is folded up inside it.

Here is the trouble in one line. There is another way we use the word *believe*, and it is not
this way at all, and we have let the two collapse into one. *I believe it's going to rain.
I believe the keys are in the drawer. I believe there is intelligent life somewhere in the
galaxy.* Those are opinions — guesses about facts, held more or less firmly, rising and
falling with the evidence. And *that* is the sense we now hear, by reflex, when someone says
*I believe in God.* We hear a person reporting the odds: *given the uncertainty, my estimate is
that a God exists.* We hear a hypothesis, held without proof, about an invisible fact.

But that is not what the words are doing when a mother says them to a child at the free-throw
line. And it was never what they were doing in the mouth of the tradition that built its whole
life on them. The person who first wrote *I believe in God* was not rating a probability. He
was doing what you do at an altar. The word for that thing has a history, and we have lost it,
and losing the word cost us the thing. This essay is an attempt to find it again — starting
from the one sentence you have already said, and already meant.

## II. Two Words in One

Start by pulling the two senses apart, because everything follows from the fact that they are
not the same, and not even the same *kind* of thing.

Call the first one *belief-that*. Its object is a proposition — a statement that can be true or
false. *I believe that it's raining.* It lives in the head; it is a verdict on the evidence;
you can hold it at any strength from a hunch to near-certainty, and its strength tracks the
case for it. Give me a reason and it goes up; give me a counter-reason and it goes down. It is,
at bottom, an opinion — a very useful kind of thing, the kind of thing science is made of, and
the kind of thing you want your engineer to have plenty of about the bridge.

Call the second one *belief-in*. Its object is not a proposition but a person. *I believe in
you.* It does not live in the head; it lives in the will and the affections. It is not a verdict
on evidence — it is a stance you *take*, a leaning-toward, a decision to trust and to stay. And
here is the test that shows the two are different in kind and not merely in degree: they can
come apart completely, in both directions.[^1] You can believe *that* every true thing about a
person — that she is competent, honest, and kind, each proposition well supported — and still,
in the moment it costs something, fail to believe *in* her. And you can believe *in* someone —
throw your lot in, stake yourself, stand there when it is expensive — well past the point where
the propositions you could cite would justify it. A parent believes in a child the world has
written off. That is not bad statistics. It is a different act entirely.

The philosophers who have worked this seam have tried, some of them, to reduce the second to
the first — to say that *believing in* your friend is just *believing that* she has a list of
good qualities, plus a warm feeling.[^2] Even at their most reductive they cannot quite make it
go: there is always a residue left over, an element of *trust*, of *esteeming and entrusting*,
that the flat proposition *I think she is real and competent* does not contain and cannot
supply. That residue is the whole thing. That residue is what the mother hands the child at the
free-throw line, and it is not a fact she is reporting. It is a self she is offering.

Which is why the plainest human picture of *belief-in*, stripped of every distraction, is a
person at an altar saying two words. *I do.* No one has ever mistaken a wedding vow for a claim
about the groom's existence. The bride is not announcing her opinion that he is real. She is
pledging herself — her loyalty, her future, her whole weight — to a person standing in front of
her whom she cannot see the inside of and cannot prove. The vow does not report a fact about the
world. It *puts something into the world* that was not there before. Hold that picture. It is
the oldest meaning of the word we are chasing, and it is about to turn out to be the biblical
one.

[^1]: The distinction between *belief-in* and *belief-that* is developed by H.H. Price,
*Belief* (London: Allen & Unwin, 1969) — his Gifford Lectures, delivered 1960 — and in "Belief 'In' and Belief
'That'," *Religious Studies* 1.1 (1965), 5–27. Belief-*in* takes a person (an object of trust
and reliance) as its object; belief-*that* takes a proposition.

[^2]: Price himself leans partly reductive — much "belief in" can be unpacked into a set of
"beliefs that" — yet concedes an irreducible **evaluative** element (esteeming the object as
good) that propositional belief lacks. Non-reductive accounts (in the religious-epistemology
tradition, and W.C. Smith's use of it — see §III n. 5) hold trust/self-commitment to be *sui
generis*. Either way, the residue of trust is granted; that is all the argument needs.

## III. The Word Remembers

Lead with the claim the etymology will support, and then let it support only what it can. The
older language kept these two senses apart, and though the seam has been painted over, you can
still find it in the grain of the words.

Begin with *believe* itself. It is built — this is not in dispute — on the same ancient root
that gives us *love*, and gives us the old word *lief*, meaning *dear*.[^3] To *be-lieve* is
kin to to *be-love*. I have to be careful here, because there is a beautiful overclaim waiting
in this fact and I would rather hand it back than lean on it: it is not that Old English
speakers went about *defining* "believe" as "to love" — the earliest sense is a reconstruction,
hedged by the people who reconstruct it. What is solid is the kinship of the roots, and the
direction it points. The taproot of *believe* is closer to *holding dear* than to *rating
probable*. Whatever else the word was, it grew out of the soil of loyalty and love, not the
soil of evidence and opinion.

And the split I have been describing is not something I am reading back into history. It is on
the record. There was a time when English kept the labor divided between two words: *belief*
carried the sense *trust in God*, and *faith* carried the sense *loyalty to a person, pledged by
promise or duty.*[^4] Then, in the fourteenth century, the words traded places and jobs. *Faith*
took over the religious sense; *belief* was pushed the other way, toward *mental acceptance that
something is true* — and by the fifteen-hundreds it had hardened into the definition a dictionary
will still give you, the fatal one: *the conviction of the truth of a proposition* — and then
three words that ruin everything — *without knowledge.* That is the moment the trap was set. From
then on, to say *I believe in God* could be heard as *I hold, without knowledge, the opinion that
a God exists* — and the sentence a bride says at an altar was quietly refitted to mean the sentence
a gambler says at a table.

One more word, the most important one, because the whole tradition put it first. The creeds do not
begin *I have concluded that.* They begin *credo* — *I believe* — and *credo* is built on *cor*,
the Latin for **heart** (the same root under *cordial*, and *courage*, and *record*).[^5] To say
*credo* is to do something with the heart. Here is where I owe you the second piece of honesty,
because there is a cherished gloss that says *credo* is *cor* plus *do*, "I **give** my heart,"
and it is very nearly right and not quite. The second half of the word is not the verb *to give*;
it is an older root meaning *to place, to set, to put.*[^6] So the accurate rendering is a shade
less sentimental and, I think, better: not *I give my heart* but *I set my heart on* — *I put my
trust here, I entrust myself.* It is the language of a deposit, of a thing handed over for safe
keeping; the same root gives us *creditum*, a thing entrusted, which is why, when we lend money on
trust, we still say we extend *credit*. The creed was never a checklist of propositions a person
certified as true. It grew out of a vow — the thing a new believer said at baptism, closer in
form to a soldier's oath of loyalty than to a scientist's estimate. *Credo* meant: *here is where
I place my heart.*

Wilfred Cantwell Smith, who spent a career on exactly this, put it as plainly as it can be put: to *believe*, in older English, was to *belove* — to hold dear.[^7] The word we now use for holding a shaky opinion about an invisible fact once meant holding a person dear. We did not just change how we talk. We forgot what we were talking about.

German *glauben*, Gothic *galaubjan*), from Proto-Indo-European *leubh-* "to care, desire,
love" — the root also of *love*, *lief* ("dear"), and *libido*. The root kinship is standard
(Watkins; OED; etymonline). The gloss that the Germanic verb meant "to hold dear" is offered by
the etymologists themselves with a "**perhaps**" — a reconstruction of the semantic bridge, not
an attested Old English definition. Claimed here only as far as it goes: shared root, and the
direction it leans.

[^4]: Per etymonline, *belief* formerly carried the sense "trust in God," while *faith* meant
"loyalty to a person based on promise or duty"; in 14c. translations *faith* took the religious
sense and *belief* was pushed toward "mental acceptance of a thing as true." The noun *belief*
narrows by the 1530s to "conviction of the truth of a proposition or alleged fact **without
knowledge**." (etymonline.com/word/belief; /word/believe.)

[^5]: Latin *crēdere* "to trust, confide, entrust, believe," from PIE *ḱred-dʰeh₁-* =
*ḱerd-* "heart" (→ Latin *cor*; English *cardiac, cordial, courage, record*) + *dʰeh₁-* "to
put, place, set." The Sanskrit cognate *śrad-dhā-* ("faith, trust") is the identical "heart +
place" compound. The heart-connection is consensus (de Vaan, *Etym. Dict. of Latin*; Watkins).

[^6]: This is the honest flag. The popular parse *credo = cor + dō* ("I **give** my heart")
mistakes the second root: it is *dʰeh₁-*, "to put/place/set" (behind Greek *títhēmi*, English
*do*), **not** *dare* / *deh₃-*, "to give." The two fell together in Latin form, which is how
the folk refinement got in. "I give my heart" captures the spirit and tidies the grammar; the

[^7]: Wilfred Cantwell Smith, *Faith and Belief: The Difference Between Them* (Princeton, 1979);

"belief" misrepresents them. Smith is much criticized (the charge is a genetic fallacy — an
older meaning is not thereby the *true* one — and that faith cannot be wholly emptied of
propositional content). Both cautions are fair; the argument here needs only that the two are
*different in kind*, which even his critics grant.

## IV. The Demons Pass the Test

If the distinction were only a matter of English usage, it would be a curiosity. It is more than
that, and the tradition itself is where the knife comes out. Scripture does not merely permit the
line between believing-*that* and believing-*in*; it draws the line, and then it uses the line to
condemn the wrong kind of faith by name.

Here is the verse, and it should stop you. *"Thou believest that there is one God; thou doest
well: the devils also believe, and tremble."*[^8]

Read it slowly. The demons believe *that there is one God.* On the question that our whole modern
argument treats as the entire game — does God exist? — the demons are not agnostics. They are not
even theists in the hopeful, uncertain way a person might be. They *know.* Their belief-*that* is
flawless, better-evidenced than yours or mine will ever be, a proposition held with total
conviction and zero doubt. And James's verdict on that faith is devastating and flat: *thou doest
well* — said the way you would say it to a parrot that got the answer right — and then, *the
devils also believe, and tremble.* The one population in all of scripture with certain,
unclouded knowledge that God exists is the population of hell, and their certainty is
indistinguishable from terror.

So whatever the faith is that the tradition asks of you, it cannot be this. It cannot be assent to
the proposition *God exists*, because the damned have that in full and it saves no one. The thing
being asked for is of another kind entirely — the kind you cannot get to by being more sure. You
could raise your confidence that God exists to a hundred percent, could have it proved to you past
all possibility of doubt, and be, by this verse's own accounting, no closer to faith than a
demon. Certainty was never the thing. The demons are certain. What the demons cannot do — what
they refuse, which is why they tremble — is the other thing: to trust, to hand themselves over, to
say *I do.*

[^8]: James 2:19 (KJV). The Greek verb is *pisteuō* (believe/trust); the demons' response is
*phrissousin*, "shudder, bristle." James's whole argument in 2:14–26 turns on the uselessness of
a *pistis* that is bare propositional assent (the demons have it) apart from the trust that acts
— which is why the chapter insists faith without works is dead. The verse is the tradition making
the belief-*that* / belief-*in* distinction in its own voice.

## V. What the Tradition Actually Asked

If the faith being asked for is not belief-*that*, then the natural question is what it *is* — and
the answer is written into the very words the tradition used, in every language it used them.
Follow the word back and it says the same thing each time: trust, faithfulness, fidelity, the
setting of the heart. Never once: a good opinion about an uncertain fact.

Start in Hebrew, because the taproot is there. The word is *emunah*, and it does not mean
*intellectual assent* — it means *firmness, steadfastness, faithfulness, reliability.*[^9] It is
what a thing has when it holds; it is said of a pillar that bears weight and of a friend who does
not leave. Its root is *aman* — and if that root sounds familiar in your mouth, it should, because
it is the root of *amen.* The word a congregation says to seal a prayer, to throw its whole
assent behind what was said — *let it be firm, let it stand, truly* — is the very word for faith.
To say *amen* is to perform an act of *emunah*: not to note that a proposition is probable, but to
plant yourself on it. When the tradition wants to show you faith, it does not point to a person
weighing evidence. It points to Abraham, of whom it says the sentence Paul would build half his
theology on: *"And he believed in the LORD; and [They] counted it to him for righteousness."*[^10]
The verb is from that same root — *he entrusted himself, he leaned his weight on God* — and what
was counted to him as righteousness was not the accuracy of his cosmology. It was the leaning.

Then cross into Greek, where the New Testament lives, and the word is *pistis*, with its verb
*pisteuō.* Reach for the standard lexicon: one of its two governing definitions of the verb is not an opinion at all but *to entrust oneself.*[^11] The word means trust, fidelity, faithfulness, the loyalty that binds two
parties in a covenant — the same word a Greek would use for keeping faith with an ally. And watch
what John's Gospel does with the grammar, because it is almost untranslatable and it gives the
whole thing away. John does not, as a rule, write that you believe *that* something is the case.
He writes *pisteuō eis* — you believe *into.* *Whosoever believeth into him should not perish.*[^12]
You cannot believe *into* a proposition; the phrase is nonsense applied to a fact. You can only
believe *into* a person — move toward, adhere to, entrust yourself, fall in. The grammar itself is
a body leaning across a threshold.

So here is the shape of what happened, and it is a translator's tragedy in four steps. The Hebrew
*emunah*, *steadfast faithfulness*, was carried into Greek as *pistis*, *trust and fidelity*, which
was carried into Latin as *fides*, *the loyalty of a pledge* — and then into an English *faith*
that still held some of it, and finally shunted onto an English *belief* that had, as we have seen,
been hollowed out into *an opinion held without knowledge.* Each handoff was faithful enough. But
by the end of the relay the word at the finish line meant something close to the opposite of the
word at the start. The tradition asked *will you entrust yourself?* and the question reached us as
*do you happen to think it's true?*[^13]

[^9]: Hebrew *emunah* (אֱמוּנָה), "faithfulness, steadfastness, reliability," from the root *aman*
(אמן), "to be firm, reliable" — whence also *amen* (אָמֵן), "truly, let it be firm." Used of human
steadfastness (Hab 2:4) and of God's (Deut 32:4; Lam 3:23). Habakkuk 2:4, "the just shall live by
his faith," most naturally reads *by his faithfulness*; note the disputed suffix (the righteous
one's fidelity, or God's — the Septuagint has *ek pisteōs mou*, "by **my** faith[fulness]").

[^10]: Genesis 15:6 (KJV). The verb is *he'emin* (Hiphil of *aman*): trusted, relied on, entrusted
himself to. Paul makes it the hinge of Romans 4 and Galatians 3:6 — Abraham reckoned righteous by
trust, prior to law and apart from works.

[^11]: BDAG (the standard NT Greek lexicon) gives two principal senses for *pisteuō*; the second is "to entrust oneself to an entity in complete confidence," and even the first — "to consider something to be true and therefore worthy of one's trust" — is defined through *trust*, not bare assent. *pistis* ranges over trust, confidence, faithfulness, fidelity,
and (in the wider Greek world) a pledge or guarantee given in a relationship. The fullest recent
study is Teresa Morgan, *Roman Faith and Christian Faith: Pistis and Fides in the Early Roman
Empire and Early Churches* (Oxford, 2015): *pistis/fides* is primarily *relational trust* binding
persons, not first of all propositional belief. (Matthew Bates, *Salvation by Allegiance Alone*
[2017], presses *pistis* toward "allegiance"; it is a stronger, contested reading — noted, not
leaned on.)

[^12]: John 3:16 (and 3:18, 6:29, 20:31): *pisteuō eis* + accusative, "believe into." Grammatically
unusual — one does not "believe into" a proposition — and read by most exegetes as adherence to,
self-entrusting to, a person. John 20:31 holds both together: "that ye might believe *that* Jesus
is the Christ… and that believing ye might have life through his name" — belief-*that* issuing in
belief-*into*. Don't sever them.

[^13]: The relay *emunah → pistis → fides → faith → belief*. Each translation is defensible; the
drift is cumulative, and lands (per §III) on an English "belief" that by the 1530s meant
"conviction of a proposition without knowledge" — the far end from Hebrew *firmness/fidelity*.

## VI. About the Unseen, Not the Unevidenced

There is one verse that the modern misunderstanding leans on harder than any other — the verse
everyone reaches for to prove that faith just *is* believing without evidence — and it is worth
stopping on, because it says very nearly the reverse of what it is made to say.

*"Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen."*[^14] There it
is, the case for the prosecution: faith deals in *things not seen*, therefore faith is belief in
the unproven, therefore faith is exactly the epistemic vice its critics say it is. But look at the
two words the English softens. *Substance* is *hypostasis* — a heavy, concrete word; in the legal
papyri of the period it is the word for a **title-deed**, the document that stands under a claim
and guarantees it. And *evidence* is *elenchos* — not a gap where evidence should be, but the
Greek word for *proof*, for the cross-examining test that demonstrates a thing is so. So the verse
is not confessing that faith lacks grounds. It is claiming, audaciously, that faith *is* the
grounds — the title-deed of what is hoped for, the proof of what is not seen.

And that last phrase is the one that has been misread for centuries by turning one word into
another. *Things not seen* is not *things not evidenced.* The unseen is not the unproven. There is
a vast territory of the real that no eye has ever fallen on and that is not thereby doubtful — your
own mind, to begin with, which you have never seen and do not doubt; the loyalty of your oldest
friend, which does not show up on any instrument and which you would stake your life on. Faith, on
the tradition's own account, is not the willful ignoring of evidence about the visible. It is the
faculty by which a person stands firm toward the *invisible* — the same faculty, exactly, by which
you stand firm toward a friend across a distance you cannot see into. The critics have caught a
real fish in the wrong net. There is a kind of "faith" that means believing against the evidence.
The tradition's word was never it — and its own most-quoted sentence on the subject says the
opposite in the plainest Greek it has.

[^14]: Hebrews 11:1 (KJV). *hypostasis* = "that which stands under," foundation, substance; in
Hellenistic legal usage, a title-deed or guarantee of ownership. *elenchos* = proof, conviction,
the evidence that tests and demonstrates (cf. the verb in 2 Tim 3:16, "reproof"). The verse
predicates *assurance* and *proof* of faith regarding *things unseen* — a claim about the
invisible, not a licensing of the unevidenced.

## VII. The Two Who Make the Same Mistake

Now let me put the strongest possible version of the other side on the table, in its own words,
because if I am right it is not only wrong but wrong in a way that reveals the whole confusion at
once.

Richard Dawkins has said it as well as anyone, and he has said it more than once, so it is fair to
take it as his settled view: *"Faith is the great cop-out, the great excuse to evade the need to
think and evaluate evidence. Faith is belief in spite of, even perhaps because of, the lack of
evidence."*[^15] I want to concede this fully, because a great deal of it is true and the
concession costs me nothing. There really are people who use the word *faith* to mean precisely
that — the willed holding of an improbable belief in defiance of the evidence, with the strain of
the holding counted as a virtue and the demand for evidence treated as the sin. That faith exists.
It is common. And it deserves every word of Dawkins's scorn; I have no interest in defending it,
and neither did the tradition, whose own scripture (we just read it) says faith is a title-deed and
a proof, not a refusal to look.

But here is the turn, and it is the whole point of the essay. That definition — *faith is belief
without evidence* — is a modern stipulation, and the one who most fiercely *attacks* it and the one
who most fiercely *defends* it are making the identical mistake. The New Atheist and the
fundamentalist agree, completely, on what faith is. Both of them define it as *assent to an
improbable proposition.* They differ only on the follow-through — one prosecutes the assent as a
crime against the intellect, the other preaches it as a duty of the soul — but they are bent over
the same object, arguing about the same thing, and it is the wrong thing. It is the broken half of
a word. Somewhere back in the fourteen-hundreds the language snapped *belief-in* off from
*belief-that*, and the trust-and-fidelity half fell to the floor and was swept up, and the
opinion-about-a-fact half was left on the table — and here are two armies who have spent a century
fighting over the piece on the table, each certain the other is the enemy, neither having noticed
that the thing they are fighting about is a fragment. The atheist is right that *belief-that God
exists, held without evidence* is a poor foundation for a life. The believer who builds on it has
built on the same poor thing. They are, God help them, on the same side of the only line that
matters, and the line runs somewhere else entirely — between the piece on the table and the piece
on the floor.

[^15]: Richard Dawkins, from a lecture at the Edinburgh International Science Festival, 15 April
1992, printed as "A scientist's case against God," *The Independent* (London), 20 April 1992. The
line is frequently misattributed to *The God Delusion* (2006); it predates that book by fourteen
years. Cognate definitions ("blind trust, in the absence of evidence, even in the teeth of
evidence") recur in *The Selfish Gene* and "Viruses of the Mind." Read generously: Dawkins's
*target* — willed belief against the evidence — is real and worth attacking; the error is that he
has mistaken it for the whole of what the word means.

## VIII. The Vow

So come back to the altar, where we started, because everything has been walking toward it.

No one has ever asked the bride, before she says *I do*, to first establish that the groom exists.
The question would be a kind of madness; it would mean she had misunderstood, at the deepest level,
what she was there to do. She is not there to certify a fact about the world. She is there to
*pledge a self* — to hand over her loyalty and her future and her whole weight to a person she
cannot see the inside of and could never prove, and to do it not because the evidence has forced
her hand but because she has decided, freely, where to place her heart. *I do* is *belief-in* in
its purest human form. And it is, to the letter, the word the tradition used for faith in God.
Faith is the *I do*, said to the One you cannot see.

Let me add one last thing, and lay it down lightly, because it is the deepest reading and I do not
want to freight the close with it. The tradition's own central phrase for the faith that saves —
*pistis Christou* — is grammatically ambiguous in a way that has kept scholars busy for fifty
years, and one of the two readings is quietly enormous.[^16] It can mean *faith in Christ* — our
trust, directed at Him. But it can also mean *the faith of Christ* — His own faithfulness, His own
fidelity unto death, as the thing that actually saves. And if that reading holds, then even faith
turns out not to be a burden laid on us — not a proposition we must strain to believe, not a feat
of certainty we must perform — but first of all *His* steadfastness, into which we are invited to
lean. The vow that matters most was already spoken, and faithfully kept, by someone else. Ours is
only the *amen.*

I am not going to end by handing you a proof that God exists. That was never the thing being
asked, and to offer it now would be to make, at the last line, the very mistake the whole essay has
been trying to undo. I am going to do something smaller and, I think, harder. I am going to take the
question we have all been trained to think is the religious question — *do you believe that God
exists?* — and set it down on the floor next to the other broken piece, where it has been lying all
along, and leave the cleared place empty. And in the empty place I will put back the question the
word was always asking, the one the bride answers, the one that has nothing to do with whether you
can prove anything and everything to do with what you will do with the one life you have been
handed. Not *what do you believe?* But: *in whom? To whom will you give your heart?*

[^16]: *pistis Christou* (Galatians 2:16, 20; 3:22; Romans 3:22; Philippians 3:9): the genitive is
ambiguous. The **objective** reading, "faith *in* Christ" (our faith directed at Him), is
traditional (defended by James D.G. Dunn). The **subjective** reading, "the faith[fulness] *of*
Christ" (His own faithful obedience as the ground of salvation), was pressed by Richard B. Hays,
*The Faith of Jesus Christ* (1983), and is supported in various forms by Morgan and others.
Galatians 2:16 is the flashpoint, containing both the phrase and the verb "we believed in Christ"
in one sentence. Offered here as *a* reading, held lightly — the close does not depend on settling
it.
