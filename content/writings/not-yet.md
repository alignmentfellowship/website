## I. The Dozenth Quit

I quit smoking a few days ago. That would be unremarkable except that I have now done it about a
dozen times.

And I have been good at this, which is the part that matters. I quit in the autumn of 2019 and did
not smoke again until the autumn of 2021 — two years, clean, no particular drama. Then I did it a
second time: autumn of 2023 to autumn of 2025, another two years. At any point inside either of those
stretches, asked whether I was a smoker, I'd have said no. I'd have believed it. I'd have had two
years of evidence in my hand.

Then this year. I smoked from February to April, quit, and stayed off through the summer — four
months, which felt like nothing next to the two-year runs and which I was quietly proud of anyway. In
August I started again; two weeks of that, and I quit a few days ago. You can see the shape of the
thing, and that the shape is getting shorter.

Notice what that record rules out. **Two years is not a failure of willpower.** Two years, twice, isn't
a man who didn't try hard enough; it is a man who succeeded, completely, at exactly the thing he
set out to do — and then found it waiting for him at the far end of the success. Whatever this is, it
is not a shortage of resolve. I have plenty of resolve. It has never once been the variable that
decided anything.

And then there was the summer, which I haven't told many people about — and note that I was four
months clean of cigarettes for the whole of it, which turns out to have protected nothing at all. For
weeks at a stretch — not evenings, weeks — I played three video games. *Civilization*, *Heroes of Might and Magic*, and the
Battlegrounds mode of *Hearthstone*: three turn-based games, which is to say three machines built on
the principle that the next turn is always cheap and the sequence never resolves. I left the
apartment to buy groceries and to walk my dog. That was the whole of it. I sat down after breakfast
and I played until it was dark. Exactly: nothing slipped. I was not ambushed by
the hours. And when I wasn't playing I was watching other people play — Battlegrounds streamers on
Twitch, hours of it. The appetite didn't even stop at my own turns.

And the usual version of this story is a lie of a particular and comfortable kind. **I was enjoying
it.** It didn't curdle in the second week into something joyless that I performed against my own
will. I was having a good time. The received
account of addiction wants the pleasure to drain out early — wants the addict miserable, going
through the motions, so that the thing can be filed under suffering rather than under wanting — and
that account is enormously convenient for everybody who isn't in it. Mine stayed fun. I lost the
summer anyway.

There is a second comfortable lie in the same family, and I have to give that one up too. **I was
choosing it.** Not helplessly, not in a fog, not carried off by something stronger than me —
deliberately, every morning, with my eyes open. Nothing overpowered me. I wanted to play video games,
and I played video games.

I knew the whole time what I was supposed to be doing instead. There was a responsible voice
underneath all of it, entirely explicit and never once vague: apply for work. Update the résumé. Ship
something to the public GitHub so there is visible evidence of an engineer at this address. Every so
often I'd turn and face it for an afternoon, half-heartedly, being *productive*, and then go back. I
did none of it across the whole summer, and the reason does me no credit and took no effort to find.
**I didn't want to.** They were not as fun as the games.

The only place any of it hurt was in the telling. I'd be with the men in my group, or on the phone
with my mother, and I'd reach the part where I say what I had actually been doing with my week, and
there it was — not guilt, exactly. Embarrassment. Nothing in the apartment ever produced that. It
arrived when somebody was listening.

So the two of them are not one experience, and the difference is going to matter. The cigarettes are
the thing everyone pictures: the promise made in full sincerity at eleven in the morning and broken
with peculiar quietness at one — not with a struggle, usually, but with a kind of administrative calm,
as though some other department had reviewed the decision and reversed it without needing to inform
me. The games were nothing like that. There was no struggle to lose. There was a man having a good
time, for weeks, and a small clear voice off to one side that he could hear perfectly well and
declined to obey.

And in all of it I have never once concluded that you are like this.

It hasn't occurred to me to write that the human condition is a hand reaching for a pack, or that the
hidden engine under every civilization is a creature who wants one more turn. If I proposed it you'd
laugh, and you'd be right to, and I'd laugh too. The idea is absurd on its face.

Two men did the equivalent, on a subject far better suited to it, and we have been living inside
their conclusions ever since.

## II. The Report Is Old, and It Is Exact

Before either of them, the phenomenon itself — because it's real, and the tradition already holds it
with a precision nothing since has improved on.

The oldest version in our scriptures is Paul's, and he gives it in the voice of a man genuinely
baffled by his own behavior. *For that which I do I allow not: for what I would, that do I not; but
what I hate, that do I.* Four verses later he says it again, having apparently found no better way to
put it: *For the good that I would I do not: but the evil which I would not, that I do.*[^romans] It
is not a confession of wickedness. It is a report of a mechanical failure — the lever is pulled, the
machine does something else.

It is also, nearly, the summer I have just described — and the *nearly* is worth stopping on. Paul's
is the flattering version of the trouble: a man who wants the good and cannot reach it. Mine was not
that. The good was entirely within reach; I simply liked the other thing better. *For the good that I
would I do not* — except that I am not certain, on the evidence, that I would.

For the less flattering form of it we have to wait three hundred years, and the man who supplies that
supplies this first. Augustine gets closer to the seat of the strangeness than anyone before or
since. The mind, he observes, commands the body, and the body obeys at once; the mind commands
*itself*, and is resisted.[^divided] Notice what that sentence isolates. The scandal isn't that a
person wants a bad thing. It's that the party refusing the order is the same party giving it.

And then, sixteen centuries later, in an America of drunks who had run out of every other idea, the
identical report is filed a third time, in the flattest possible language, as the opening sentence of
a program: *We admitted we were powerless over alcohol — that our lives had become
unmanageable.*[^stepone]

Three documents, nineteen centuries apart, describing one thing. What is identical in all three is
not the theology, which differs, and not the vocabulary, which differs more. It is the
**astonishment** — not at the wanting, which is never mysterious to the person doing it, but at the
mechanism. Every version of this report is given in the voice of somebody surprised by their own body.
Nobody performs this; there's no dignity in it to perform. That is exactly why the report
should be trusted, and I concede its full universal force without reservation: **the divided will is
not a condition of some people.** It is the human situation and the
tradition has said so, in writing, three
times, without flinching.

Everything that follows depends on that concession, and none of it is an argument with the diagnosis.

[^romans]: Romans 7:15 and 7:19 (KJV), quoted verbatim. The passage runs from 7:14 to 7:25 and is the fullest
description of a divided will in the New Testament; Paul's conclusion — *O wretched man that I am!* —
is a cry, not an analysis, which is part of the evidence for its sincerity. Whether Paul is speaking
autobiographically or in an assumed voice is a live scholarly question this argument does not need
settled: either way, the phenomenon being described is described exactly.

[^divided]: Augustine, *Confessions* VIII.9.21: "Whence is this monstrous thing? And why is it?...
The mind commands the body, and it obeys immediately; the mind commands itself, and is resisted."
(Pusey, in the NPNF revision, as at New Advent; the chapter is headed "That the Mind Commands the
Mind, But It Wills Not Entirely.") It is the sharpest pre-modern statement of the problem in the
Western canon, and Augustine's own word for it is the right one: a *monstrous thing* — not a sin he
is confessing but a mechanism he cannot account for.

[^stepone]: Step One of the Twelve Steps, quoted verbatim, first published in *Alcoholics Anonymous*
(1939) and unchanged since; text © Alcoholics Anonymous World Services. The grammar of this sentence
becomes the argument in §V.

## III. The Man Who Had the Report and Nowhere to Put It

Augustine's account of his own compulsion is the most exact in the Western canon; he gave it about
himself, in public, under his own name; and then, having no one to give it to who could receive it as
a peer, he gave it to the species. The charge here isn't against his honesty. That is the greatest
honesty in the literature, and his own sentences say it better than I can.

*Da mihi castitatem et continentiam, sed noli modo.* **Grant me chastity and continence, but not
yet.**[^notyet] Everyone knows the line and most people hear it as a joke about a young man's
appetite. There is nothing clever in it. It is a prayer, sincerely offered, with a rider attached
that guarantees it cannot be answered — and every person who has ever set a quit date three weeks out
has prayed it word for word. What he's reporting, with unusual accuracy, is that he wanted the thing
taken away and wanted it not taken away yet, and that both wants were his.

And notice what the rider is made of. In his own gloss on the prayer, he says he was afraid of being
heard too soon — of being cured of a disease he would rather have had satisfied than extinguished.
The pleasure was entirely intact. That is neither mitigating nor aggravating; it is the ordinary
condition of the thing. Any account that requires the enjoyment to have drained away first will not
fit most of the people who are in it — and neither will any account that requires a struggle.
There is no fight going on here. There is a man asking, in good faith, for the fight to be scheduled
later.

Then the passage that costs the reader something. The woman he had lived with for something like
thirteen years, who had borne his son, was sent back to Africa because she was an obstacle to a
respectable marriage — *torn from my side*, he writes, his heart *racked, and wounded, and bleeding*.
She took a vow never to know another man, and by every account kept it. The betrothed was two years
too young to marry. And Augustine — waiting, wounded, grieving — **procured another**, *not a wife, though*, to get him
through the interval, being, in his own phrase, *not so much a lover of marriage as a slave to
lust*. The wound was not healed, he says. It was **mortified**.[^concubine]

That's a relapse during a waiting period, described in the exact shape a room would recognize
instantly: the loss, the resolve, the interval, the substitute, the honest note that the substitute
made it worse. Anyone who has sat in a circle of folding chairs has heard that share, in nearly those
words, from somebody three seats over.

And he never records her name.

I don't raise that to convict him. I raise it because it tells you what kind of document the
*Confessions* is. It is addressed to God and overheard by everyone; the woman is in it as an event in
his interior life, which is what she had to be, because the book has no room for a second person with
her own account of things. That isn't a flaw in the book. It's the *genre* of the book, and the genre is going to matter in a
minute.

Now: what he did with the report.

Out of that material — his own material, his own inability, examined with a rigor nobody had brought
to interior life before — came the account of **concupiscence** that the West then inherited as
doctrine: disordered desire as the wound itself, and the sexual act as the channel along which the
inherited fault runs.[^concupiscence] The most private affliction of one North African rhetorician,
which he had reported with complete truthfulness, was installed as the mechanism of the human
condition.

The charge has to be exact, because the loud version of it is false and I'd rather retire that myself. **Augustine did not invent Western sexual anxiety.** It is older than he is by centuries —
the ascetics predate him, the Stoics predate him, Paul predates him, and Christianity had been
arguing about marriage and continence for two hundred years before he was born, and he spent real
effort defending marriage against people who thought worse of it than he did. The claim here is
narrower and it is the only one that holds: he gave that anxiety its most durable **theoretical
form**, and lent it the authority of doctrine, so that what the West received and transmitted for
sixteen centuries was one man's particular affliction wearing the robes of anthropology. Legitimation,
not origin. That is enough to be going on with.

And underneath it is the structural fact this whole argument turns on.

The *Confessions* is the most famous confession in the language and it is entirely **vertical**. It
confesses upward, to God, and outward, to every reader since. What he never did — in the
one sense that would have made a difference — is confess **across**. There was a mother, and a
bishop, and a God, and in time a diocese of his own. There was no person in the next chair: somebody with the same affliction, holding no authority over him, and standing to gain nothing whatever by what they said.

[^notyet]: Augustine, *Confessions* VIII.7.17: *Da mihi castitatem et continentiam, sed noli modo* —
"grant me chastity and continence, but not yet." Augustine's own gloss is more damning than the line's
reputation, and it is worth having exactly: "I was afraid lest You should hear me soon, and soon
deliver me from the disease of concupiscence, which I desired to have satisfied rather than
extinguished." (Pusey/NPNF, which renders the prayer "Grant me chastity and continency, but not yet";
*continence* is the more usual English form and is used in the body.)

[^concubine]: Augustine, *Confessions* VI.15.25 (Pusey/NPNF), the chapter headed "He Dismisses One
Mistress, and Chooses Another." The woman is unnamed throughout the *Confessions*; she had been with
him roughly thirteen years and was the mother of his son Adeodatus. Verbatim: "my mistress being torn
from my side as an impediment to my marriage, my heart, which clave to her, was racked, and wounded,
and bleeding. And she went back to Africa, making a vow unto You never to know another man... But I,
unhappy one, who could not imitate a woman, impatient of delay, since it was not until two years'
time I was to obtain her I sought — being not so much a lover of marriage as a slave to lust —
procured another (not a wife, though)... Nor was that wound of mine as yet cured... but after
inflammation and most acute anguish it mortified, and the pain became numbed, but more desperate."
The two-year wait is the betrothed's age; she was below the legal minimum.

[^concupiscence]: The mature doctrine is in the anti-Pelagian works rather than the *Confessions* —
*De nuptiis et concupiscentia* above all, its first book written in 419 against the Pelagians and its
second in 421 answering Julian of Eclanum — where concupiscence is the disordered desire that remains
after baptism and by which original sin is propagated, and sexual desire is its named instance
because generation is the channel of transmission. Two fairness notes, both against this argument's
convenience. First, Augustine defends marriage as a genuine good: the three goods of *proles, fides,
sacramentum* — offspring, fidelity, the indissoluble bond — are set out in *De bono coniugali* (c.
401), and he is arguing throughout against opponents who thought worse of marriage than he did.
Second, he is not the origin of Christian sexual asceticism, which long predates him. The claim in
the body is therefore **legitimation, not origin**: he gave the anxiety its most durable theoretical
form and the standing of doctrine.

## IV. The Only Analyst Who Was Never Analyzed

The second case is not the same as the first, and the argument is stronger for saying so. I'm not
going to tell you that Freud was a sex addict; the record doesn't support it, and I have no interest
in an argument needing a diagnosis I cannot make of a man who cannot answer. The parallel with
Augustine is not clinical. It is **structural**, and structurally it is exact.

The founding fact is stranger than its familiarity lets it seem. **Psychoanalysis was founded on a
self-analysis.** After his father's death in 1896, Freud spent several years
analyzing himself — his dreams, his memories, his feeling about his father — and the book that came
out of it, *The Interpretation of Dreams*, he later described as a portion of that self-analysis, his
reaction to his father's death.[^selfanalysis] The founding document of the discipline is a man's
report on himself, produced alone — and he says so.

Now hold that beside the discipline's own first premise, which is that **no one can see their own
unconscious.** That is not a peripheral claim; it is the whole reason the profession exists. It is why
every analyst trained since is required to undergo an analysis themselves before they are permitted to
conduct one.[^training] The first one never did. The man who established that a person cannot be
trusted to read their own depths read his own, alone, and published the result as a method.

There was a confidant, of a kind. Wilhelm Fliess received the letters in which the self-analysis was
conducted — a correspondent, not an analyst, and a crank besides: author of a theory locating neurosis
in the nose, and surgeon of a catastrophe that nearly killed one of Freud's patients.[^fliess] The one
man Freud confessed horizontally to could not perform the office. When the friendship ended, that
channel closed and nothing replaced it.

What replaced it was a court. In 1912, on Ernest Jones's proposal, a Secret Committee was formed
around Freud to guard the doctrine — imagined by Jones, in his own words, as a united small body,
like the Paladins of Charlemagne, to guard the kingdom and policy of **their master**. At its first
meeting Freud handed each member a Greek intaglio mounted in a gold ring.[^committee] Adler and Stekel
were already gone; Jung was going; Rank and Ferenczi would go in their turn, and in each case the
departure was the departure of a man who had disagreed.

A man surrounded by people who cannot contradict him is a man alone.

And out of that solitude came the theory that made desire the hidden engine of everything — of
dreams, of jokes, of slips, of art, of religion, of the whole architecture of the mind. I am not
claiming a causal chain from a private arrangement to a public doctrine, and I do not need one. The
shape is the shape of the last movement: no peer to hand a self-report to, and an institution ready
to receive it as a general account of human beings.

Which yields the difference between the two men, and it is why they belong in one essay.
**Augustine universalized the compulsion. Freud universalized the management of it.** One made the
grip of desire the human wound; the other made its containment the human machinery. And those are not
opposite errors — they are the same error, from two sides, because the addict and the abstainer are
the same posture. Both have organized an entire life around one thing. The rooms know this and say it
in four words: white-knuckling is not sobriety. Someone who hasn't had a drink in twenty years and
thinks about it every hour hasn't put it down; they are holding it very tightly with both hands.

Now set against the Paladins and the rings the way the rooms are actually built, because the contrast
is not modesty. It is engineering. There is no membership roll. There are no experts and no
credentials. There is a sponsor, who is not a therapist and not a superior and holds no office — only
somebody a little further along the same road. And there is the second of the Traditions, which reads:
*our leaders are but trusted servants; they do not govern.*[^traditiontwo]

That sentence is not humility for its own sake. It is a device for making sure the room never
produces a master, and so never produces a court.


[^selfanalysis]: Freud's self-analysis began in earnest after his father Jacob's death on 23 October
1896 and was conducted largely in his correspondence with Wilhelm Fliess. *The Interpretation of
Dreams* was published on 4 November 1899, postdated 1900 by the publisher, in an edition of 600
copies that took eight years to sell. In the preface to the second edition (dated Summer 1908) Freud
wrote of the book: "It was, I found, a portion of my own self-analysis, my reaction to my father's
death — that is to say, to the most important event, the most poignant loss, of a man's life."


[^training]: The training analysis — the requirement that a candidate be analyzed before practicing —
was proposed as obligatory by Hermann Nunberg at the 1918 Budapest congress, with Freud's agreement,
and became an official rule in 1926; the machinery was Max Eitingon's Berlin institute (founded 1920)
and the International Training Commission set up at the Bad Homburg congress of 1925. So the body's
claim is about the discipline as it settled rather than as it began — which is the honest form of it,
and it does not soften the point: the rule was in force for the last thirteen years of Freud's life
and was never going to be applied to him.

[^fliess]: Wilhelm Fliess, a Berlin otolaryngologist, held that neuroses originated in the nose and
could be treated surgically, and maintained an elaborate theory of biological periodicity. In 1895 he
operated on Freud's patient Emma Eckstein and left roughly half a meter of surgical gauze in the
wound; she haemorrhaged repeatedly, nearly died, and was permanently disfigured. **What Freud then
did with it is the part that belongs in this movement:** he exonerated Fliess entirely, denied any
negligence, and came to regard Eckstein's bleeding as hysterical — the expression of a longing for
love. That is the exact inverse of the office a peer performs. The confidant was a partisan and a
fellow theorist, and the correction ran the wrong way down the line.

[^committee]: The Secret Committee was formed in 1912 at Ernest Jones's suggestion, in the wake of
Adler's and Stekel's departures and with Jung's break underway. The members were Jones (chairman),
Ferenczi, Rank, Sachs and Abraham, with Eitingon joining in 1919 and Anna Freud replacing Rank in
1924; Freud presented each man a Greek intaglio mounted in a gold ring at the first meeting, 25 May
1913. The description of the committee as a united small body "designed, like the Paladins of
Charlemagne, to guard the kingdom and policy of their master" is Jones's own, in *The Life and Work
of Sigmund Freud* — **a page reference is not pinned here**, and it is quoted as the consistently
reported wording rather than as a located citation.

[^traditiontwo]: Tradition Two of the Twelve Traditions, © AAWS: "For our group purpose there is but
one ultimate authority — a loving God as He may express Himself in our group conscience. Our leaders
are but trusted servants; they do not govern." The house pronoun convention is not applied here: this
is another organization's fixed text quoted as that text, which the convention expressly excludes,
and the same exclusion covers "God as we understood Him" wherever the Steps are quoted below.

## V. What a Room Actually Does — and It Is Not Absolution

I have argued elsewhere, in [The Sheep in the
Basement](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-sheep-in-the-basement), that the twelve-step rooms
reliably produce the thing the church upstairs keeps trying to compel and cannot. I am not going to
run that case again. I want a different mechanism out of the same room, and it is the one this whole
essay has been walking toward.

**A room is not, first of all, an absolution machine. It is a sizing instrument.**

Say the thing out loud to a dozen people who're sick in the same way you are, and two corrections
arrive in the same moment. The two are different, and both are necessary, and this is the part that
matters: only one kind of confession can deliver both.

The first is **you're not uniquely disgusting**. The shame comes off, because the man across the
circle has done worse and is sitting there in a fleece drinking bad coffee, unexecuted. Every
tradition that has taken confession seriously knows this one, and the vertical version does it
beautifully. Augustine got it. It is much of what the *Confessions* is for, and it has been doing it
for other people for sixteen hundred years.

The second is **you are not universally representative**, and the theory dies in your mouth as you
speak it, because eleven other people share your affliction and do not share your account of it. It's
very hard to believe your compulsion is the secret structure of the human person while a woman two
chairs down describes the same compulsion in terms you don't recognize at all.

Vertical confession cannot do the second one. Not badly — *at all*.

Think about what the vertical actually returns. God doesn't tell you that your affliction is ordinary
and bounded and yours; God forgives it, which is a different and larger thing, and the forgiveness
arrives with no information about scale attached. And readers don't tell you either. A reader recoils
or a reader nods, and both of those are ratifications — the recoil says *you have found something
terrible in the human heart* and the nod says *yes, this is all of us*, and neither of them says the
sentence you actually needed, which is: **that part is just yours.**

A congregation is, structurally, a machine for converting one person's private report into a general
truth. That isn't a criticism; it's what a congregation is for, and how a tradition transmits anything
across centuries. But it means a man who is both the most honest self-examiner of his age and the
bishop of a diocese is in the worst position on earth to learn the size of his own affliction. The
honesty guarantees the report gets made. The office guarantees nobody in earshot can hand it back.

Only a peer can hand it back. That's not sentiment — it's a set of three requirements, and you can
check them:

- **Sick in the same way**, so they cannot be shocked, and so their comparison is worth something.
- **No authority over you**, so they cannot absolve, and therefore cannot substitute absolution for
  measurement.
- **Nothing to gain**, so they cannot flatter, and cannot be building anything on your account of
  yourself.

The obvious counter-example is the confessor, and he deserves better than to be waved past, because on
the face of it he is better placed than anybody in a church basement. A priest who has heard ten
thousand confessions holds comparative data no meeting can match, and he knows, with a precision no
layman will ever have, exactly how ordinary your sin is.

Now look at what he cannot do with it. Bound by the seal, he cannot tell you what the others said.
Holding the authority to absolve, he gives the encounter somewhere better to go — and absolution is so
much larger a gift than measurement that nobody in the box ever thinks to ask for the smaller one. And
he is not, so far as you know, sick in the way you are; he is there in the office of a man who is
well. So the confessional delivers the first correction with more authority than any meeting on earth
and the second not at all — and not through any failure of the priest. Through its design. It was
built to forgive, and it forgives.

Augustine's own tradition of confession to a superior is that arrangement exactly: the best thing
available, and the wrong instrument for this particular measurement. Nobody around him met even one of
the three requirements. Freud built an institution engineered to prevent anyone from meeting the
third.

And the proof that the rooms know all of this — that this is the actual thing they are for, and that
they knew it in 1939 — is not a doctrine or a slogan. It is a piece of grammar.

The First Step does not read *we admitted we were powerless.* It reads *we admitted we were powerless
**over alcohol***. The object is named. The object is bounded. It is stated as a specific relationship
to a specific substance, and the sentence stops before it can become an anthropology. Every one of the
two hundred-odd fellowships that adapted the Steps kept that structure and changed the noun.[^object]

Whoever wrote that line knew exactly what happens when the object comes off the verb. Augustine's
first step, had he ever written one, would have stopped at the verb — and that difference is not one
of holiness or of intelligence, because on both he has me and most of the rest of us comfortably
beaten. It is a difference of who was in the room.

[^object]: The point is structural and survives the variation: each fellowship names its own object —
alcohol, narcotics, food, gambling, another person's drinking, the effects of family dysfunction —
and the sentence remains a bounded statement about a particular relationship rather than a claim about
human nature. The Adult Children fellowship, which is the room I sit in on Thursday mornings, retargets
Step One to powerlessness over the effects of alcoholism or other family dysfunction; the grammar is
identical. Texts © AAWS and © ACA WSO respectively.

## VI. The Counterfactual, and It Is a Counterfactual

The premise this piece is named for — *if they had had a room* — is a thought experiment, and the
first fact about it acquits both men of the charge they would otherwise be carrying: **there was no
room.** Sex and Love Addicts Anonymous wasn't founded until 1976.[^sla] The whole apparatus — the
bounded first step, the sponsor with no office, the anonymity, the tradition that forbids anyone to
govern — is younger than the paperback. Augustine had a monastery, which is a place where you confess
to a superior, and Freud had a profession he had personally invented. Neither of them declined to
walk into a meeting. There wasn't a meeting to decline. This is the absence of an invention, not a
failure of two characters, and if any of this has read as a verdict on either man it has gone wrong.

And when the room was finally built, it named itself after Augustine.

The founders, meeting in Boston in the mid-seventies, ran into a church that wouldn't host a group
called Sex and Love Addicts Anonymous — the name was too provocative — so they took a second name,
which is still the fellowship's legal name today: **The Augustine Fellowship**. The basic text
explains the choice, and the sentence is worth reading slowly. *Augustine of Hippo — as those who
have read his autobiography,* Confessions*, know — was probably one of us.* They go on to say that
whether a church later canonized him was not their business, that being an outside issue and
forbidden them by their own Tenth Tradition; but that the dynamics of his story, and the inner
workings and struggles of the man himself, left them *with little doubt that he would have
understood, and felt welcome among us.*[^augustinefellowship]

So the counterfactual is not really mine. A room of compulsives in the nineteen-seventies read the
*Confessions*, recognized their own symptoms in it without the least difficulty, and concluded that
the author was one of them and would have been at home in the meeting. That is the horizontal reading
of that book, performed at last, sixteen centuries late, by the only people on earth qualified to
perform it. And notice what they took and what they left. What they didn't take was his doctrine.
What they declined, on principle, was any opinion about his sainthood. What they took was the man, as
a fellow sufferer — and they put his name on the door.

None of which lets me claim that the *Confessions* would have come out differently, or that the
doctrine of concupiscence would not have been written. Those are unfalsifiable. What I can say is not
speculative at all, because I watch it happen: a man describes the sincere prayer with the rider
attached, and the relapse in the waiting period, and the substitute that made the wound worse — and
what comes back is not absolution and not a doctrine. It is somebody saying *yes, that one, I know
that one*, and then, in the same hour, eleven other people describing lives his account does not
cover. The man leaves with the affliction still on him and its dimensions corrected. That is not a
hypothetical about the fifth century. It happens on the Upper West Side on Thursday mornings.

Now the objection I find hardest, which is not an objection to the argument but to the wish behind it.

Perhaps Book VIII is one of the great things ever written **because** he had nowhere to put it.
Perhaps the pressure of having no peer is exactly what drove that quality of self-examination —
perhaps a man with a Tuesday night says the thing on Tuesday and does not need to spend four years
finding the sentence, and we lose the sentence. I think that is genuinely possible. I am not going to
pretend that a fellowship is straightforwardly better for literature than solitude and torment, when
the evidence of the shelf is so obviously against me. Grant the whole objection.

Grant it, and then notice who paid.

The cost of that book was not paid by the man who wrote it; he got the room he never had, in the form
of readers, and he has had it ever since. It was paid downstream, by people who were handed one
man's affliction as their diagnosis — and specifically by those for whom the diagnosis did not fit,
who were told what the deep truth about their own body was by a document that had never met them and
could not be corrected. A theory produced without a peer is a theory that has never once been told
*that part is just yours*, and it goes out into the world in that condition, and it is very durable.

## VII. The Objections

**"You are diagnosing dead men across sixteen centuries. It is cheap, and it is unfalsifiable."**

Nearly all of that lands and I'll take it. Retrospective diagnosis is a bad genre, the dead can't
answer, and an argument that requires me to know what was going on inside Augustine or Freud is an
argument I should lose.

So, the claim in the form that doesn't require it. **I am not diagnosing anyone.** The affliction is
not my inference; it is Augustine's own testimony, given at length, in public, under his own name, and
the least disputed thing about him. And the case against Freud rests on no
diagnosis at all — every fact in it is his own or his successors', on the record and in their own
words. What I am charging is not a condition. It is a **move**: the passage from *this is
what is true of me* to *this is what is true of everyone*. That move is on the page in both cases, in
their own published work, and it is the only thing I need.

**"Augustine was not wrong. The will is divided — universally. That is the doctrine, and it is true."**

The strongest objection in the set, and it deserves the most room, because it is correct. I conceded
it early on purpose, before it could be used against me: the divided will is not Augustine's private
trouble, it is Paul's report and the drunks' report and mine, and any account of human beings that
leaves it out is a false account. Augustine was right, and more than right — he saw further into the
mechanism than anyone had, and the tradition was correct to keep what he saw.

The overreach was never the diagnosis. **It was the location.**

Right that we are bound, wrong about where. What he did was take the site of *his* binding and make
it the site of everyone's, which is a different claim from the true one, and the difference is the whole subject
here. The binding is universal. The object never was. Mine is a pack of cigarettes and a turn-based
strategy game — and I have to be careful even there, because the moment I say *and yours is something
else*, I'm doing a smaller version of the same thing. Yours is yours, and naming it is not a service
I can perform for you. That is the point.

Which means the repair I am proposing is not a correction of Augustine from outside. It is a
subtraction of one word from what he found. The description of the bound will is unmatched; the
object was then named on the basis of the only case he had close access to. Keep the description.
Drop the object. What is left is truer than what he wrote and is entirely his.

**"You are doing exactly what you accuse them of — and not only about the program. *This* is a theory
of everyone. You have taken what happens in a room you personally attend and made it a general
account of how human beings go wrong. By your own standard, who corrected you?"**

The fairest hit in the set, and the second half of it is the part with teeth. I'm not immune to the
temptation: I've written two essays now in which a room I attend turns out to be the place where the
important thing is happening, which is precisely the shape of the error I am describing. Somebody
should say that to me and I'd rather say it first.

And the honest answer to *who corrected you* is: the room did, on the only point I'm actually
qualified to report. It told me the size of my own affliction. It did not tell me this thesis was
true, and nobody in that circle has read it. So the argument is owed no credit from the room. Take it
on its evidence or leave it.

And the program itself is more careful with the temptation than I've been. It bounds its first
sentence. It has never, in ninety years, told the world that the world is alcoholic. The same
Tradition that kept it from ruling on Augustine keeps it off every other outside question, and
another commits it to **attraction rather than promotion**.[^traditions] Its founding documents are a sustained refusal to become a theory of
everything — written, remarkably, by people who had just had the most important experience of their
lives and every incentive to generalize it.

The rooms are better at this than I am. That is a concession, not a defense, and I'll let it stand as
one.

**"Freud is a strawman. Nobody believes pansexualism anymore."**

The clinical half of that is simply true, and I concede it without reservation: drive theory isn't
the working model of any serious contemporary practice, the profession moved on decades ago, and a
version of this argument aimed at practicing analysts would be aimed at nobody.

But the theory went and the **grammar** stayed. It's now the ordinary speech of people who have never
read a page of him: that the real reason is always the hidden one; that it's usually about sex; that a
person's own account of themselves is the least reliable evidence available. Those three assumptions
are in the water of every conversation about motive any of us had this year, and they don't come from
the clinic. A theory can be abandoned entirely and its grammar still be the one everybody speaks.

[^traditions]: Traditions Ten and Eleven (short form), © AAWS, verbatim: "Alcoholics Anonymous has no
opinion on outside issues; hence the A.A. name ought never be drawn into public controversy," and
"Our public relations policy is based on attraction rather than promotion; we need always maintain
personal anonymity at the level of press, radio, and films." Both were written in the 1940s out of
hard experience, and both function to keep a private program from becoming a public doctrine.

## VIII. Not Yet

I do not know whether I will smoke again. The record is a dozen to nothing, and twice it has
gone two full years before turning over, which means the good stretches prove nothing and I have the
evidence for that in my own hand. I notice that I'd like to end on a sentence implying otherwise.
I'm not going to.

What I can report is the one change saying it out loud has made, which is smaller than the change I
wanted and is the only one on offer. **The affliction did not shrink. It stopped being everyone's.** It sits where it is, roughly the size of a pack of cigarettes and a summer, and it is
mine, and it is not a discovery about you, and I know that because there are people who would tell me
so.

The summer had exactly two things that reliably got me out of the apartment, and one of them was
groceries. The other was a dog who needed walking at a particular hour and did not care what turn I
was on. Not resolve — I didn't have any that summer; I had a schedule imposed from outside by a
creature with his own opinion about seven o'clock. I said something once about what it is to love a
dog, in [For the Love of Dogs](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/for-the-love-of-dogs), and this is not
that. It is only an observation: for weeks what interrupted the loop was not a decision but an
appointment with someone alive. And an interruption is not an ending. The walks got me down to the
street and back, every day of it, and the loop was waiting when I came up.

What ended it was this. Three weeks ago, in August, I turned my life over to the LORD. That is the
only honest account I have of why the games stopped. The games stopped, and I have not gone back.

And then I started smoking again. Not *still* — **again, and after.** I had been four months clean
when I turned my life over in August, and within days of that I was at a pool hall in Williamsburg,
and I wanted to talk to a friend, and my friend was outside smoking. So I went outside and I bummed
one, because that is what you do when you want to stand next to somebody. That was all it took. Two
weeks, and then I quit again, a few days ago, for the dozenth time.

The asymmetry stays unresolved, because resolving it is precisely the move
everything above has been objecting to. Something real happened to me in August; it took one thing
and did not take the other, and the other arrived days later on a sidewalk because I wanted company.
I do not know what to do with that, and the moment I explain it I will have a theory. And a theory
built out of one man's good month, handed to everyone, is the thing Augustine did and the thing Freud
did and the thing I've been asking you to watch for. I'm not going to tell
you that what worked on me will work on you. I don't know what has hold of you. It is not my
business, and it never was.

One more thing, and it is unresolved, which is why it comes last.

I'm following the Sermon on the Mount now, as literally as I can manage it. And I don't know how my
rent gets paid when the savings run out. That is not a rhetorical posture; it is a number, and the
number is going down. What the summer took from me was precisely the work of not being in this
position — the applications, the résumé, the public evidence of an engineer at this address — and
what has replaced that work is not, yet, that work. It is trust that the LORD will provide.

Which I'll say plainly and then decline to offer you as evidence of anything. I have just spent an
essay on what a man's account of himself is worth when nobody is positioned to correct it, and I'm
not going to exempt this account. It is not a finding. It is where I am standing, and the outcome is
not in.

*Not yet* is the addict's sentence. It was also Augustine's, and he wrote it more honestly than any
of us manage, and he wrote it in a book because there wasn't a circle of chairs to say it in. The same
words in two rooms — and the difference between the man who says them into a page and the man who says
them into a room is not holiness and it is not honesty. On both counts he outclasses me badly.

The difference is whether anyone was sitting close enough to say it back.
