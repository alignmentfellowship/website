*Pickle, in his thunder earmuffs.*

When I adopted Pickle, he was terrified of me. He'd been a stray for the first two
years of his life, and someone, at some point, had not been kind — you could read
it in him. The first time I tried to play fetch, he cowered: he thought I was
throwing the ball *at* him. He still flinches when I carry anything across the room,
bracing for it to land on him. Thunder and fireworks undo him, so he has earmuffs.
Actual dog earmuffs. I bought them.

The first time Pickle growled at me — I was trying to force him to do something — my
instinct was to intimidate him: show him I was bigger, let him know I would not
tolerate aggression. It terrified him. It confirmed the one thing he already
believed, that I was dangerous, and a dog that certain of danger can't learn
anything else. He was too overwhelmed with fear to obey his fear. It didn't even buy
me the obedience fear is supposed to buy.

So I did what the behavior books kept telling me: I stopped trying to frighten
him.[^1] When he first came to me he was a terror on the leash — growling and lunging
at every dog we passed. My instinct was to correct it hard: a sharp word, a yank, let
him know it wasn't allowed. Instead I filled my pockets with treats. When another dog
came up the path, I'd move us aside, put ten feet between us, and feed him one treat
after another while the other dog passed. I never scolded him for the aggression. I
set it up so the right thing was the easy thing, and paid him in love for it.
The growling thinned, and then it stopped.

I didn't stop being stern — I still hold clear boundaries. I just stopped trying to
intimidate him into keeping them. It was slow. It worked.

Nine years on, Pickle trusts me. It feels immeasurably better to be loved by a dog
than feared by one. I've had it both ways.

*Nine years in.*

---

Fear is not love, and it does not make love.

With some dogs, fear doesn't paralyze — it weaponizes. A beaten dog fears one hand
and bites all the rest. The compliance is real — they mind the one they fear — but
nothing in them was won over; they only learned where the danger was and arranged
themselves around it. Pickle broke the other way, into a fear too big to function.
Neither is love. Neither is what love makes.

There is usually no cruelty in it. The one who rules by fear mostly believes he's
doing right — I did. Trying to intimidate Pickle out of his fear felt like
leadership. It was a sincere mistake about how love works.

This isn't "never say no." I say *no* to Pickle every day — he'd still eat what's on
the counter, bolt into the road, swallow the one thing in the yard engineered to
kill him. Loving him is not letting him do whatever he wants; it's the opposite. The
line was never *whether* you say no. It's what the *no* is for — to keep them safe,
or to make them afraid of you. Same word. Opposite animal.

---

I didn't want Pickle to fear me — the thought was unbearable, my sweet boy flinching
when I reached for him. I wanted him to trust me.

And here is the part that undoes me. Pickle is certain he understands the world. He
does not. He is terrified of the vacuum and cannot be reasoned out of it. He is
convinced that his barking is the only thing keeping the mailman from murdering us
all — the proof being that it works, every single day. He is a small, confident
expert on a world he has entirely misread — and I love him more for it, not less. I
have started to suspect I am exactly this to whoever holds my leash. Sure I know what's going on.
Wrong about most of it. Pulled back, more than once, from a road I was certain was
fine. Loved anyway.

That is what I had backwards. I was afraid of the one who keeps me the way Pickle was afraid of me —
obeying to stay out of trouble, going cynical the moment I thought no one was
watching.

We've inherited an instruction to be afraid of whatever is larger than us — as if
reverence and dread were the same thing. They aren't. The old word for standing
before something vast and good meant awe: you go very still; you don't cower.[^2]

Being afraid of what loves you is exactly what Pickle did when I first brought him
home.[^3]

Here is the difference.

Fear says: do what I say, or you'll suffer.

Love says: do what I say, or you'll suffer — but not by my hand. My instructions are
the fence at the edge of the cliff, not the fist. Over the edge is the fall. I can
see the edge and you can't, and I am asking you to trust me because I love you.

And if there is a love over us at all, it can want no less from me than I wanted from
him. What I'm being taught, slowly, is the other thing: not to fear the master but to
trust them. To be a good boy — not scared of what happens if I'm not, but because
I've started to believe someone wants me to make it home.

When the first thunder comes, Pickle doesn't run from me — he runs to me. He finds me
wherever I am and leans into my leg until I stop. So I stop. I get the earmuffs I
bought him, make a place for him against my side, lay a blanket over him, and hold him
while the thunder goes on outside, indifferent to us both.

I can't call off the storm. His ears are better than mine; the sky is enormous and it
terrifies him, and I can't change that. What I can do is make a place at my side and
stay there until it passes.

For a long time I thought being good meant getting the storm to stop — being obedient
enough, afraid enough, that nothing would ever break. It doesn't work like that. The
thunder comes anyway. Being good, it turns out, is knowing where to go when it does:
not away, not into a corner to hide, but toward the one who loves you, to the place
already made at their side.

I still flinch. The sky still frightens me. But more and more, when it opens, I look
for my master instead of the corner — and I am learning there's a place
kept for me there. A blanket. An arm. And someone who won't leave me alone in it.

[^1]: The training book I followed was Karen Pryor's *Don't Shoot the Dog! The New
Art of Teaching and Training* — the reward-based approach I shifted to. Modern
veterinary-behavior guidance agrees: the American Veterinary Society of Animal
Behavior's *Position Statement on Humane Dog Training* (2021) holds that only
reward-based methods should be used, because aversive, punishment-based methods
raise the risk of fear, anxiety, and aggression and can damage a dog's relationship
with its owner. See also Gal Ziv, "The effects of using aversive training methods in
dogs—A review," *Journal of Veterinary Behavior* 19 (2017): 50–60, reviewing
seventeen studies to the same effect.

[^2]: The word behind "the fear of the LORD" (Proverbs 9:10) is Hebrew *yirah*
(Strong's H3374), which ranges from dread to reverent awe; in the wisdom literature
it stands parallel to the *knowledge* of God, reading as awe rather than terror.
Flattened to "fear" in English, it sounds harsher than the Hebrew.

[^3]: 1 John 4:18 (KJV): "There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out
fear: because fear hath torment." The Greek there is *phobos* (Strong's G5401) — a
word that *can* mean reverence, but here the tormenting, punitive fear that love
drives out, not the reverent awe of *yirah*. Cf. 4:8, "God is love."
