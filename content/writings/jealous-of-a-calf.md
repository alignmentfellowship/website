## I. The Residue

Isaiah watches a man make a god, and refuses to skip a single step of it. The man has a tree.
He cuts it down. He burns half of it — warms his hands at it, bakes his bread on it, roasts his
dinner over it, and says, with real satisfaction, *Aha, I am warm, I have seen the fire.* Then
he takes what is left. Not the heartwood, not the first cut: the residue, the offcut, the part
that would not fit in the stove. He carves it, stands it up, kneels down in front of it, and
prays. *Deliver me; for thou art my god.*[^1]

The prophet is not being unfair. He is being exact. That is what the competition looked like.

This is the part of the Old Testament that modern readers skim, because we assume the argument
is over and we won it. But it is the situation every sentence in the Law was spoken into, and
two of that Law's most famous claims cannot be read at all without it. When the God of Israel
says *thou shalt have no other gods before Me*, the other gods on offer are not rival
infinities. They are not competing accounts of the ground of being. They are, in the plainest
and most literal sense, objects — a bull cast out of jewelry, a pole in the ground, a length of
lumber that an hour ago was going to be firewood.

We don't read it that way, because two attributes of that God have become unsayable in
educated company. The first is that God is to be **feared**. The second is that God is
**jealous** — and the text does not soften it; it says the name is Jealous.[^2] Between them,
those two words have done more to make the God of the Old Testament sound like a bad husband
than every conquest narrative combined. A being who demands to be feared and cannot stand to be
shared: we know that man. We have met him. Some of us grew up in his house.

I am not going to soften either word. Word-softening is what has been done to this material for
a century, and it doesn't work; the reader can smell it, and the words come back the moment the
sermon ends. Instead, both of them go back in the room where they were said. Both turn out to
mean something other than what we hear, and — this is the part that surprised me — they turn out
to mean *the same thing*, approached from two sides.

And the room has a consequence I didn't expect when I started, which is most of why I am
writing this down. If the jealousy was aimed at a calf, then it was never aimed at anybody's
rival infinite — because nobody has one. There cannot be two. Which means the commandment we
have spent three thousand years using as a boundary between religions was never a boundary
between religions at all. The line it draws runs somewhere else, and it runs through the middle
of each of them, including mine.

## II. Two Fears in One Sentence

I should say where I am standing. I was raised without any of this — no church, no God-language,
nothing to lose my faith in. Jesus found me as an adult, late, and I can tell you exactly which
half of the inheritance went down easily. *God-fearing* needed no explaining: my first experience
of authority was a fearful one, so a God you had to watch out for was a shape I already knew the
feel of. *God is love* was the sentence that sounded like a slogan. That is the wound it
is written from. It is not the argument, and I will not lean on it again.

Here is the argument, and it starts with a concession, because the move I am about to make is
one you have every right to distrust.

The move is: *the original word didn't mean what you think — in the Hebrew it means awe.* You
have heard it from a pulpit. It is a favorite of the reassurance industry, and it is very often
a fraud. James Barr spent a career on exactly this, showing how theologians build doctrines out
of etymologies and then smuggle a word's entire lexical range into a single verse — the root
fallacy, and what he named illegitimate totality transfer.[^3] He was right. A word does not
mean everything it has ever meant, and a Hebrew dictionary is not a decoder ring for getting out
of a hard text.

So, precisely: *Yirah* doesn't secretly mean *awe*. It runs the whole range, terror to
reverence, exactly as the English word *fear* once did. In 1611 the reverence sense was
ordinary — ordinary enough that an English dictionary was still separating the reverent kind
from the frightened kind two hundred years later, which is the whole reason *God-fearing* was
ever a compliment.[^4] The King James translators were not wrong. **English drifted.** The word
narrowed under us until it meant only the flinch, and we kept reading the old sentence with the
new word in it.

Which would leave us where we started, if the text did not settle it itself. It does, and it
does it in one verse.

The people are at the mountain. There is thunder and fire and a sound like a trumpet, and they
are so frightened they beg Moses to take the message himself and spare them the voice. And Moses
answers them:

> Fear not: for God is come to prove you, and that [Their] fear may be before your faces, that ye
> sin not.[^5]

*Fear not* — and, in the same breath, *that [Their] fear may be before your faces.* Both words come
off the same Hebrew root. One is a verb he tells them to stop; one is a noun he wants left
standing. That is not a translator's fumble and it is not a modern rescue: it is the sentence
making a distinction, in the one place the distinction matters most, at the mountain, to people
who were already terrified. Whatever Moses is telling them to drop, and whatever he wants them
to keep, cannot be the same thing.

And you can find the difference elsewhere by usage, without touching an etymology. The wisdom
literature sets *the fear of the LORD* in parallel with *the knowledge of the holy* — the second
line glossing the first, which is what Hebrew poetry does.[^6] Deuteronomy names what is
required of Israel and puts fearing God and loving God inside a single sentence, as one
requirement.[^7] Note what that costs you if the word means dread. **You cannot command a
phobia.** You can command an orientation — a direction to face, a thing to hold steadily in
view — and you can command love, which is also not a feeling you can produce by deciding to. Put
those together and you have the shape of what is being asked: not a flinch, but attention that
does not look away.

The concession, and it belongs on the page rather than in a note.

There are texts where the fear is terror and no amount of lexicography touches them. Uzzah puts
out his hand to steady the ark when the oxen stumble — a reflex, a good one, the instinct of a
man who does not want the holy thing in the dirt — and he dies there beside it.[^8] I have no
reading of that which makes it comfortable, and I distrust everyone who does. It stays on the
page.

The claim I am making is smaller and firmer than the one that would explain Uzzah. It is this:
**the fear the Law commands is not the fear the Law tells you to drop.** Two things, one root,
distinguished in one sentence by the man who was standing there.

## III. Jealousy Is a Covenant Word

The other word is harder, because it does not sound like a mistranslation. It sounds like a
confession.

The Hebrew is *qanna'*, and its distribution is strange enough to be worth stating carefully. As
an adjective it is used of no one but God — five verses, six occurrences, and one of them is the
astonishing line in Exodus 34 where it is not a description but a name: *the LORD, whose name is
Jealous.*[^9] I want the precision here, because this is exactly where an essay like this
overclaims and gets knocked over: the *root* is ordinary, and it is used of human beings all
over the Hebrew Bible. It is the adjective, the fixed epithet, that belongs to God alone.

And the register it belongs to is not the throne room. It is the marriage law. The word's
ordinary home is the covenant between two people who have promised each other exclusively, and
the spirit that rises in one of them when the promise is in question.[^10] That is the frame the
text is reaching for when it applies the word to God — not a king guarding his prerogatives, but
a spouse.

Which brings up the distinction English has almost entirely lost. **Envy wants what another
person has. Jealousy guards what is already one's own.** They are not two intensities of the
same thing; they are aimed in opposite directions. Envy looks across at a rival and wants to
take his place. Jealousy looks at a bond it is already inside of and refuses to see it divided.
We collapsed them into one word somewhere along the way, and now every time the Old Testament
says *jealous* the modern ear hears *envious* — and hears, therefore, a God anxious about Their
standing among competitors.

Which matters, because the accusation people actually make — that this is a God anxious about
being outranked — is an accusation of *envy*. And envy is the word the text did not use.

I don't think that finishes it. A covenant word is still a possessive one, and *I will not
share you* is a sentence that has been used to justify a great deal of harm by people who had
no business saying it. Naming the philology does not dissolve that. What dissolves it is a
different question, and it is a historical one:

**Who, exactly, was the rival?**

## IV. There Were No Rivals

The scene is famous enough that we have stopped looking at it. Moses is up the mountain too
long. The people ask Aaron for gods to go before them. Aaron tells them to bring the gold, and
they do, and he casts a calf, and they say: *These be thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up
out of the land of Egypt.*[^11]

Two hundred years or so later, a king does it deliberately. Jeroboam, needing his new northern
kingdom to stop walking to Jerusalem to worship, makes two calves of gold, puts one in Bethel
and one in Dan, and says — the same sentence, almost word for word — *behold thy gods, O Israel,
which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt.*[^12] So this is not a panic in a desert. It is
a standing institution, with royal backing and a functioning priesthood, and it lasted.

Now, the honest scholarship, because it cuts against the cheap version of my point and I would
rather run it myself than have it run at me.

The people who made these calves weren't simpletons who thought a cow made the world. Across
the ancient Near East, gods were routinely depicted **standing on** a bull — the animal serving
as the deity's mount or pedestal, the way a throne serves a king. Baal appears astride the bull
in Aramean reliefs; at Ugarit the high god El carries the epithet *Bull*. On that reading the
calf at Sinai was not the god at all. It was the pedestal for a god who remained invisible, and
the sin was not zoolatry but a breach of the ban on images.[^13]

Some scholars accept that reading; others answer, reasonably, that in actual ancient practice
the line between a god and its image blurred almost immediately, which is why every prophet who
attacks idolatry attacks it as though the statue were the point — because for the man kneeling,
it was.

And here is why I am glad to concede the sophisticated reading: **it makes the situation worse,
not better.** Take it at its most refined, and the alternative to the God of Israel was
*furniture for a local weather-god* — a piece of temple equipment representing a being who ruled
a region, could be relocated, and had colleagues. Take it at its most ordinary, and it was the
furniture itself. Either way, nothing in that room was infinite. Nothing in that room made
anything.

The prophets say so, and they are funnier about it than we are. Elijah, on Carmel, watching four
hundred and fifty men shout at an altar all morning, waits until noon and then offers
suggestions: shout louder, he says, maybe he is talking, or busy, or on a journey — *or
peradventure he sleepeth, and must be awaked.*[^14] The joke only works because Baal was the
kind of god who might, in fact, be asleep. It wouldn't land against the God who has no
competitor to be preferred over.

One more piece of the room, and it is the one that keeps this from being an essay about
foreigners. On storejars at a desert waystation called Kuntillet 'Ajrud, dated to around 800
BCE, someone wrote out blessings by *YHWH of Samaria and his asherah.*[^15] That is Israelite
handwriting, invoking the God of Israel by name, paired with something else. A second one on the
same site blesses by *YHWH of Teman and his asherah* and then finishes *may he bless you and watch
over you* — which is the priestly blessing out of Numbers, word for word, with the addition
carried along inside it. Whatever the exact meaning of that pairing — the scholarship argues about
it — the practical situation is not in doubt, and the prophets describe it constantly: the covenant kept alongside a second
arrangement, insurance taken out quietly, the name retained and the exclusivity dropped.

So the jealousy language is not aimed at strangers who never knew better. It is aimed at people
who believed they were keeping the covenant.

And that is what dissolves the insecurity reading. **Jealousy of a peer is insecurity. There was
no peer.** A God who has to worry about being displaced by a mount for a storm-god is not the
God who had just emptied Egypt; the anxiety would be incoherent. Whatever the
jealousy is protecting, it cannot be a position that was never under threat.

And once you have seen that, a second thing follows that is harder to say in public, because
the same reasoning does not stop at the desert.

## V. There Cannot Be Two

We now use *thou shalt have no other gods before Me* as a boundary marker between religions —
my God against your God, the jealousy being what makes the border hostile. That reading is
unavailable, and the reason is not tolerance. It is arithmetic.

**There cannot be two infinites.** Two would have to differ, and to differ is for one to have
something the other lacks — a place where one stops and the other keeps going. That place is a
limit, and a limited infinite is not one. This is not a modern ecumenical convenience; it is the
standard scholastic proof of the unity of God, and Aquinas runs it straight off the infinity of
the divine perfection: if there were many, they would have to differ, and to differ is to be
missing something.[^unity] The same argument is the spine of *tawhid* in Islam and of the Shema
in Judaism.

So when a Christian and a Muslim argue about God, whatever they are doing, they are not arguing
about two infinites. There is only one who answers to that description, and both of them are
pointed at Them.

In the Muslim case the word gives it away before the argument starts. **Allah is not the proper
name of a rival deity.** It is the Arabic word for God — *al-ilāh*, "the god" — cognate with the
Hebrew *Eloah* and with the Aramaic *Alaha* that Jesus Himself would have used for God in His own
language.[^allah] Arabic-speaking Christians used it before Islam existed and use it today; an
Arabic Bible opens with *Allah* creating the heavens and the earth. Whatever else is in dispute
between the two religions, at the level of the word the "different God" question is a question
about a translation.

And the Qur'an makes the claim itself, in the specific case. Addressing Jews and Christians — the
People of the Book — it instructs believers to dispute with them only in the better way, and to
say: *We believe in that which hath been revealed unto us and revealed unto you; our Allah and
your Allah is One, and unto Him we surrender.*[^ankabut] The word standing in that sentence where
an English Bible would put *God* is the word the last paragraph was about.

The commandment is there too, in the same shape. The gravest sin in Islam is *shirk* —
associating a partner with God, setting anything at all alongside the One.[^shirk] Change the
vocabulary and you are standing at Sinai. And the most concentrated statement of the doctrine in
the Qur'an would not have been out of place at the mountain: *Say: He is Allah, the One!… He
begetteth not nor was begotten. And there is none comparable unto Him*[^ikhlas] — with, elsewhere,
the phrase that has anchored a thousand years of Muslim negative theology: *naught is as His
likeness.*[^shura] That is Maimonides' sentence in another accent.

Now the harder case, because it is further away and the vocabulary genuinely differs.

When the Upanishads reach for the reality underneath everything, they decline to describe it. The
refusal has a name — *neti neti*, which Müller renders bluntly as *"No, no"* — and it is applied
to every predicate that offers itself, on the grounds that each one would be a limit on One who
has none. What is
left is *nirguna* Brahman: without attributes, ungraspable, not to be had by any description that
would make it one item among items.[^neti] A reader who has just come through Maimonides — every
positive statement about God is already an error, only the negations hold — will recognize the
operation exactly. Two traditions with no meaningful contact, running the same method, for the
same reason: not because anyone agreed to, but because of what the infinite is. And the
tradition's oldest text carries the line most often quoted for exactly this — *ekaṃ sad viprā
bahudhā vadanti*, which Griffith renders *"To what is One, sages give many a title."*[^ekam] Read
the whole verse rather than the fragment and it settles its own meaning: the titles it goes on to
list are Indra, Mitra, Varuṇa, Agni, Yama, Mātariśvan. It is a statement about **names**, which is
the whole of what I want it for. Its later career as a charter for religious pluralism is a
development of the modern schools, and I am not resting anything on that.

Which raises the objection a Western reader has been holding since that paragraph started: yes,
but they have *statues*. Rooms of them. Whatever the philosophers say, the practice is images,
and images are what the commandment was about.

The tradition's own answer deserves to be heard before it is dismissed. The form is not held to
**be** the infinite. It is held to be a form the formless takes for the sake of someone who has to
love with a human heart and cannot love an abstraction — *saguna* standing in front of *nirguna*,
a face lent to the faceless.[^murti] The devotional traditions are built on precisely that: God as
a **You** to be adored rather than an *it* to be dissolved into — which is the argument of
[*Krishna Is Not Christ*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/krishna-is-not-christ).

I don't think it settles the question, and I'm not going to pretend it does. Whether a form
confessed to be a form protects you — or whether it is exactly the arrangement Sinai forbade, the
right name on the wrong object — is a live question, and I am leaving it open. It is also, and
this is the part worth noticing, **an argument Hinduism has been having with itself for more than
a thousand years**, formless against formed, and it didn't need Sinai to start it.

## VI. Same God, Different Accounts

None of which makes the traditions the same, and the version of this argument that says so is
worth nothing.

The disagreements are not decorative. Judaism does not grant that God became a man, and from
inside the Shema the Christian claim that God did is precisely the thing in question. Islam is
more specific still: the Qur'an denies the sonship in as many words, warns Christians against
saying "Three," and denies the crucifixion outright — *they slew him not nor crucified him, but
it appeared so unto them.*[^nisa] I hold that the crucifixion happened and that it is the center
of everything. Those two sentences cannot both be true. Somebody is wrong about a Friday outside
Jerusalem, and that is not a difference of emphasis.

Now notice where every one of those disagreements sits. Not one of them is about the infinite.
They are about **what that God has done** — whether They took a body, whether the body was Their
Son, whether He died on a Friday afternoon. Every live dispute between these traditions is a
dispute about the Son. Not one is a dispute about the Father. Ask instead *who* is being
disagreed about — who the Shema confesses, who *tawhid* insists on, who *neti neti* refuses to
describe — and the argument has nothing to work with, because there is nobody else it could be.
There is only one infinite, and the arguing parties are all facing it.

That is not a truce and it costs nothing. The Christological disagreements stay exactly as sharp
as they were a paragraph ago; I have not moved them an inch. They are simply not disagreements
about which God, and they never were.

A reader who has been with me a while will want to put an essay of mine in the way here, one
called [*Krishna Is Not Christ*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/krishna-is-not-christ), and it
is the right objection to raise. But look at what that
essay refuses and what it grants. It refuses the identity of the two **figures**: the names,
which are not cognate, and a devotion organized around a God who wins set next to one organized
around a God who bleeds. What it grants, in its own last sentence, is the referent — Someone had
been sung to all along, in a language the singer couldn't speak, and was recognized at the end.
The refusal is about the Son. The grant is about the Father. I was making this distinction before
I had a name for it.

So here is the distinction the whole thing rests on, and it is not a compromise: **a disagreement
about what is true of someone is not a disagreement about who is being discussed.** It is nearly
the opposite. Contradiction requires a shared subject. Two people who describe one person
incompatibly are, by that very fact, talking about one person; if they were describing two
different people there would be no contradiction at all — just two accounts sitting quietly side
by side, with nothing to argue about. The heat between these traditions is evidence of a common
referent, not evidence against it.

Which puts both of the usual moves out of reach. The Christian who says Muslims worship a
different god has made a claim the arithmetic does not allow. The person who says the differences
don't matter has made a claim the texts do not allow. Both are ways of not having the
conversation: the first refuses the shared subject, the second refuses the disagreement.

And now the commandment can be put where it actually goes. **It does not run between the
traditions. It runs through each of them.**

Every one of them holds both things at once. Islam has *tawhid*, and has in every century people
its own scholars accuse of *shirk*. Judaism has the Shema, and had, demonstrably, Israelites
blessing by YHWH and his asherah at a desert waystation. Hinduism has *neti neti*, and has the
image argument I just declined to settle. And Christianity — the tradition I am inside, and
therefore the one I have any standing to be rude about — has the whole apophatic lineage, and also
a mass-produced portrait of a mild Northern European man that hangs in ten thousand halls and is,
for a very large number of people, simply what God looks like.[^portrait]

So the question the second commandment puts to you was never *is your neighbor's God the wrong
one.* It is a worse question than that, and it is addressed to the reader rather than to the
neighbor: **is the thing you are actually kneeling in front of infinite — or is it the
residue?**

Which means the text still owes us a reason. If the jealousy is not protecting a position, and
was never a border, it is protecting something. It gives one.

## VII. What the Harm Actually Is

Psalm 115 walks the length of an idol and inventories it, part by part, the way you would
inventory a body at an autopsy. Silver and gold, the work of men's hands. A mouth that does not
speak. Eyes that do not see. Ears that do not hear, a nose that does not smell, hands that do
not handle, feet that do not walk. It is a catalogue of absences.

Then the psalm turns, and the turn isn't what a modern reader expects:

> They that make them are like unto them; so is every one that trusteth in them.[^16]

That is the indictment. Not *God is offended.* Not *the Almighty will not stand for it.* The
charge is about **what happens to the worshipper.** You become like what you worship. Kneel to
something with no eyes and your own sight narrows; give your life to something that cannot
answer and you'll slowly become someone who doesn't expect an answer. The psalm says it as a
flat statement of consequence, the way you would warn someone about a chemical.

Which is precisely why a lover's word is the right word for it. Jealousy in the covenant sense
is not concern for one's own standing; it is refusal to watch the other person be diminished by
something that cannot love them back. If God is not in danger of being displaced — and nothing
in that room could displace anything — then the jealousy is not on Their own behalf. **It is on
yours.** The objection to the calf is not the insult. It is what the calf will do to the face of
the person kneeling in front of it.

The tradition noticed this a long time ago. Maimonides, reading the whole of the Law and the
Prophets, points out that the vocabulary of burning anger, provocation, and jealousy is applied
to God in one connection only: idolatry. Nowhere else. And then he presses it somewhere no
modern preacher would follow him — arguing that a person who believes God has a body provokes that jealousy *more* than an idolater does, because they have made the statue on the inside, where
no commandment can reach it.[^17]

Whatever that is, it is not a deity defending a threatened standing. It is closer to a physician's
severity: an insistence, sounding harsh from outside, about what a false picture does to the
mind holding it. (The larger case about pictures of God belongs to the companion essay
[*They/Them*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/theythem).)

## VIII. Allegory Is the Oldest Reading, Not the Escape Hatch

At this point somebody is entitled to object that I am doing what religious people always do
with the embarrassing parts: turning a story about a cow into a metaphor about *the human
heart*, and thereby getting to keep a text I no longer believe.

It's a fair suspicion, so here is where the allegorical reading of this particular story
comes from. It's not from the nineteenth century, and not from a seminary trying to survive
Darwin.

It is from Paul, who does it to this exact story, in writing, within a generation.

He is capable of stating the method outright — *which things are an allegory*, he says of Hagar
and Sarah, and then reads them as the two covenants.[^18] But it is in the letter to Corinth
that he does it to the calf. He walks the congregation through the exodus — the cloud, the sea,
the water from the rock — and then says: *Neither be ye idolaters, as were some of them; as it
is written, The people sat down to eat and drink, and rose up to play.* That last line is a
verbatim quotation of the golden calf feast. And Paul's reason for citing it is explicit: these
things were written for our admonition.[^19] Not *they did that*. **We are in it.**

So the allegorical reading of the golden calf is not a liberal softening of the Old Testament.
It is the New Testament's own handling of it, performed by the apostle least willing to give
ground on anything.

The lineage after him is continuous and mainstream. Origen distinguishes the senses of scripture
and reads for the spiritual one as a matter of course. Gregory of Nyssa writes an entire book on
Moses built in two explicit halves — first the *historia*, the story as told, and then the
*theōria*, the contemplation of what it means for a soul; the calf is in there, ground to powder
and drunk.[^20] Augustine, who is nobody's idea of a loose reader, states the rule flatly:
whatever in scripture cannot, taken literally, be referred to purity of life or soundness of
doctrine is to be taken as figurative.[^21] The medieval schools formalized it into four senses
and taught it as ordinary competence.[^22]

But notice why Augustine had to write a rule down. Allegory is dangerous. It can make a text say
anything, and in the hands of people who wanted it to, it has. A method that can dissolve any
sentence you dislike is not a method; it is an appetite.

So here is the limit I am accepting, and I would like to be held to it: **allegory that keeps the
plain sense and adds to it is reading. Allegory that deletes the plain sense to spare our
feelings is editing.** I'm not proposing that the calf is a symbol and never happened to
anybody. I am proposing the opposite: that it happened, that it kept happening, that a king
later institutionalized it — and that the reason it was written down is that we are in it too.
The story is not dissolved. We are enrolled.

## IX. Keeping the Name and Swapping the Object

Read it that way and three details in Exodus 32 stop being scenery. None of them requires an
allegorical key. They are all plain sense; they just do the work by themselves.

**First: the gold was already theirs.** Aaron doesn't send anyone to a mine. He says, *Break off
the golden earrings, which are in the ears of your wives, of your sons, and of your daughters* —
and they do, off their own ears, and bring it to him.[^23] That gold has a history. It is the
gold they walked out of Egypt wearing, the payment for four hundred years. The idol is made
entirely out of what they were given on the way out. Nobody in this story acquires anything to
build it. They spend what they were handed.

**Second: they attached the right story to the wrong object.** *These be thy gods, O Israel,
which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt.* Look at the sentence. The exodus is true. It
happened, and it happened to them, and they are correctly naming it as the central fact of their
lives. Every word of the confession is accurate except the object it points at. They did not
lose the story. They kept the story, in full, in the right words, and aimed it at a statue.

**Third — and this is the one that reorganized the essay for me — Aaron proclaims a feast to the
LORD.** By name. Having built the calf, having watched the people hail it, he builds an altar in
front of it and announces a festival *to the LORD*, using the covenant name of the God who
brought them out.[^24]

Which means the golden calf is not the story of a people leaving their God for a different one.
There is no defection in it. Nobody converts. They keep the name, the story, the festival, the
altar, and the priest — and swap the object.

That is the only version of idolatry that was ever actually available to anybody, then or now.
The Israelite at Kuntillet 'Ajrud did not renounce YHWH; he blessed by YHWH and added something.
Jeroboam did not found a new religion; he kept the exodus sentence and moved the furniture
closer. Nobody wakes up and decides to worship a cow. What happens is smaller and much more
familiar: the thing you were promised is slow, and the invisible is difficult, and there is
something right here that can be seen, and touched, and improved, and carried at the front of
the column — and the name stays on it the whole time.

I will not give you a list of contemporary idols. The list is the sermon, and the sermon is how
this material gets wasted; you would recognize your own items and file them under *other people*
anyway, which is what lists are for. The story is more precise than a list. What it names is not
a set of objects but a preference — for a god who can be **managed**. Something with a location.
Something you can carry, feed, improve, and be reassured by on the days when reassurance is what
you came for. The calf's appeal was never that it was divine. It was that it was *there*, and it
was *portable*, and it did exactly what you would expect an object to do, which is nothing.

And this is where the first word comes back, because the two commandments turn out to be one
instruction seen from two sides.

**You do not stand in awe of something you can manage.** *Yirah* — the fear that is attention
you cannot look away from, the thing Moses wanted left standing after the terror was told to sit
down — is the one response a calf can never earn. It is not that the calf is forbidden *and*
separately God is to be feared. It is that where the awe is real there is no calf, because
nothing you could cast out of your own jewelry could produce it. And where there is a calf, a
fear does remain — the other one, the one Moses said to drop. The fear that has torment. Because
an object you have made load-bearing has to be kept fed, and defended, and can be lost.

(That the criterion has always been the will done rather than the name said is the argument of
[*The Sheep in the Basement*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-sheep-in-the-basement) and
[*In the Name*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/in-the-name-the-ambassador); here it is enough to
notice that Aaron said the name perfectly.)

Which is the whole of it, and it is smaller and stranger than the tyrant we made of it. A jealous
God was never jealous of another infinite; there is not one to be jealous of. Not of Mecca, and
not of Vrindavan. Jealous of a calf.

## X. The Objections

Six, in the strongest form I can give them.

**"This is a word study used as a dodge."** Partly true, and I would rather concede it than
defend it: the *original word really means* move is a standard evasion and Barr was right to
name it. That is why the argument here does not run on etymology at all. It runs on usage — two
forms of one root in one sentence at Sinai, giving opposite instructions; a poetic parallel that
glosses fear with *the knowledge of the holy*; a legal sentence that asks for fear and love as
one thing. And it is why Uzzah is still on the page, unexplained. A dodge would have taken him
off it.

**"The historical framing is condescending — *primitive people worshipped cows.*"** It would be,
and the objection has teeth, but the text got there first and refused it. Scripture does not
record the calf as something foreigners did. It records it as something the covenant people did,
at the mountain, six weeks after the deliverance, with God's own name proclaimed over the altar
— and then again, deliberately, with a king's authority and a functioning clergy. And the best
scholarship on the pedestal reading says these were not credulous people; the object was
theologically sophisticated. Which is not a defense of them. It is the removal of our last
excuse for reading it as somebody else's problem.

**"You have laundered a violent book into a kind one."** This is the hard one, and I am not going
to answer it, because I cannot. There are texts in the Old Testament — commanded slaughter, the
ban, cities put to the sword — that no reading of *yirah* touches and that I do not
address. I am making a claim about two words in a specific setting, not issuing an acquittal for
a library. If you came here for the whole ledger to be balanced, it isn't, and I would be lying
to tell you the fear question settles the rest. It doesn't. It settles the fear question.

**"This is perennialism with extra steps — you have flattened three religions to be
agreeable."** It would be, if the claim were that the differences are surface. It isn't, and I
put the sharpest of them on the page in the Qur'an's own words rather than in a paraphrase that
would have gone down easier. Perennialism says the traditions teach the same thing in different
idioms. I'm saying something more specific and a good deal less comfortable: that the identity
holds at one level and the disagreements sit at another. There is one infinite and they are all
addressing Them — that part really is flat, and I'm not going to dress it up. What they cannot
agree on is what that God has done: whether They took a body, whose Son He was, what happened on
a Friday outside Jerusalem. Somebody is wrong about that Friday. It is not a difference of idiom,
I haven't dissolved it, and it is not a disagreement about which God is being discussed.

**"You quoted one verse of the Qur'an and ignored the rest."** I quoted the verse that makes the
claim and the verses that cut against my own tradition, which is the right ratio if either. But
the real form of this objection is better than that: I'm not a Muslim,
and a Muslim reader is entitled to be wary of a Christian arriving with *tawhid* in hand to
settle a Christian argument. Fair. So the load I am putting on it is deliberately minimal, and it
is not in dispute among Muslim theologians of any school: God is one, has no partner, and nothing
whatever is like Them. I need nothing further, and the same courtesy is owed in the other
direction.

**"Allegory lets you keep the text by not believing it."** It can, and that is why I named the
limit: keeping the plain sense and adding to it is reading; deleting the plain sense to spare
our feelings is editing. By that standard I have added nothing to Exodus 32. Every detail I
leaned on — the earrings, the sentence, the feast proclaimed in God's name — is in the plain
text, sitting there in the narrative, doing nothing but waiting for somebody to notice that it
is a description of us.

## XI. Fear Not, and Let His Fear Be Before You

The New Testament makes the same split the mountain made, in a different language and without
appealing to the Hebrew at all: *there is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear:
because fear hath torment.*[^25] The fear with torment in it — that one goes. The letter is not
quietly retiring the *yirah* of the mountain; it's doing in Greek what Moses did in Hebrew,
which is to distinguish two things that English has one word for.

So we are left with the sentence we started with, and it is the whole instruction rather than a
contradiction in it: *Fear not* — *and that [Their] fear may be before your faces.*

What is left after the torment is taken away is not comfort, and I wouldn't sell it as comfort.
Standing in front of Someone who is actually infinite doesn't become cozy with familiarity. It becomes
something more useful than cozy. It becomes **unmanageable** — no location, no dimensions,
nothing to carry at the front of the column, nothing you could improve, nothing that fits in a
stove. Which is, when you look at it, the only condition under which you were never going to
build it out of your own jewelry.

The calf never asked anything of anybody. That is what made it so easy to love.
