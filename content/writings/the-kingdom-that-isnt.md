## I. The Accusation

People accused Jesus of working for the other side more than once, and mostly it was an insult
thrown in an argument — *thou hast a devil*, said the way you would say *you are out of your
mind.* Once, though, the charge arrived with a mechanism attached, and that is the one He
answered with an argument instead of a shrug.

A man is brought to Him who cannot see and cannot speak, and he leaves able to do both. The
crowd is doing what crowds do — amazed, asking out loud whether this is the son of David — and
some of the religious professionals standing at the edge of it supply the explanation.
*This fellow doth not cast out devils, but by Beelzebub the prince of the devils.*[^beelzebul]

Those men are Jewish, and so is He. What follows is several thousand words of hard sayings, and
every one of them is a Jewish teacher arguing with other Jewish teachers about an inheritance
they hold in common. His disciples are Jewish. The tradition all of them are fighting over is
Jewish. What has been done with these arguments since — lifting them out of a family and turning them into a
verdict on a people — is the worst thing this material has ever been made to do. When the
fiercest of them turns up later, that is the room it was said in.

Sit with what that charge assumes, because the assumption is the subject here. It assumes
a board with two sides on it. It assumes there is a dark power with a name and a rank — *prince*
— and a payroll, and that the interesting question about any given miracle is which of the two
offices authorized it. The accusers aren't skeptics; they believe something happened. Having watched a man who could not speak begin speaking, they didn't dispute it for a
second — they filed it under the other jurisdiction.

Now the answer, which most of us have never actually heard. He does not say
*no, I am on the good side.* He does not produce credentials. He says this:

> *Every kingdom divided against itself is brought to desolation; and every city or house
> divided against itself shall not stand.*[^divided]

And then, one sentence later, the question everything after this lives inside:

> *If Satan cast out Satan, he is divided against himself; how shall then his kingdom stand?*

He was handed a two-sided board and He would not sit down at it. He did not claim the light
square. He asked what happens to a kingdom that fights itself, and left the men who had drawn
the board holding it.

I want to take that seriously, because I have argued three times now, in print, that there is no
second power anywhere in reality — and I have never once put the claim in front of the evidence
that most obviously contradicts it.

[^beelzebul]: Matthew 12:22–24 (KJV); parallels at Mark 3:22 and Luke 11:14–15. The three accounts
differ in who brings the charge: Matthew has the Pharisees, Mark has scribes come down from
Jerusalem, Luke only "some of them." The man in Matthew is blind and dumb; in Luke, dumb alone.

[^divided]: Matthew 12:25–26 (KJV); Mark 3:24–26; Luke 11:17–18.

## II. The Room There Isn't

The claim I have made three times is that the God the tradition actually confesses leaves no
space for a rival, and that the space we imagine for one is the space we invented at a tree.

Those arguments are made and published, and a fourth telling would be a reprise rather than an
essay. In one paragraph each:

The first is arithmetic, and it belongs to the schoolmen rather than to me. **There cannot be
two infinites.** Two would have to differ, and to differ is for one of them to have something the
other lacks — a place where one stops and the other keeps going. That place is a limit, and a
limited infinite is not one. Aquinas runs the argument straight off the infinity of the divine
perfection, and the same reasoning is the spine of *tawhid* in Islam and of the Shema in
Judaism.[^unity] I spent a movement of [*Jealous of a
Calf*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/jealous-of-a-calf) on it, in order to say that a jealous
God was never jealous of another infinite, because there is not one to be jealous of.

The second is the two words the tradition has repeated so long they have gone smooth. *All-powerful*
and *all-present.* Take them at full weight and they do not describe a very large being standing
beside a world; they describe a reality with no *beside.* If God's power is total then there is no
power that is not God's, and the moment you grant one thing a single native watt of its own you
have made God large and no longer all-powerful. If there is nowhere God is not, then there is no
empty shelf to set a not-God on. That is the argument of [*The Novelist
DM*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-novelist-dm), and it's why I can't have a second power
without giving up a word I say every week.

The third is the oldest and the strangest. [*The Knowledge of Good and
Evil*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-knowledge-of-good-and-evil) argues that the fruit at
the center of the garden was not wickedness but a *worldview* — that the knowledge of good and
evil is the knowledge of two powers, the conviction that reality is split down the middle into a
good force and a bad one with each of us enlisted at birth. On that reading the belief in a
cosmic adversary is not our defense against the fall. It is the fall, still running.

Three arguments, three directions, one conclusion. And there is an obvious objection to all
three, which I have answered exactly once, in a single sentence, and only out of the Hebrew
Bible: that the adversary in Job is not an independent kingdom but a member of the divine court,
on a leash.[^job]

That sentence is true and it isn't enough, because the objection isn't really about Job. The
objection is that the man at the center of this whole tradition spent His public life casting
demons out of people, talked about the devil in what sounds like operational detail, and told a
story about binding a strong man before you can rob his house. If there is no second power,
somebody has to account for that.

So let us count it.

[^unity]: Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I.11.3, which gives three arguments for the unity
of God; this is the second, and it runs off the infinite perfection of the divine being — if
there were many they would have to differ, and the difference would be either a privation or a
perfection, so one of them would lack something either way. The first argument runs from
simplicity and the third from the unity of the world; neither is used here.

[^job]: Job 1–2; Zechariah 3. In every occurrence in both books the Hebrew is *ha-satan* —
"**the** satan" — with the definite article, which makes it a role rather than a proper name:
the accuser, a functionary of the divine court. English versions almost universally print
"Satan," as though it were a name, and the King James is among them; the article is the thing
that disappears in translation.

## III. What Counting Can and Cannot Do

Here is the instrument, and I'd rather hand it over with its defects showing than present a
number and hope.

I took the four gospels in a text that marks the words of Jesus and tags every word with its
Greek lemma, and I counted by lemma rather than by English word — which matters more than it
sounds like it does, because the King James renders three different Greek words as *hell*, and a
count run on the English cannot see that. The body here quotes the King James, which is the house
translation; the counting ran on the tagged text.[^method]

The figures. Jesus speaks about thirty-nine thousand words across the four gospels, in a little
under two thousand verses. The vocabulary of demons — *daimonion*, *Beelzeboul*, *unclean
spirit* — appears in **seventeen** of them. Satan and the devil appear in **sixteen**. The entire
vocabulary of hell — *gehenna*, *hades*, the punishment, the worm, the weeping and gnashing of
teeth — appears in **twenty-four**, or twenty-seven if you're generous about what counts as a
furnace. Against that, the words He actually spends His time on: *kingdom* in a hundred verses,
*Father* in nearly two hundred, and the whole warm end of the vocabulary — love, kingdom, Father,
forgiveness, mercy, life, blessed — in something over four hundred. Call it eighteen to one.

Four things a count like this cannot do.

**Red letter is an editorial judgment, not a manuscript fact.** No Greek manuscript marks the
words of Jesus. Where a gospel's narration blends into His speech, careful editors disagree —
the seams in the third chapter of John are the standing example — and my count inherits somebody
else's calls about where the quotation marks go.

**A verse is not a unit of weight.** The judgment of the sheep and the goats is one row in my
tally and a whole scene on the page. The rich man and Lazarus is one row and an entire parable.
Nobody reads by the verse and nothing important was ever settled by counting them.

**Rarity is not unimportance,** and this is the one that could sink the essay if I let it pass.
Jesus never once says *God is love.* That sentence is in the first letter of John, and no honest
person concludes from its absence that He did not mean it. A doctrine's weight is not its word
frequency, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you a trick.

**And a count like this can be wrong where nobody can see it.** Miss one word — one of the two
or three the gospels use for the same thing — and a figure comes out short while looking exactly
as confident as a right one. There's no defense against that except leaving the workings open,
so the text, the terms and the arithmetic are all recoverable, and anybody who runs it again and
gets a different number is owed a hearing.

So what is a count good for, if not for weighing? This: it can tell you what a word was **used
for.** Not how much it mattered — what job it was doing in the sentence, and who was standing
there when it was said. That is a question about aim, and aim is checkable.

[^method]: The count ran on the Berean Standard Bible in USX, enriched with words-of-Jesus
markup and Strong's numbers. The King James renders *geenna*, *hadēs* and (outside the gospels)
*tartaroō* alike as *hell*: *hadēs* is *hell* at Matthew 11:23 and 16:18, Luke 10:15 and 16:23
and elsewhere, the notable exception being 1 Corinthians 15:55, where it has *grave*.

## IV. What He Actually Does With a Demon

Seventeen verses, and the argument is in how they sort.

Six of them are Jesus rebutting the charge that He is demon-powered — the scene we opened on, in
its three tellings. Two more report that people said the same thing about John the Baptist, who
neither ate nor drank, and *they say, He hath a devil.*[^john] One is Jesus flatly denying it of
Himself: *I have not a devil; but I honour My Father.*[^honour] Three commission the work or
report it as done. One announces a girl released, to her mother, in a sentence with no
metaphysics in it at all: *the devil is gone out of thy daughter.*[^daughter] One is a command,
addressed to the spirit and not about it. Two are a single saying told in two gospels. And one —
the strangest of the seventeen — is the scene where people who cast out demons in His name are
the ones He does not recognize.[^lordlord]

Add them up by what the sentences are *doing* and a shape appears. **More than half of the
demon-talk in the mouth of Jesus is defensive.** It is Him, or somebody He
loves, being accused of demonic power and saying no. The gospels' demonology, in His own words,
is mostly a man batting away a slander.

And then the part that is not defensive is not a warning either. Not one of the seventeen tells
anybody to be afraid of a demon. Not one describes their organization, their ranks, their
methods, or their aims. There is no verse where He briefs the disciples on the enemy. The two
that carry a positive claim carry the same one, and it is not about the demon:

> *But if I with the finger of God cast out devils, no doubt the kingdom of God is come upon
> you.*[^finger]

The demon is not the subject of that sentence. The demon is the occasion — the thing whose
removal is evidence of something else, and the something else is the whole message. He isn't
saying *look what I can do to them.* He is saying *look what has arrived.*

The hinge of the whole argument is four verses long.

He sends out seventy people, and they come back thrilled — thrilled about exactly what you'd
expect: *LORD, even the devils are subject unto us through thy name.* They've been out in the
field and they have won. He answers them first with the widest promise
anybody makes in the gospels — *nothing shall by any means hurt you* — and then, in the very next
breath, He takes the victory away from them as a thing to care about:

> *Notwithstanding in this rejoice not, that the spirits are subject unto you; but rather
> rejoice, because your names are written in heaven.*[^rejoicenot]

So: they came back having won the war, and He told them the war isn't the thing.

There's a version of that sentence that is merely modest — *don't get a big head about it* — and
that reading is available and I can't refute it from the Greek.
But look at the shape. Every element of a two-powers cosmology is on the table in that scene:
authority, an enemy, a campaign, a victory report. And the answer redirects off all of it, onto
something with no adversary in it at all. If you were building a movement around a cosmic war,
this is the moment you consolidate. He does the opposite.

I argued in [*The Knowledge of Good and
Evil*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-knowledge-of-good-and-evil) that a man in recovery did
not beat his demons — he stopped granting them the war. That was Luke's sentence before it was
mine.

**One saying cuts against me and I would rather raise it than be caught with it.** When an unclean
spirit goes out of a man, the story runs, it wanders through dry places, finds no rest, and comes
back to find the house swept and garnished and empty — so it fetches seven worse than itself and
moves in, *and the last state of that man is worse than the first.*[^returning] That is a warning, and pretending otherwise
would cost me the essay. But notice what it warns about: not a menacing spirit lying in
wait, but **an emptied house that nobody ever filled.** The danger in the sentence is the vacancy.
And notice who is standing there when He says it — Matthew appends *even so shall it be also unto
this wicked generation*, which is to say, the audience that had just demanded a sign from Him.

So the claim I will defend is narrower than the one I wanted, and it is still the one that
matters: **He never tells anybody to be afraid of a demon.** Not once, in seventeen verses.

[^daughter]: Mark 7:29 (KJV).

[^lordlord]: Matthew 7:22–23. The criterion in that scene belongs to [*The Sheep in the

## V. The Kingdom That Isn't

Sixteen verses for Satan and the devil, and the one everybody quotes has been read backwards for
a very long time.

If you have ever heard that Satan has a kingdom, this is where it comes from:

> *If Satan cast out Satan, he is divided against himself; how shall then his kingdom stand?*

That is a conditional — the second half of a reductio whose first half is *if* — and the grammar
is worth being exact about, because a great deal rides on it. This is what
grammarians call a first-class conditional — *if* plus the indicative — and it is often glossed
*since*, which is very likely how the flat reading got manufactured in the first place. But the
gloss is a convenience rather than a rule: the construction grants its premise for the sake of
the argument, and whether the speaker believes it is settled by context, not by the
grammar.[^conditional] The context here is not in doubt, because the very next verse runs the
identical construction on a premise Jesus certainly does not hold — *if I by Beelzebub cast out
devils, by whom do your children cast them out?* Nobody reads that one as a confession. It is the same *if.*

So: Jesus is not telling His audience that there is a dark realm with a throne in it. He is taking the
premise the accusation requires, running it forward, and handing it back broken: *your charge,
believed, destroys the thing it needs.* The sentence is an argument against the men who made it.
The only reason it sounds like a doctrine is that we have spent centuries lifting the second
clause out of a conditional and reading it flat.

And Mark, who is always the plainest, finishes the thought in a phrase I cannot get a two-powers
cosmology to absorb: *if Satan rise up against himself, and be divided, he cannot stand, but hath
an end.*[^hathanend] An end. Whatever else the adversary is in this book, he isn't one of two
balanced forces locked in a long war; he is a thing that is finishing.

Set the rest of the sixteen beside that and they stop matching the picture almost entirely.
Luke has Him saying *I beheld Satan as lightning fall from heaven* — which is a report of a
defeat, delivered as an aside, to people who had not asked. Luke also has Him naming Satan for
one purpose only: to justify untying a woman who had been bent double for eighteen years, on a
sabbath, over the objections of the man who ran the synagogue. *Ought not this woman, being a
daughter of Abraham, whom Satan hath bound, lo, these eighteen years, be loosed from this bond on
the sabbath day?*[^woman] The adversary is invoked in that sentence as the reason to break a rule
in somebody's favor. I do not want to explain that verse away in either direction: it says the
binding was real and it says the untying could not wait, and the person it is aimed at is the
rule-keeper.

Then the wilderness, where you would expect the doctrine if it were anywhere. Across the entire
temptation Jesus says three things to Satan, and all three of them are quotations from
Deuteronomy.[^deut] He does not explain him. He does not rank him. He does not describe his
kingdom, his origin, his angels, or his methods. He quotes Deuteronomy and He leaves. If we
wanted an account of the enemy from the one person who had just spent forty days alone with him,
we had our chance, and what we got was three verses of Torah and a closed door.

And then the sentence that undoes the picture by itself, which is that the harshest thing Jesus
ever calls anybody by that name, He calls Peter.

Peter has just heard that Jesus is going to Jerusalem to be killed, and he does what a friend
does — takes Him aside and tells Him it isn't going to happen. *Be it far from thee, LORD: this
shall not be unto thee.* And the answer is *Get thee behind me, Satan* — followed by the actual
charge, which is not possession and not allegiance: *thou savourest not the things that be of God,
but those that be of men.*[^peter]

Read that as a two-powers sentence and it makes no sense at all. Peter is not an agent of a rival
kingdom. Peter is a man who loves Him and would like Him not to die, and in that moment the love
is pointed at the wrong thing. If the name marked a separate being with a separate throne, it
could not land on a friend mid-kindness. It lands there easily.

### The strong man, which is the real objection

There is a saying that cuts against all of this.

> *No man can enter into a strong man's house, and spoil his goods, except he will first bind the
> strong man; and then he will spoil his house.*[^strongman]

That is combat imagery and there is no reading it away. Luke's version is harder still, and I
will quote the hard one rather than the easy one: *when a strong man armed keepeth his palace,
his goods are in peace: but when a stronger than he shall come upon him, and overcome him, he
taketh from him all his armour wherein he trusted, and divideth his spoils.* A palace. Armour. An
overcoming. Spoils, divided. Anybody who wants a cosmic war can have this verse, and they should
have it at full size.

What I will say is smaller than a refutation, and I think it is what the picture actually shows.
Look at the tense of it. In none of the three tellings is there a fight in progress. The strong
man is bound *first*, in a subordinate clause, and the whole action of the sentence is the
carrying out of goods from a house whose owner is already tied up. In Luke the stronger one
simply *comes upon him, and overcomes him* — one clause, no struggle in it, no outcome in doubt.
The armour is named only as the thing he had trusted — the past tense of a man who doesn't have
it any more.

This is an image of an aftermath. It grants a strong man; it does not grant a second power. And
the difference between those two is the whole difference between a world at war and a world being
quietly emptied of something that used to hold it.

The saying is combat language and it stays combat language. What it isn't is a description of
two thrones.

[^hathanend]: Mark 3:26 (KJV). The phrase is Mark's alone — Matthew 12:26 and Luke 11:18 both
end at *how shall then his kingdom stand?*, and neither carries *but hath an end.*

[^conditional]: The construction at Matthew 12:26 and Luke 11:18 is the first-class conditional
— *ei* with the indicative, about three hundred instances in the New Testament. Daniel B.
Wallace, *Greek Grammar Beyond the Basics* (Zondervan, 1996), in the chapter on conditional
sentences, gives its force as **the assumption of truth for the sake of argument**: *if — and
let us assume this is true for the sake of argument — then.* Matthew 12:26 is among the standard
examples. The popular gloss *since* overstates it, and Matthew 12:27 is the control in the same
paragraph: the identical construction, on a premise the speaker plainly rejects.

[^woman]: Luke 13:16 (KJV); the episode runs 13:10–17. Luke 10:18 for the lightning.

[^deut]: Matthew 4:1–11; the three replies quote Deuteronomy 8:3, 6:16 and 6:13. Luke 4:1–13 has
the second and third in the reverse order — kingdoms before temple — and the three replies are
otherwise the same. One wrinkle worth stating rather than smoothing: Deuteronomy 6:13 in the
Hebrew, and so in the King James, reads *thou shalt **fear** the LORD thy God, and serve him.*
The gospels' *thou shalt **worship*** follows the Greek of the Septuagint. The reply is a
quotation of that verse; it is not a quotation of the King James's rendering of it.

[^peter]: Matthew 16:22–23; Mark 8:33 (KJV). Mark lacks Matthew's *thou art an offence unto me*
and has Jesus turning to look at the disciples before He speaks; the rebuke and its stated
ground are otherwise identical in both.

[^strongman]: Mark 3:27; Matthew 12:29; Luke 11:21–22 (KJV). Luke's is the fuller and more
martial version — the palace, the armour, the divided spoils — where Mark and Matthew have only
the house, the binding and the goods.

## VI. And What About Hell

The same pattern is in the other vocabulary, and it's the vocabulary most people actually mean
when they say the gospels are frightening. So: twenty-four verses, and I read every one of them
for the only question worth asking of them. Who is standing there?

The scribes and the Pharisees, twice, by name.[^woes] *The children of the kingdom* — the
insiders — cast out into the dark.[^children] Capernaum, which is not a wicked city on a plain
somewhere but the town that had actually watched Him work, brought down.[^capernaum] The people
who presume on Abraham and find themselves outside.[^abraham] The servant given his portion *with
the hypocrites.*[^hypocrites] Then a long run of verses that are not about anybody else at all:
your right eye, your right hand, your foot, and the man you called a fool.[^members] And then a
rich man who ate well while a beggar lay at his gate,[^lazarus] and a crowd at the end of
everything who did not feed, clothe, or visit anyone — and who, the passage is at pains to say,
had no idea.[^sheep]

I have now read all twenty-four several times, and of the ones that are warnings at all, I can't
find one aimed at an outsider.
Not at a pagan, not at a foreigner, not at a heretic, and — the one almost anybody would bet on —
**not at anybody for failing to believe something.** The warnings in the gospels run
consistently toward the middle of the room — landing on the religiously confident, on the
hearer's own hand, and on the man who stepped over somebody on his way in.

There is one more, and it runs backwards from all the others. *The gates of hell shall not
prevail.*[^gates] It's the only place in the four gospels where hell is the subject of a
sentence with a verb, and what the verb reports is a failure. Gates do not besiege anybody. Gates
are what you have when something is coming for you.

And the structure is the same one the seventy walked into. Luke's only mention of *gehenna* sits at the fifth
verse of the twelfth chapter — inside a speech that says *be not afraid* at the fourth verse,
says it again at the seventh with the sparrows and the numbered hairs, and says it a third time
at the thirty-second: *Fear not, little flock; for it is your Father's good pleasure to give you
the kingdom.*[^luke12] Matthew's version of the same speech has the identical shape. The most
frightening sentence He is on record as saying is a subordinate clause inside His most comforting
speech, and its grammatical job is to move fear off the people who could actually hurt you.

For what it is worth: verses in which He tells people not to be afraid, counted strictly, come to
nineteen; count *take no thought* as well and it is twenty-six. Against twenty-four to
twenty-seven for hell. The ranges overlap, and the honest statement is the dull one.
**They are the same order of magnitude, and they occur in the same speeches.**

John's gospel does attach condemnation to unbelief, and does it sharply — which is where the
objections begin.

[^children]: Matthew 8:12.

[^capernaum]: Matthew 11:23; Luke 10:15.

[^abraham]: Luke 13:28.

[^hypocrites]: Matthew 24:51.

[^members]: Matthew 5:22, 5:29–30, 18:8–9; Mark 9:43–48.

[^lazarus]: Luke 16:19–31, at 16:23 and 16:28.

[^sheep]: Matthew 25:31–46, at 25:41 and 25:46. That neither group recognizes what it has done —

## VII. Objections, at Full Strength

The first is the one that could take the whole thing down.

**If there is no evil power, is nothing evil?** No, and I need to be exact, because the sloppy
version of what I am arguing is genuinely dangerous. Denying two cosmic *powers* is not denying
that a cruel act and a kind one differ. It is not saying that the harm done to a child is
anything less than harm. What dissolves is not the difference between good and evil *acts*; it is
the sorting of *persons* into good and evil *kinds*, the filing of a human being onto the far side
of a line so that we can stop owing him anything. I argued that at length in [*The Knowledge of
Good and Evil*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-knowledge-of-good-and-evil) and I'm not going
to re-argue it, but I won't let this essay be read without it. And there is a companion claim
holding the same seam from the other side: [*The Freedom to Walk
Away*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-freedom-to-walk-away) argues that God did not create
sin, because sin is not a created object but a *use* of a created freedom. A real wrong with no
rival deity behind it is exactly the thing that essay was written to describe.

**And no part of this says a suffering is unreal.** That's the trapdoor under every argument of
this shape, and I'd rather nail it shut than step around it. That a thing has no independent
power behind it has never once meant it does not cut.[^unreal]

**"You are attacking a caricature."** Fair. Jesus did talk about the devil — sixteen separate
verses of it — and the argument here has never been that the verses are absent. So take the
passages the picture actually stands on, at their strongest.

The wilderness is the first of them, offered as proof that the adversary has power enough to
bargain with the Son of God. But one of the three verses usually brought forward is *thou shalt
not tempt the LORD thy God*, which does not mention him at all. It is a sentence about God,
quoted out of Deuteronomy to somebody who had just misquoted a psalm.

Then the demoniac among the tombs, where the sentence most often quoted — *what have I to do with
thee, Jesus, thou Son of the most high God? I adjure thee by God, that thou torment me not* — is
spoken by the demon. His own words in that scene are a command to the spirit to come out of the
man, and nothing else. And the episode does not end where it is usually left off. It ends with the man *sitting, and clothed, and in his
right mind*, and sent home to tell his friends how great things the LORD has done for him.[^gerasene]

Then the seventy, back from the field with the demons subject to them, quoted to the end of the
promise — *nothing shall by any means hurt you* — and stopped there, one verse short of *in this
rejoice not*.

And the everlasting fire of Matthew 25, read as Satan's kingdom, where the text says it was
*prepared for the devil and his angels*: something made ready for him, which is the opposite of a
realm he rules.

It is the same lift every time. A phrase is carried out of its sentence and stood up on its own,
and the sentence it came out of is doing something else.

None of that needs anybody to be arguing in bad faith. It is what all of us do with a text — fill
the gaps from the picture we were handed long before we ever opened the book. The picture is the
subject here. The people holding it are not.

**"The Church teaches this, and you don't."** This is the objection with the most weight behind
it, and it ought to end the argument. The Catechism defines hell as a *"definitive self-exclusion
from communion with God,"* arrived at *"by our own free choice"* — a door held shut from the
inside rather than a sentence handed down — and then says flatly that *"God predestines no one to
go to hell."* The same article affirms hell's existence and its eternity and calls its chief
punishment eternal separation from God; that stands, and I've no interest in trimming it.[^ccc]

It also describes an adversary, and quoting only the paragraphs that suit me would be a cheat. It
has him a created angel who went wrong by his own choice — which is the freedom argument and not
the dualist one. And then it says the thing this argument has been walking toward, in words I
can't improve: ***the power of Satan is not infinite.*** He is *"only a creature."*[^ccc2] The
church that gave us the fresco denies the second power in its own catechism, and has done all
along.

**John 3:18.** *He that believeth not is condemned already, because he hath not believed in the
name of the only begotten Son of God.* That is condemnation attached to unbelief in the plainest words
available, and the distinction between condemnation-language and hell-language does
not get me out of it cleanly. What that verse means turns entirely on what *the name*
is, and I have spent a whole essay on that question already; [*In the Name (The
Ambassador)*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/in-the-name-the-ambassador) argues that to do a
thing in someone's name is to do their will, so that the name you are asked to believe in is not
a word to say but a will to come under. I will let it carry this, and note only that John's own
next verse gives the criterion in his own voice, and it is not a vocabulary test: *men loved
darkness rather than light, because their deeds were evil.*[^deeds]

**John 8:44**, the fiercest verse in this territory. *Ye are of your father the devil.* The room
is the one described at the start, and the detail that keeps it a family fight rather than a
verdict sits eight verses upstream: the people He is addressing are introduced as *those Jews
which believed on him.*[^john831] The framing that did the damage — *speaking to a group of Jews*
— flattens exactly that. Having said it: this is the one place He uses the name to build a
genealogy of a disposition, and it belongs on the honest side of my ledger.

**"You have made it comfortable."** This is the objection I would make, and it is wrong in a
specific way that is worth naming. A world with one power is not a softer world. It is a world
with nothing left to blame. The two-powers picture has a great consolation in it that nobody
admits to: if there is an enemy, then the wrong in me arrived from outside, and the war explains
me. Take the second power away and the explanation goes with it. Add the finding about aim — that the warnings
run toward the middle of the room — and the reader most exposed here is the one who was sure he
was fine. That includes the man writing it, who has now published a dozen essays
about how other people read their tradition wrong.

## VIII. The Picture Was Painted

Here's what I think happened, and it isn't a conspiracy. It's just how pictures work.

Almost everything you can describe about the enemy, you didn't get from the sixteen verses. Say
the word and see what arrives: horns, a tail, a pitchfork, a cloven hoof, red. None of that is in
the gospels. As far as I can find, none of it is anywhere in the Bible at all. It came
from somewhere else, and the somewhere else is traceable. The horns and the cloven hooves are
Pan's — lifted off a goat-legged fertility god by way of the satyrs that Jerome had already
identified with demons. The pitchfork is a trident, and the earliest one anybody has found in
Christian hands is carved on a stone cross at Monasterboice, in Ireland, around the tenth
century, possibly the ninth. By about then the whole costume was conventional.[^horns] The rest came from a
poet who supplied the architecture and the nine descending circles, and from the doom paintings
on the west wall where the congregation could see them right through the sermon, and from the
tympanum carved over the door — so that for most people in most centuries the picture of the
last judgment was literally the thing you walked under on your way in.

Translation did some of the work too. English collapses *gehenna* — a valley outside Jerusalem
that you could walk to in an afternoon — and *hades*, the realm of the dead, into one word, and
the word arrives in your ear already furnished. A place became a cosmology partly because two
words became one.

I have done this several times now, and by now I know the shape. [*They/Them*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/theythem)
took down the picture of God made of grammar and pigment; [*The Life Is Not in the
Book*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-life-is-not-in-the-book) took down the one made of
paper; [*Son of Joseph*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/son-of-joseph) took down the one made of
biology. This one is made of paint.

And the rule I have followed every time is the one that matters most here, because this is the
piece most tempted to break it: when you take a picture down you do not hang another one in the
gap. So I'm not going to finish by telling you there is no devil. I don't know that, and this
essay hasn't shown it, and a confident denial would be the same furniture rearranged. The strong
man stays where he is, bound in his own house, in a sentence I cannot explain away. So does the
everlasting fire in Matthew 25, and the last state of that man, worse than the first.

What I think the counting shows is narrower and, I have come to think, harder to live with. In
the words we have from Him, the darkness is never given a throne, never given a briefing, never
given a war, and never given to us to be afraid of. It gets a subordinate clause. It gets an
aside about lightning. It gets three lines of Deuteronomy and a closed door. And when the people
He sent came back from the field with a victory to report, He told them to rejoice about
something else.

The men who started all this handed Him a board with two sides and asked which one He was on.
He asked them how long a house lasts when it is fighting itself, and He went back to the crowd,
and He kept healing people.
