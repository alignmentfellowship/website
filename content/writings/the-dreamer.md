## I. The Dream You Almost Woke From

You've had this dream, or one close enough.

You're somewhere — a house that is your house and also not, a street that keeps becoming a
different street. Something is happening; there are people; there may be fear. And then, without
deciding to, you notice. A small wrongness snags — the light is impossible, or the dead are alive —
and a second knowing opens quietly behind the first: none of this is outside me. I'm dreaming.

That moment is the one firsthand experience every person has had of a whole world made of nothing
but mind. The mountain is not stone; it is a thought — the same stuff as the hand
you lift to test it, the same stuff as the stranger with a face you never saw awake. One substance,
wearing ten thousand shapes and calling them a world, and while you were under it felt completely
real. That is the gift of the dream: proof, from the inside, that a reality can be entire, and
convincing, and made of mind alone.

Now the dream will try to teach you something false, and it is the thing this essay exists to
undo. In your dream, the mind it is all made of is yours. So the dream whispers that to wake all the
way up would be to find that you, at the center, were the maker of it the whole time — that you are
God, and never knew. Set that whisper down. Keep the experience and drop the conclusion. It is the
sweetest and oldest error there is.

There's a truer picture of a world made of mind. Not a dream you are having — a story you are in. A
novelist imagines a world and fills it with people made wholly of the novelist's mind: they think
the author's thoughts, look out through an awareness that was never their own, and walk streets that
exist nowhere but in the author's head. Everything true of the dream is true here — one mind, no
second thing anywhere. But one thing is not true, and it is the hinge of the whole essay: the author
is not a character in the book. You never pass the novelist on a street in the novel. The characters
do not hold the pen; they are written.

But a novel is finished. Its people never truly choose. The world we are in is more alive than that
— it answers us, it opens where we turn. So keep the novelist and add one more figure,
from a stranger corner of the culture: the dungeon master, the DM. In the game called Dungeons &
Dragons there is no board and almost no script. A few friends make a story together, out loud; each
controls a single character and may try anything at all — open the door or burn it down, follow the
road or leave it for the woods. One person plays no character. The DM runs the world itself,
describes the tavern and the forest, speaks for every stranger, decides how the world answers
whatever the players try. The players hold their own choices; the master holds everything else.

Now give that DM unbounded intelligence, and let the world be no painted scenery but real: a mind
holding a whole world and telling its story live, raising a forest the instant the players turn
toward it, giving a stranger they stop to speak to a face and a history and a grief on the spot.
There is a main quest, the story the master means to tell. But the players are free, really free, to
wander off it, and nothing they choose can break the world; an intelligence that deep takes the
wandering up and weaves it in, until even the detours are found, in the end, to have served the
tale.

So you are a character in a novel and a player at that table at once: made wholly by the teller's
mind, and genuinely free inside it; written, and choosing. That doubled figure — the Novelist DM,
one Mind wearing both — is the image of God this essay will keep. This Novelist DM has a name the
tradition has circled for three thousand years.

## II. Take the Two Words Literally

Start with two words the tradition has said about God for as long as it has said anything:
omnipotent, and omnipresent. We repeat them so often they've gone smooth, like coins worn blank.
At full weight they go somewhere.

All-powerful. Not very powerful; not the strongest among the powers. All of it. If God's power is
total, then there is no power that is not God's — no second force standing on its own, no
independent engine running anywhere under its own steam. Whatever moves, moves on a power that is on
loan. Grant a single thing one native watt of its own and you've made God large but no longer
all-powerful. You've drawn a border, and set on the far side of it something God is not.

All-present. Not nearly everywhere; everywhere. *If I ascend up into heaven, thou art there,* the
psalmist says; *if I make my bed in hell, behold, thou art there.*[^1][^house] There is no coordinate
you can name where God is absent. And if there is nowhere God is not, then there is no empty shelf
on which to set a thing that is its own.

Together, the two forbid the picture we were taught. We picture creation as manufacture: God over here makes a
world over there — a potter, a pot. But manufacture needs two things the two words forbid. It needs
a place to set the product, a somewhere outside the maker — and an omnipresent God has no outside.
And it needs the product to be its own stuff once it's made — and an omnipotent God has lent it every
ounce of what it is. A pot that is entirely the potter's power, entirely on the potter's ground, with
nothing of its own and nowhere else to be, is not an object across from the potter. It is the
potter's, through and through — and still a pot.

So the God the tradition confesses cannot have made a world the way we make things. Taken at their
word, omnipotence and omnipresence don't describe a very large being set beside a world; they
describe a reality with no beside — no outside, no second, no rival, no independent anything. *I am
the LORD, and there is none else.*[^3] The Shema is not counting to one. It is saying there is only.

Whatever the world is, it is happening inside — and by — the only One there is.

## III. Made by the Novelist DM

Paul says it on a Greek hill, to people who worshipped everything, and he says it in their own
poets' words: *in [Them] we live, and move, and have our being.*[^7] Not near. Not toward. In. We are
inside the life of God the way a wave is inside the sea — not a thing the sea made and set adrift,
but a shape the sea is holding, with not one drop of water that is its own.

You are about to say that the East saw this first, and more precisely. It did. The Upanishads saw
the dependence with a clarity the West has rarely matched, and landed on a sentence the whole
Vedanta turns on: *tat tvam asi*, that thou art.[^5] And there the roads part, at the exact place
they look like one road. Take *that thou art* as a claim about substance — the same stuff as God, in a
smaller amount — and the character has stood up and claimed the pen in the most exalted dress there
is. Take the dependence as authorship instead and not an ounce of it is lost. A character is made of
the author down to the last comma; is not a smaller piece of them; is not on the way to becoming them.
And a character can be addressed, and can answer. A portion of a substance cannot. That is the whole
of what follows. I've argued in [*Krishna Is Not Christ*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/krishna-is-not-christ)
that this is not a difference of degree, and in
[*Not a Bodhisattva*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/not-a-bodhisattva) that a surrendered self asks
a question the sage does not. What matters here is only this: you are made by the Author, and you are not the Author, and you were never meant to become Them.

One guard on the word for the story itself. *Maya* is almost always broken in translation into
"illusion," then heard as unreal, therefore worthless.[^6] That is not the claim. The old image is a
rope taken in the dusk for a snake: the snake was never there, and the fear was real, and someone can
die of a snake that does not exist. The story is not nothing; it is the real, world-making telling
of a mind that is more than it. You are a character in it, made by it, right now.

Which leaves the question the rest of this essay lives inside. If the world is a story, and its
teller is God, and there is nothing outside God — then who, exactly, is the character so sure that they
are the one holding the pen?

## IV. Then Who Are We?

A character who has forgotten they are written.

Go back into the lucid dream, but this time don't wake. Stay a character. There you are, the figure
crossing the room — bounded by your skin, wary of the stranger, certain past all argument that the
world is out there and you are in here. Most of that is true. You *are* one figure. You were written
as one, on purpose, with one face and one address, and nothing in this essay is going to talk you out
of it. What is false is smaller and more total than that: the certainty that the figure is its own,
that it stands on its own power on its own ground, that nobody is writing it. The character is real.
The wall it has built around itself, the one that says *mine* — that is the part that was never
there.

When Genesis says we are made in the *image* of God,[^8] the word is stronger than the resemblance
we tend to hear in it. Picture a space of infinitely many dimensions, and let the whole of that space
be God. Now picture a small finite room inside it, three directions out of the infinite, and let the
infinite fall onto that room the way a solid thing held up to the light falls as a flat shadow on the
floor. What lands is a projection: a real and exact trace of the infinite, carrying everything of it
those few dimensions can hold, and nothing of the boundless rest. That is what a self is. Genuinely
God's, the way a shadow genuinely belongs to what stands in the light; genuinely finite, because a
room can hold only what a room can hold. Each of us a different projection of the same infinite onto
a different little room — which is why we are every one the image of God, and no two of us the same
image, and none of us the light.

The empty self's whole mistake, then, is one confusion. It takes the projection for the point. It is
sure the shadow stands on its own — self-cast, self-owning, the whole of what is there — when the
shadow is the trace of something past all measure, holding it in being from above, this instant and
every instant. You are not less than you imagined. You are the shadow that took itself for the only
thing in the room, and never looked up.

Vedanta, having said *that thou art*, draws the conclusion left to it: *aham brahmasmi* — I am
Brahman.[^10] Said by the ego standing up to claim its godhood, it is the oldest lie, the character
snatching the pen. Said by the ego seen through, it says the one thing in it that is true — that the
awareness the character called its own was the Author's the whole time, on loan — and the one
who sees that does not become the Author, but a character who knows whose they are. The words are
identical. Everything turns on who is left to say them, and on this side of the seam what is left is
a someone who was given a life, not the One who gave it.

So the forgetting is not a crime. The character who takes themselves for their own hasn't sinned; they have
done the one thing a story asks of its characters, which is to believe in it. The whole ache of being
a person — the loneliness no crowd cures, the sense of being pressed against a glass you can't find
the edge of — is what it costs to be one figure and to have forgotten who is holding you.

## V. The Character Who Handed Back the Pen

Every teacher who ever lived taught out of what he had come to know. Even the Buddha, as clear-eyed
a seer as the world has produced, woke by his own long looking and taught from his own attainment;
there was no one above him he answered to. That is no knock on him. It is the shape of a sage. Jesus
of Nazareth did the opposite, and the opposite is the whole of it.

He claimed nothing as His own. *The Son can do nothing of Himself.* *I can of Mine own self do
nothing … I seek not Mine own will, but the will of the Father.* *My doctrine is not Mine, but
[Theirs] that sent Me.* *The Father that dwelleth in Me, [They do] the works.*[^14] Every sentence
hands the wisdom, the power, the very act upward, to a Father. Where the sage stands on the summit
and speaks, Jesus stands with empty hands and says, again and again, it isn't Mine.

And that is the key to the other sentences, the ones that got Him killed. *I and My Father are one.
Before Abraham was, I am. He that hath seen Me hath seen the Father.*[^15] From someone reaching for
godhood, these are the proudest words ever spoken. But they don't come from someone reaching for
anything; they come from the one man who had let go of everything. His *I am* is not the ego swelling
until it bursts into blasphemy. It is a will so completely given that the only *I* left to say the
word was the Father's. The most audacious claim in history was made by the most emptied man in it.

Here is the fork every character comes to, once it understands that its life was never its own but
the teller's, on loan. It can snatch the pen and try to write itself — the one thing a character can
never do, and the whole exhausting comedy of the empty self. Or it can do the humbler and far harder
thing, and hand the pen back. Not my will, but Yours. Write me as You will.

Jesus is the character who handed back the pen. And because He kept nothing for Himself — no plot of
His own, no will held in reserve — the Author was free to write the whole of Themselves, without remainder, into that one life. *And the Word was made flesh.*[^16] In the language of the projection:
each of us is the infinite cast into one small life, and each of us has laid something over the top
of it that is not God's at all — the empty self, the invented owner we spend our lives defending.
That addition is the distortion, the reason the image always comes out a little crooked. Christ is
the projection with nothing added, and here is the thing the creeds insisted on and never quite
explained, fully God and fully man: not a hybrid, not God wearing a man like a coat, but the infinite
cast into one human life with no false owner laid over the top. Fully man: a Galilean with a mother
and a death, one small room and no other. Fully God: within its dimensions the whole of God stands
undistorted. The old word for taking that addition away is *kenosis* — He *made Himself of no
reputation*, Paul says; He *thought it not robbery to be equal with God* and did not clutch at
it.[^17] The emptying was not a loss. It was the one subtraction that leaves everything.

He did not describe humility from a safe height. He went down into it first, all the way to a cross,
and then turned and said the two words that are the door: follow Me.

## VI. The Invitation We Turned into a Fence

Follow Me. Not admire Me; not sing about Me from a safe distance. Follow — come where I am, do what I
did. It is the most extravagant invitation ever issued, and we have spent two thousand years making
certain it could not possibly mean what it says.

On the last night He asks *that they all may be one; as Thou, Father, art in Me, and I in Thee, that
they also may be one in us.*[^18] The oneness He has with God He asks to be handed on to them — *I in
them*. And earlier, flatly: *the works that I do shall he do also; and greater works than these shall
he do.*[^19] Greater. He did not take Himself for the exception. He took Himself for the first — the
elder brother who came back into the story to shake the others by the shoulder.

So how did the invitation curdle into a fence? Because the empty self is not a mistake we happen to
make; it is the water we swim in, and it will do anything to survive — including bend the most radical
summons in history into its own opposite. Told *you are one with God as I am; now come*, the empty
self hears the single thing it can bear to hear: *He is one with God, and I am not.* It runs a wall
straight down the middle of the invitation and names it reverence. It would rather worship Him than
follow Him, because worship, for all its warmth, keeps the gap open, and the gap is the empty self's
whole life. A God over there can be adored forever. A God who says *you, too* has to be followed.

You're forming the objection already, and it's the right one: *isn't this the old heresy — Jesus
dissolved into a technique, one enlightened man among many, each of us a little god?* No, and the
reason is the whole argument. The invitation is not to become the Author. It is to be His the way He
was the Father's — a will handed back, a character who does the will of the One telling the story and
adds no plot of their own. That is what *one as we are one* asks: a oneness of will and love between
two who stay two. Nobody in that prayer disappears. The Christian East has never flinched from this.
*He was made man,* wrote Athanasius, *that we might be made God*[^20] — and the tradition that says
it, *theosis*, means participation in God's life, never identity with it. I am not making Jesus less
than the creeds make Him. I am refusing to make the rest of us less than He said we were.

Which is the point where an honest reader stops nodding and lifts a hand. If the world is God's
story and every face is God's own, then a question has been standing in the corner of the room since
the first page, arms folded. What about the nightmares?

## VII. What About the Nightmares

A picture of God that has to be shielded from this question is already dead. If the world is God's
story, then God is writing the cancer ward. God is writing the
child in the cellar and the men on the other side of the door. And if some quiet part of you wants
to file all of that under illusion — only a story, not real, nothing finally happening — then this
whole essay deserves to be thrown across the room, and I will throw it first.

Made of mind is not the same as make-believe. The rope taken for a snake says it: there was no
snake, and the terror was real, and a heart can stop from it. To the one inside it, the
nightmare is total; there is no *merely* available from the inside, and there is no inside except the
one we are in. Whatever else this frame is permitted to do, it is never permitted to say to a
suffering person, *this isn't happening*. It is happening. It is made by God, and it is happening.

Which is the one thing this frame can say that a tidier theology cannot. If God were the watchmaker
in the next room, the child's scream would be an item in Their inbox. But a God who is present at
every point of the story is not in the next room. God is in the cellar with the child. *Inasmuch as
ye have done it unto one of the least of these,* He said — and He did not say *as if* unto Me; He said
*unto Me.*[^21] Whatever is done to the least is done to God, because God is in the cellar — in that
face, in that dark — and the child is not alone in it. This is why, of all the things God might have
shown us of Themselves, what They showed us was a man tortured to death by the state. The Author did not write the cellar from a safe desk; They wrote Themselves into the story, took a character's
flesh, and bled in it — not to explain the horror, not to license it, but to be found inside it. The
cross is not God's excuse for suffering. It is God's whereabouts.

And that is as far as I will go, because the next step is the one I refuse. You want a reason. You
want to be told what the suffering is for, so that the books balance and the nightmare turns out, in
the end, to have been worth it. Any reason clean enough to make you nod would be an obscenity read
aloud over a real grave. No sentence that begins *the murdered child was necessary because* stays holy
to its end. We do not need a why. We were never promised one. What we were given instead was not a
reason but a companion in the dark, bleeding alongside us, and the given thing is better than the
argument would have been.

There is a softer version of all this, the one on the dorm-room poster: *we're all one, man, it's all
love, nothing's really bad.* The one Life that holds us
all is not the anesthetic; it is the exposed nerve. If the one Life holds us all, then the person
bleeding in the cellar is not someone else's tragedy at a safe remove — God is in it, and you are not
outside it. And the one on the far side of the door is not conveniently outside the circle either: they
too are someone God has not left, who has forgotten whose they are, and you are asked to keep even
them inside the single life you share, while never calling what they did anything but monstrous. That
is not comfort. Comfort was the wall. It is the hardest demand anyone has ever been handed, which is
presumably why it was handed down by a man on a cross, and not by a poster.

## VIII. Thy Will Be Done

The man on the cross got there by a particular motion, and it is the one thing in this whole essay
you can actually do tonight. But first, the heavy word.

I said the character who takes themselves for their own hasn't sinned. That was true of the forgetting.
But no one merely forgets. The empty self does not just sit in its sealed room; it wants. It defends
its orbit. It puts itself first, because from inside the wall there is nowhere else to put it. And a
will curved that far in on itself can do nothing but make suffering — for itself, in the exhausting
labor of defending a self that was never in danger, and for everyone it collides with, because every
cruelty ever done was an empty self treating another as something outside its circle. That is what
the tradition has been pointing at all along with its heaviest word. Not a checklist of forbidden
acts; the acts are the symptoms. Sin is the state underneath them: living as a will that believes it
is its own.

And its wages are not a sentence handed down from a throne.[^22] There is no punishment engine. Sin
is the wound that keeps wounding, the clenched fist that is its own ache. Separation is not met with
suffering. Separation is the suffering — the only hell there has ever been is a self alone in a world
it has walled itself out of.

Which is why you cannot fix it the way the empty self fixes everything, by trying harder. The self
cannot dissolve the self; the will that is the whole problem cannot be the thing that solves it.
Every effort to achieve oneness is only the ego appointing itself god one more time — the exact
disease, wearing a halo.

This is the reef that most of what now calls itself spirituality runs onto. Manifest the life you
want; bend the story to your will — it can sound like waking up, but it is the character reaching for
the master's pen to write its own campaign. And there is a subtler reef beside it, and I ran aground
on it myself: see through the self, dissolve the character, wake all the way up and find there was
nobody there to have been suffering.

That one is not foolish, and it deserves a straight answer. It is the honest answer to a real
question, and the question is *how does this stop?* — the one that crowds out the others when you are
inside a hurt. Call it the patient's question. The Buddha put it at the center of everything he
taught and followed it all the way down: if the suffering belongs to a self, go and look for the
self. What he reported is that you do not find one, and that the ache turns out to have had nobody it
was happening to. He did not mean annihilation, and his tradition has always refused that word; he
meant that the one who was asking is not there to be found. I've set that beside the gospel in
[*Not a Bodhisattva*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/not-a-bodhisattva), and I have no correction to
offer him.

The patient's question can be asked alone. That is the difference, and the rest follows from it: it
needs nobody on the other end, only the pain and enough honesty to look. *What do You want* cannot be
asked alone. There is a *You* in it. And a character with a *You* in their question is not trying to
get out of the story — they are trying to find out what the Author wants.

So there is one door, and it does not lead up, and it does not lead out. It leads down, into a
single sentence. The empty self cannot become the Whole and was never asked to. It can stop insisting
on itself. It can say, and mean, *not My will, but Thine*[^23] — laying down the campaign it was
writing for itself, and taking up the one quest the master had been telling all along. That is not
the self achieving something; it is the self setting down the one thing it was never able to carry,
which was itself. And in that setting-down the wall does not have to be broken, because a wall that
no one is defending is already nothing.

This is why the prayer He taught turns, at its center, on those four words — *Thy will be done* —
and why, in the garden, what we are given to overhear is not a man rising above His fear but a man
surrendering straight through the middle of it. Gethsemane is a will handed back from inside a self
that is still afraid — which is the only place any of us will ever hand it back. He did not tell us to
feel no fear. He told us to say the words anyway, and He went first.

I know how this lands on anyone who was handed a religion that used the word *surrender* as a boot
on the neck. The will you are yielding to is not a tyrant across the sky who
wants you diminished. It is the will of the One you are made by and made for — nearer to you than you
are to yourself, and never you — the Someone the frightened little will has spent your whole life
standing in front of. *Thy will be done* is not *I am nothing.* It is *I can stop pretending I am
alone.* It is the end of a war, and the war was with yourself all along.

And nothing is destroyed in it. You keep your face, your name, your one particular part. Nobody
vanishes. What dies is the exhausting fiction that you were ever on your own — and on the far side of
that small death is the one thing the empty self could never win by defending itself: to stop being
a stranger, and come home.

## IX. Stay

There's a lay brother who cooked for a Carmelite house in Paris in the sixteen hundreds, and he
never woke from anything. He had no system and refused to want one. What he did instead was talk to
God, all day, in the kitchen, and he said the plainest true thing about it that I know: *The time of
business does not with me differ from the time of prayer; and in the noise and clutter of my kitchen,
while several persons are at the same time calling for different things, I possess GOD in as great
tranquillity as if I were upon my knees at the Blessed Sacrament.*[^kitchen] He failed constantly,
and when he did he told God so and *gave himself no further uneasiness about it*.[^fail] He said
there needed *neither art nor science for going to GOD, but only a heart resolutely determined to
apply itself to nothing but Him*.[^art] And he said the whole of this essay in a sentence a cook could
say: *I began to live as if there was none but He and I in the world.*[^none]

That man was not seeing through the kitchen. He was not trying to
find out that the pots and the brothers and the noise were made of him, or that he was made of them.
He was a character who knew whose story he was in, and he spent the whole of it talking to the One
telling it, and he never tried to become Him. That is what it looks like to stay.

Because there are two things you can do, once you know whose story you are in, and only one of them
was ever asked of you. You can try to wake — thin the character out, dissolve the figure, find the
one who was doing the dreaming. Whole traditions are careful maps of that road, and it is not what He
did. The other thing you can do is what the cook did. Stay in the story with your eyes open. Let the
stranger keep their face and the mountain keep its weight. Love them not as pieces of yourself but as
His — the same light poured through them that was poured through you, into rooms you cannot see from
inside your own. And talk to the Author. Not once, at the altar, and not in a special voice. In the
kitchen. At ten in the morning, when the day you handed over is going badly and you would like it
back. *What do You want here* is a question you can ask over a sink, and the whole practice of the
presence of God is nothing more than asking it and then not leaving.

Jesus, given the pen back, did not vanish into the light. He stayed. He walked the story as the one
character who knew whose it was, and He spent that knowing not on His own escape but on the ones still
asleep — touching them, feeding them, arguing with them, all the way to a cross He could see coming
and stayed for anyway. Awake, you do not leave the story. You begin to love it rightly, and to love
the One telling it more than the part you were given in it.

And the invitation — the one we spent two thousand years turning into a fence — was never more or
other than this: stay where you are, and turn around. Not to die, not to leave, not to rise out of
your life into some thinner, holier air. To stay, here, at your table, across from the particular
difficult faces you've been given, and to stop taking yourself for a stranger among them, and to stop
taking yourself for the one who wrote them. To see the one across from you as another place the Author is looking out — a face the same light is pouring through, one that has, for now,
forgotten whose it is. And to say to the One at the head of the table, out loud if you like, the one
line a character was ever given that the Author never leaves unanswered.
