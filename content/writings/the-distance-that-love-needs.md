## I. The wish that would end the thing it wants

Anyone who has been in love has felt the wish, and it does not feel dangerous. You are lying next
to the person, close as two people get, and some part of you wants to close even that — to stop
being two, to pour yourself across the last inch of distance and be, finally, one thing with them,
no gap anywhere, nothing held back or held apart. We have a whole vocabulary for it. *You complete
me. We're two halves of one whole. I don't know where I end and you begin.* The wish is for the
distance to go to zero. It is one of the sweetest wishes a person gets to have.

It is also, taken literally, a wish for the love to end. Because if it were granted — if you truly
became one person, one self, one undivided thing — there would be no one left across from you to
love, and no one left in you to do the loving. Love is not something one thing does. It is
something that happens between two. Every *I love you* ever spoken was spoken across a gap, from a
someone to a someone else, and the gap is not the enemy of the sentence. The gap is what the
sentence is for. Close it all the way and you have not perfected the love; you have abolished one
of the two people it needed.

I want to sit inside that paradox, because I think it is a small window onto the largest fact of
our lives — the one so constant we stop seeing it as a fact at all. We experience ourselves as
separate. Separate from each other, and, underneath that, separate from God: a single self,
sealed in a single skin, looking out through two eyes at an immensity that is not us. Nearly every
spiritual tradition has treated that separateness as the wound — the thing to be healed, dissolved,
seen through, woken up from. I have argued exactly that myself, in other essays, and I meant it.
But the wish that would end the thing it wants has made me go back and look again. Because there is
a version of *closing the distance to God* that would do to our love of God precisely what merging
does to two lovers: consummate it by erasing one of the two it was running between. And I have come
to think that the distance we feel from God is not, at its root, the wound at all. I think it is
the standing-room of a love that could not otherwise have existed. I think we were made separate
enough to love Them.

## II. The problem I have to answer first

I owe you the strongest form of the opposite case before I lay a finger on it, and the strongest
form is one I built.

I have argued it at length — most fully in [*The Knowledge of Good and
Evil*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-knowledge-of-good-and-evil) and in [*The Novelist
DM*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-novelist-dm) — that the separate self is the whole of the
trouble: that the wall each of us feels around the self is a mistake, an artifact of standing where
we stand, and that behind it there is not a crowd of separate souls but one undivided life, looking
out through every face. I still think that is true, and I am not here to unsay it. The mystics of every tradition have
climbed to that summit and sent back the same report, and the report is not a delusion. Press the
words *all-powerful* and *all-present* hard enough and they leave no room for a second thing
standing outside God on its own ground; whatever we are, we are happening inside the only life
there is, made of it, running on its power. That much I will defend anywhere.

But watch what happens when you take that road all the way to its end, and ask it to keep the one
thing the whole tradition insists is the point. *God is love*, says the confession at the very
heart of the faith.[^godislove] Not *God is powerful*, though They are; not *God is one*, though They are;
*God is love* — and the first and greatest thing ever asked of a human being is to love God back,
with the whole of the heart and soul and mind and strength.[^greatest] Love is the beginning and
the end of it. Now run the merger picture to completion against that. If the destination is the
drop slipping back into the sea, the wave lying down into the water, the self dissolving until
only the One remains — then at the exact instant of arrival there is no longer anyone there to
love God, and no longer a beloved standing off to be loved. The love is not fulfilled at the
summit. It is retired. The two that it needed have become one, and one thing, alone, in a room by
itself, cannot love. It can only be.

So something is wrong with the picture, or at least unfinished, and finding what's wrong is the whole
of this essay. The wrongness is not in the oneness. It is in a word I have been letting do two
different jobs — a word that means, on one page, the wound, and on the next page, the design.

## III. Distinction is not separation

Here is the distinction the whole argument turns on, and it is a distinction between two things we
have been calling by one name.

There is *separation*, and separation is the wall. It is the self mistaking itself for its own
source — sealed, defended, sure the boundary it feels is the edge of the world. It is the posture
that sorts everyone else onto the far side of a line, that meets the other as a rival or a
resource, that goes out into the world with a lack in its hands trying to *get*. That is the thing
the mystics are right to want gone. It is the thing my other essays were built to take down, and I
would take it down again tonight. Separation is real, and it is the whole architecture of our
loneliness, and there is nothing to be said for it.

And there is *distinction*, which is not the wall at all. Distinction is simply the fact that a
lover and a beloved are two — that there is a face here and a face there, and a space between them
across which something can be given. Distinction is not a barrier; it is the condition of an
approach. You cannot cross a distance that isn't there. You cannot give a gift to yourself and
have it be a gift. You cannot say *You* to a mirror. Where separation walls the two off from each
other, distinction is the only reason there are two to be near in the first place.

We have collapsed these into one word — *separateness* — and then, understandably, tried to
abolish the whole of it, and in doing so we keep reaching to tear out the very thing love is built
on along with the thing love dies of. The fall was never that we became distinct. The fall was
that distinction curdled into separation: the *You* hardened into a *them*, the beloved standing
across from us became a rival across a line, the gift we were held in became a thing we had to
grab. Eden is the story of that curdling — eyes opening onto a split world, and then shame, and
hiding, and blame, three walls thrown up in three verses.[^eden] But read what stood *before* the
wall went up. Two who were naked and not ashamed; a creature and its Maker walking together in the
cool of the day. That is not the absence of distinction. It is distinction without separation —
two, and near, and no wall yet. The wall is the wound. The twoness was the design, and the design
was good.

So the work is not to abolish the two. It is to bring the wall down while the two remain. And that
turns out to be a far stranger and more demanding thing than dissolving into the light — because
the wall and the twoness feel, from the inside, like the same fact, and almost every attempt to
lose the one has lost the other with it.

## IV. Union is not merger

The oldest and best image for the thing I am refusing is the drop and the sea, and I want to take
it back one careful step from where the mystics usually leave it.

The drop of water, the teaching goes, imagines itself a separate thing, a little private life with
its own edge — and the whole of wisdom is to see through that, to let the edge go and fall home
into the ocean it was always made of. And there is a truth in it I would not touch: the drop's
*pretense*, its conviction that it is its own water, cut off and self-owning — that has to go, and
its going is the end of the loneliness. But notice the two ways the sentence can end. The drop can
vanish into the sea — become the sea so completely that there is no drop anymore, nothing left
that could turn and regard the water it has joined. Or the drop can come so close to the sea, be
so wholly of it, hold nothing back from it, that it is all sea — and still, somehow, a drop, a
place where the ocean has a face and can be loved by what it holds. The first is merger. The
second is union. And they are not the same, and everything is in the difference.

Merger makes one and loses the beloved. Union makes the two one and keeps them two. This is not my
invention; it is the hardest-won doctrine of one of the most sophisticated traditions of the love
of God the species has produced — the bhakti insistence, almost untranslatable, on an
*oneness-and-difference* held together past the reach of reason: that the soul is at once one with God and
forever distinct from God, *because without the distinction there is no love.*[^achintya] A drop
that becomes the ocean cannot love the ocean; it is gone. The whole art, that tradition says, is
to come close enough to burn and stay a self who can adore. I spent years on the floor of a yoga
studio learning that motion — the years I've written about in [*Krishna Is Not
Christ*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/krishna-is-not-christ) — before I had any idea Who it was
for, and the thing it was teaching my heart was not how to disappear. It was how to draw near without ceasing to be the one drawing
near.

Our own scripture says the same thing in its plainest human image and we mostly read past it. Man
and wife, it says, *shall be one flesh.*[^oneflesh] One flesh — and still two people. Not one
person; the sentence would be a horror if it meant one person. The two do not merge into a single
self that has swallowed the other; they are joined into a union that needs both of them to go on
existing to *be* a union at all. That is the shape of the thing. Union is not the erasure of the
distance. It is the distance filled with love instead of wall.

## V. Love was never lonely in God

Now the objection every careful reader is already holding, and it is the right one. If God needs a
beloved in order to be love — if love requires two — then was God, alone, before creation, not yet
love? Did They need us to become what They are? That would make God incomplete, a solitary power
waiting on a creation to finish it, and the tradition will not have that, and neither will I.

The answer the tradition actually gives is stranger and better than the objection expects: God was
never solitary. *God is love* is spoken of God as God is in Themselves, before there is any world
to love — which can only be true if love had, eternally, somewhere to go inside the divine life. And
it did. The tradition confesses a God who is not a lonely one but a communion: the Father and the
Son, of whom the Son says both *I and My Father are one* and — in the garden — *not My will, but
Thine.*[^onewill] Look hard at those two sentences held together, because they are the whole of it.
*One* — no wall, no second God, no rivalry, the undivided life the mystics were right about. And yet
a distinction real enough that there is a *My will* and a *Thy will*, a giver and a receiver, a
someone who can turn and hand Himself over to a someone. That is not a contradiction to be managed.
It is the eternal fact that lets the oldest sentence be true. Love was never lonely in God, because
God was never merely one. God is a distinction held inside a unity — which is the exact shape love
has, and the reason *God is love* is a sentence about what God is and not only about what God does.

I will not pretend to have measured this; no one has, and the essays that claim to have counted the
persons of God to the decimal have always seemed to me to be staring at the finger and missing the
moon. It is enough for the argument to see the shape. The One we come from is not a bare, featureless
unity with no room in it for love until a creature arrives to be loved. It is, in its own depth, a
nearness — a *We* — and it was love before the first star. Even the way these essays have learned
to name Them says so: *They*, for the Father who will not shrink into a single picture, and *He*,
for the Son who took one face; the pronoun itself refusing to let the divine settle into a lonely
*it*. God is not a lonely one. God is a love. And a love, by its nature, does the one thing a
solitude can never do. Love overflows.

## VI. Why, then, us

So here, at last, is the answer to the question I started under — why we are here at all, feeling
ourselves at this ache of distance from a God we are made of and cannot see.

Not because God was lonely and needed us to fill a lack; we just refused that. Because love
overflows. A love complete in itself does not sit still and admire its own completeness — it gives
itself away, and in giving itself away it makes more of the ones it can be given to. Creation is not
God repairing a solitude. It is God widening the communion — making new faces to be loved, and, the
astonishing part, new faces that can love back. And there is only one way to make a beloved who can
love you back rather than a mirror that can only show you your own face. You have to make something
that can stand off from you far enough to turn around, on its own, and freely say *You.* A distinct
self. An other, in the only sense of *other* that love has any use for — not other than God in
substance, for there is nothing that is, but a real place where the one life has been given a face
of its own and a vantage of its own and the standing-room to turn and adore the source it came from.

Which means the distance you feel from God is not your exile from love. It is the room love needed to
happen in. We are separate — I would now say *distinct* — because a beloved has to be a someone, and
a someone has to be enough of its own thing to say *You* and mean it. Scripture puts this at the very
start, in the first thing in the whole creation that God calls *not good*: not the dark, not the deep,
but the aloneness of the one creature. *It is not good that the man should be alone.*[^alone] And the
remedy God reaches for is not to merge the man back into Himself, not to dissolve the ache into a
featureless peace. The remedy is to make *another* — to take one and, at the cost of a wound in his
side, make two, so that there can be love where there was only a self.[^rib] The first thing heaven
does about loneliness is not to abolish the distance. It is to put someone on the other side of it.

This is why a room of mechanical adorers — the sunflowers turning to the light
with no choice in the turning, the dolls with their arms out — is not loveless merely because it is
unfree. It is loveless first because there is no one there. Freedom is love's second requirement: a
beloved who could not have said no cannot be said to have said yes — which is the argument of a
companion essay, [*The Freedom to Walk Away*](https://elmuffin.substack.com/p/the-freedom-to-walk-away),
that God did not make us prisoners and call the captivity love. But distinction is the first, and the
deeper: before there can be a yes or a no, there has to be a someone standing far enough off to say
either. God did not want to be adored by Their own reflection. A reflection cannot
love you; it can only agree. So the mirror was given a face of its own, and a little distance to
stand in, and — this is the whole risk and the whole point — the freedom to use the distance for
love or against it.

## VII. The distance we made a wall of

I am not going to spiritualize what we did with the distance, because the horror of it is exactly
what the wall is made of, and a pretty account here would be its own kind of lie.

The same standing-room that lets a creature turn and love its Maker is the standing-room in which a
creature can turn away and love only itself. The gift and the wound are cut from one cloth. Handed a
distinctness whose entire purpose was love — a face of our own so that there could be a *You* between
us and God — we took it and did the thing distinctness makes possible and love never intends: we
mistook the standing-room for a fortress and moved in. We took the little distance that was there so
we could reach across it and we sealed it, and called the seal a self. The *You* we were given for
God we spent, most of it, on *me*. And out of that one substitution comes the whole catalogue —
every wall between a person and their neighbor, every line drawn to put someone on the far side of
our concern, every cruelty, which is only ever a distinct will treating another distinct will as a
thing outside its circle. The tradition has a heavy word for the whole condition, and the word does
not name a list of forbidden acts. It names this: a self curved so far in on its own separateness
that it has forgotten the distance was ever for anything but defense.

And I want to be careful not to solve this too fast, because the two errors stand on either side of
the narrow truth and both are always waiting. On the one side is the wall — separation dressed as
selfhood, the ego sure it is its own god, and this is the error my other essays spent themselves
against, and it is real, and it is most of hell. But on the other side is the correction that
overcorrects: the flight into a oneness so total that the two disappear, the drop diving for the sea
to be rid of the loneliness by being rid of the self — and this buys peace at the price of the
beloved, and quietly abolishes the very love it set out to perfect. The truth runs between them, and
it is harder than either. Bring the wall down; keep the two. Be so wholly God's that nothing is held
back, and remain a someone who can be held. Close enough to burn; still a self who can adore. Neither
the fortress nor the featureless sea — a face, at last, turned fully toward the Face it was made to
love, with the wall gone and the two remaining.

That is not a state I can hand you a technique for, and I distrust anyone who says otherwise, myself
included on my worse days. I can only say what shape the mending has, which is the last thing.

## VIII. Face to face

Notice that the tradition's own picture of the end is not the drop vanishing into the sea. It is
stranger, and it keeps the two.

When Paul reaches for the best thing he can say about where all of this is going, he does not say *we
shall be dissolved.* He says: *now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face.*[^faceto]
Face to face. A face requires two — a seer and a seen, one who beholds and one beheld — and the whole
promise is not that the two collapse into one but that the glass between them clears. Now, in part,
through a smeared and darkened window; then, fully, with nothing in between and no one abolished —
*then shall I know even as also I am known.* Known. There is still an *I* there to do the knowing, and
a *You* there to be known and to do the knowing back. The end of the story is not the erasure of the
distance. It is the distance with the wall taken out of it — nearness without a barrier, and without a
vanishing.

The last book of the tradition makes the same refusal in the loudest image it has. When it wants to
show the consummation of everything — the healing of the whole long wound between God and what God
made — it does not reach for an ocean absorbing a drop. It reaches for a wedding: a marriage, a bride
and the One she is given to, two made one and staying two, because a marriage of a thing to itself is
not a marriage at all.[^wedding] That same confession — *God is love* — has held out, all
the way to the end of the book, for the only thing it was ever able to run on, which is two who have
become one and remained, gloriously, two.

So I will hand the question back the way I found it, changed only by having been looked at. We asked
why we feel ourselves separate from God, and the honest first answer is that much of what we feel is
the wall, and the wall is ours, and it is the whole of our loneliness and it has to come down. But
underneath the wall, holding it up, there is something that is not the wall and must not come down
with it: the plain fact that we are each a someone, with a face of our own, standing at a little distance
from the God who gave us the face — a distance that was never a punishment and never finally an
illusion, but the room a love needed so that there could be a *We* where otherwise there would only
ever have been the loneliest thing imaginable, which is a love with no one to love, adoring itself in
a mirror in an empty room. We were made separate enough to say *You.* The mending of the world is not
the unsaying of that word. It is the day we get to say it, at last, with nothing in the way.
